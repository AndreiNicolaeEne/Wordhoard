Mukoma, let me tell you how that water came in, hour by hour, because you won't believe it.

Lekker rain two days before, everyone happy for it. Early morning, maybe five, I stepped out and the water was already at the door, only ankle deep, and I was thinking ah it's nothing, it will just go down now-now. I even went back inside and lay down small small. But by six I checked again and eish, it was higher already, past the ankle, coming in under the door itself. That's when I started moving things up off the floor while there was still time, the radio, the sacks of mealie-meal, whatever I could lift, busy carrying things the whole hour, no rest.

The mother: We move now, not just now, this water is not playing. Are we together?

By midmorning it was at the knee, and we were carrying everything onto the bed, onto the wardrobe, onto whatever stood highest in that house. The magetsi had gone off already when the water reached the meter box, so we were working in grey light only, no robot glowing at the corner, nothing moving on that road at all.

My friend: Howzit, shamwari, you also caught?

Me: Aikona, it's bad here, come and help, we make a plan together.

He came with his bakkie thinking he'd ferry us out, but even the bakkie sat stuck to its wheels before it reached the gate. My takkies were finished by then, soaked through and useless, no kombi running either, the hwindi just standing by the road watching one mshika-shika try and fail to cross.

It kept climbing, kept climbing, until early afternoon it peaked, past the highest thing we had raised, past the wardrobe even, and that's when we just sat and waited, listening to it knocking against the walls, hour after hour, nobody talking much.

The eldest: This one is not stopping, hey.

Me: Is it? Then we just wait.

Several hours after the peak it finally started pulling back, slowly slowly, no braai either, everything drenched, cold sadza the only supper that night, whatever was left in the pot. Shame, the chickens were soaked clean through, look how small they looked huddled there.

Went back the next day and there was the line on the wall, right up past where the wardrobe used to stand. Sharp-sharp we are now, but shame, that wall will remember it long after we've forgotten.
