So this whole year I was busy with my garden plot down by the stream, the one the council gave out after everyone was fighting for space, and let me tell you it was a full year, hey. From September I was already thinking what to put in, going round asking the other growers what did well for them last time, because you don't just plant what you like, you plant what the soil there is agreeing to. In the end I settled for rape and covo on one side, tomatoes and onions in the middle, and a small stretch of maize at the back because my mother said a garden without mealies is not even a garden, it's just a flower bed pretending.

I didn't rush it though. The old man two plots down, he's been there since before I was even born it feels like, he told me, don't put in before the last cold has properly gone, you'll lose everything to one frosty morning and start again for nothing. So I waited those few weeks, and only after that did I dig in the seedlings, one by one, my back finished by lunchtime every day that week.

And hey, we got lucky with the weather after that. Sun was out nice and steady, not too hot, small rains coming through when you needed them, and everything just took off. The rape was thick green in no time, the tomatoes were flowering before I even expected, I was there every afternoon after work just standing looking at my plot like a proud parent, feeling lekker about the whole thing. I remember thinking, if it carries on like this the whole year I'll be selling extra to the neighbours.

Of course it doesn't carry on like that, does it. First thing that came was some small green worms on the rape, eating the leaves into lace, holes everywhere, and I was like eish, not already. I asked around and someone said mix chilli and soap in water and spray it in the evening, so I did that, every two days, standing there with my little bottle pumping away like I'm some scientist. It helped, I won't lie, it slowed them down, the leaves stopped looking so chewed, but it never fully finished the problem, some mornings I'd still find two or three fat ones sitting there like they own the place. So I was just managing it the whole season, never really beating it.

Then came the dry spell, and that one was no joke. Weeks and weeks with nothing from the sky, the ground going hard and cracking, and I was there morning and evening with buckets, walking back and forth from the tap to the plot, arms finished, shoulders finished. Some days I was busy carrying water before work even started, six in the morning already there with my buckets, because if you leave it till after work the sun has already taken everything out of the soil. My takkies were permanently dusty that whole stretch, I don't think they were ever clean again after that month.

Now with everybody hauling water like that, the tap situation got tense, hey. There's a rota for whose turn it is at the tap because there's only the one line feeding everyone's plots, and the fellow next to me, we started having words about it. He said I was taking more than my time, filling extra containers to carry home besides watering my own beds, and I said no man, I'm only taking what's mine, you're the one who was there an hour past your slot yesterday.

The neighbour: You think I don't see you, filling five buckets when the others are only taking two. That water is for everybody, not for you alone.

Me: Ah, is it? Because when it was your turn last week you were there long after the next person's time started, I said nothing.

The neighbour: That's different, my tomatoes were dying.

Me: So are mine! We're all in the same dry spell here, my friend.

It went on like that for a few days, small grumbling, him not greeting me properly, me also not bothering to greet him first. In the end the older growers called us together one evening and we sat down properly and drew up a proper written rota, times and everything, pinned it up near the tap so nobody could argue is it my turn or not, it's just there in black and white. After that it was fine between us, we even started helping each other carry when one of us was running late from work. Shame, looking back, we were both just tired and scared for our plants, that's all it was.

Now the tomatoes, hey, those ones did me proud. By the time we got past the dry spell they just kept giving and giving, I was picking basins full every few days, red ones, some still turning, giving away to relatives, selling small small at the gate to people passing, even the murungu family I do garden work for on weekends were asking where I'm getting such lekker tomatoes from. That was my best crop by far this year, no argument there.

But while the tomatoes were sorting me out, the maize was having its own troubles. Just when the cobs were filling out nicely, the birds found them. Every morning I'd go and find another few cobs pecked half open, husks pulled back, kernels missing in rows like someone took a spoon to it. I tried tying old plastic bags on sticks to flap around, tried hanging some shiny CDs my young sister gave me, it kept them off for maybe a week before they got used to it and came back bolder than before. Lost a good portion of that stand to them in the end, there was nothing more I could do short of sitting there all day with a stick, and who has time for that.

Then, right in the middle of all this, we had that storm come through one Sunday night, wind and rain like the sky was angry about something, and Monday morning I get there and half my rows are flat, just lying down in the mud, stems bent over, the maize especially, some of it snapped clean. I stood there for a minute just looking, thinking ag, what now. But you can't cry over it, so I got my shamwari from the next plot to help me, and we spent that whole week in the evenings after work staking up what could still be saved, tying stems to sticks with strips of old cloth, packing soil back around the roots that had come loose. Some of it came right again, some of it we just had to accept was finished and pull out.

So when it came to the final tally at the end of the season, it wasn't the bumper year I was dreaming of back in September, but it was a fair one. Good basins of tomatoes, decent onions, the rape and covo that never really stopped in spite of those worms, and enough mealies to see us through even after what the birds and the storm took. I traded a good sack of onions with another grower two rows over for some of her sweet potatoes, because she had plenty and I had none, and that felt like a proper garden arrangement, nobody paying cash, just neighbours sorting each other out.

By the time the cold was properly coming back I cleared the beds, turned the soil, threw in what compost I had, and covered it over to rest till spring. Standing there at the end looking at that quiet plot, worms and birds and storm and all, I have to say it was a good year, hey. Not an easy one, but a good one. You learn more from the trouble than from the seasons that just go smooth, and next year I already know where I'm putting the maize, far from those birds' favourite tree, and that's that.
