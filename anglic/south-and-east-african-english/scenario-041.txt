Si, that job took two years exactly of my life before I finally called it. Two years, and for maybe the last one of them I was already busy telling myself, next quarter, next quarter, I'll talk to them next quarter. You know how it is. You keep pushing the thing because the salary lands on the twenty-fifth without fail and that alone can carry a person a long way, even when the person is somehow tired inside.

It wasn't one big thing that finished me, that's the truth of it, even though everyone wants a big thing when you tell the story. It was small. Imagine, it was just that the office WiFi voucher I'd been sharing with three other juniors got cut off without anyone telling us, on the same week the aircon in our side of the floor broke down again, the third time that year, and the facilities guy just shrugged, is it, like that was an answer. And then on the Thursday the supervisor forwarded an email at 9 p.m. marked URGENT that wasn't urgent at all, it could have waited till Monday, and something in me just closed. Small small things, but they were the last of a very long line, and that line had been building slowly slowly since maybe month eight.

So over that weekend I was busy rehearsing what I would say. Not shouting, not accusing anybody, just clean and diplomatic, the kind of thing you can say and still greet the person in the corridor after. I practised it in the matatu, I practised it in the shower, I practised it standing at the stove while the ugali was cooking. By Monday I had it smooth like a stone.

I went to reception and asked if I could see the manager, and the one at the desk just looked at me, is it, like she already knew, though she couldn't have known.

Me: Good morning, is she available for a few minutes, when she's free.

The one at reception: She's in a call, but let me check for you. Sasa, how are you?

Me: Mambo, I'm fine, just need a small word with her.

I was called in maybe twenty minutes later. I sat down, and I said the opening line exactly how I had prepared it, word for word, because I'd rehearsed it so many times it came out almost like reading.

Me: Thank you for the time. I wanted to talk about my role here, and I want to say upfront it's not really about any one thing in particular, it's more that I've been thinking about the next stage for a while now.

That was the polished line, and it was partly true, si, because by then it really wasn't about the WiFi voucher or the email at 9 p.m., those were just the last drops. But the real reason I held it back, the reason I didn't say the actual thing sitting under it, which was that I had felt small in that office for a long time, talked over in meetings, my ideas taken and repeated back by someone senior two minutes later and praised, the real reason I didn't say that plainly is that I still needed a reference from her one day, and also, if I'm honest with just myself and you now, I didn't want to see her face change. I wanted to leave with everybody still thinking well of me. That's the whole of it.

The manager wasn't surprised, not really, though she did that thing where she leaned back slowly and folded her hands like she was giving herself a second to choose her words.

The manager: Is it? I had a feeling something was coming, actually. Can I ask what would change your mind?

Me: I don't think it's that kind of thing, but thank you for asking.

The manager: We could look at adjusting your grade, and there's a title change coming in the new structure, you'd be senior by January. Would that help?

Me: It's tempting, and I appreciate it, truly. But I think it's just time for me to grow somewhere else, hey. It's not against the company at all.

That was the polite answer. The true one, the one I kept in my chest, was that no title was going to undo two years of being made to feel like the small one in the room, and no small raise was going to buy back the version of me that used to actually enjoy Monday mornings there. But you don't say that to someone who still signs your last payslip.

We agreed on two weeks' notice, standard, nothing dramatic, and she even said well done for handling it so maturely, which nearly finished me off right there because I wanted to laugh and cry at the same time.

That evening, walking to catch my matatu, a small doubt landed on me out of nowhere. What if I'd just done something foolish. What if the next place is somehow worse, what if I'd let pride push me out of a stable thing for a feeling that would pass in a month. I didn't sleep well that night, not even a little, turning it over and over, now thinking I was right, now-now thinking I was mad, and just now, near morning, finally deciding, no, I'll carry it through, I've said it now.

The two weeks went by fast, the way they do when everyone around you already knows you're leaving and starts being extra polite, extra careful, like you're something breakable. On the last day there was a small cake in the boardroom, somebody had written a card that everyone signed, and people were saying the usual things.

A workmate: We'll miss you around here, eh, it won't be the same.

The manager: The door's always open, you know that, if things don't work out there.

Most of it was hollow, si, the kind of thing people say because the moment demands it, not because it's true, and I said hollow things back too, we must keep in touch, let's do lunch sometime, knowing full well how rarely that lunch actually happens.

But there was one moment, right at the end, packing my few things into a carrier bag, when my closest workmate there, the one who'd sat next to me for most of those two years, came over and just stood a bit, not saying the usual line.

The one workmate: You're not really leaving because of the WiFi and that email, are you.

Me: No. Not really.

The one workmate: I thought so. Shame, boet, this place chews people slowly and calls it experience.

That was the one true thing said in that whole day, and neither of us pushed it further, we just looked at each other, is it, and left it there, and I think we both understood exactly what had been said without saying more.

It's been eight months now since that last day, and mostly what I feel is relief, a big clean relief that sits lower in the chest than the doubt ever did. There's regret too, small pockets of it, missing certain people, missing the certainty of that twenty-fifth of the month, but weighed against the relief it's nothing, it's just a shadow passing.

If I had that whole conversation to have again, knowing what I know now, I don't think I'd change the words much, the polite version still did its job, it let me leave clean, references intact, door still open behind me even if I never plan to walk back through it. But maybe, just maybe, I'd let a little more of the true reason slip out, not all of it, just enough that she'd know it wasn't really about a WiFi voucher at all. Some things, though, you only ever get to say properly to yourself, and to the one workmate who already knew.
