Before the cocks had even cleared their throats properly the mother was already up, banging on the door of the room where I was sleeping with my cousins, telling us the garden does not wait for anybody's dreams. It was still fully dark, that kind of dark where you can't even tell if your eyes are open or shut, and the paraffin lamp in the kitchen was the only proof a day had started at all.

The father called us out to the veranda while the mother was inside boiling the porridge, and he stood there dividing the work the way he always does, counting on his fingers, not wasting words this early.

Father: You, go and check the maize garden first, see if the wind touched anything in the night. You two, start on the beans near the fence. The rest, wait for me by the matooke.

The last-born, who is small but very willing, was given the easy work, the one nobody argues over, carrying the empty sacks to where they'd be needed, and chasing the birds off the drying floor with a stick. Shame, he takes that job like it's the most serious business in the country, running up and down, is it, like a soldier on parade. Look how small he still is, and already refusing to be left behind at the house with the women.

My uncle, who is the biggest of us and does not get tired the way ordinary people get tired, was given the matooke, because cutting a bunch down clean and carrying it on the shoulder without bruising it is not work for just anybody. He was busy swinging that panga since the sun was barely a rumour on the hill, one bunch after another coming down, and each time he'd call for someone to come and carry before the next one landed.

Uncle: You, come and carry this one, don't let it touch the ground, hey. A bruised bunch nobody will buy properly, and that buyer already wants to eat our money as it is.

That was the joke of the whole morning, the produce buyer who comes with his sack of coins and his long face, always finding some small small fault to knock the price down. Everybody has been cheated by him at some point and everybody still sells to him, because who else is coming all this way for bananas.

There was an argument too, between my uncle and the eldest, over how fast the maize should be pulled. The eldest wanted it done carefully, cob by cob, so nothing got left rotting in the husk; my uncle wanted it done quickly, saying the sun doesn't wait for anybody's carefulness.

Eldest: You're rushing and leaving half the cobs behind, is it? Slow down small.
Uncle: Slow down and see if this garden finishes by evening. Hurry, hurry, we'll sort the leftovers after.

Nobody really won that one, they just kept working at their own speeds side by side, grumbling.

Once the near rows of beans were cleared, the two who'd started there were shifted over to the maize, since a garden works like that, you don't stay stuck on one job the whole day, you move to wherever the work is heaviest at that hour. My aunt, though, was falling behind on her row, her fingers not as quick as they used to be, and nobody said a single word about it. The eldest just quietly came round from behind and started picking alongside her without any announcement, so that by the time anybody looked properly the row was level again with everybody else's.

Around midmorning the mother and my other aunt came out from the house carrying basins covered with banana leaves, posho still warm underneath, and beans cooked soft with a little groundnut sauce, and everybody sat wherever they were standing, on sacks, on stones, on the bare ground, eating with their hands right there in the garden. Nobody waited to be called twice.

Mother: Extend there a bit, let jjajja sit properly, don't crowd her like that.

We extended, and jjajja sat with her plate on her knees, telling the last-born off gently for eating too fast, telling him the maize is not running away, hey.

A neighbour passing on the path with his hoe over his shoulder stopped a moment to greet us properly.

Neighbour: Well done, well done. This garden is looking heavy this year.
Father: Thank you, thank you. Come and sit, eat something.
Neighbour: No no, I'm just passing, I'll come just now.

By early afternoon the clouds that had been sitting innocent on the hills since morning started gathering themselves seriously, and the wind changed the way it does before rain here, cool and sudden. The father looked up once and that was enough instruction for everybody.

Father: Leave the beans, cover the maize first, that one spoils quicker if it gets wet now. The beans we can still manage even if it rains a little.

So we all moved together to the drying floor, dragging the tarpaulins over the spread maize, weighing the corners down with stones, working fast, nobody minding whose job it had originally been. The rain held off though, just threatened and grumbled and drifted off toward the trading centre, and by the time we finished covering there was nothing left to cover, is it, so we uncovered it again, laughed at ourselves, and went back to the beans.

The sun came back stronger than before, that heavy afternoon heat, and we pushed through it, because once a garden is started it must be finished, that's just how it is. It was during that push that the handle of the second panga split clean off in my uncle's hand, and for a moment he just stood there looking at the two pieces like they had betrayed him personally.

Uncle: Eh, this thing has refused. Somebody go to the neighbour, tell him to lend me his, quickly.

The same neighbour, resting now under his own mango tree, borrowed me his panga without even standing up properly, just waved a hand toward where it leaned against his wall, calling well done again as we hurried off with it. That is how it is here, a tool failing is not a whole event, you just find another one and carry on.

As the afternoon wore on and the light began that slow orange slide toward evening, the father started calling for everybody to move quicker, no more standing to talk, no more resting on the hoe.

Father: We are finishing this section before dark, you hear me. The light is going now-now, we don't want to be cutting matooke by feeling alone.

So the last stretch became a group effort properly, everybody converging on the final rows together, the small ones carrying what they could manage, the strong ones finishing the cutting, jjajja even standing to help sort the last basin of beans by torchlight from someone's phone. There is something about a garden's last hour that pulls everybody toward the same corner, whatever job they started the day with.

When it was finally done, the father stood at the edge of the cleared garden and called out the count like he was announcing something at church: eleven bunches of matooke laid by the path, six sacks of maize tied and covered, four basins of beans, and a small heap of the odd vegetables that had come up free between the rows. Not a bad day, hey, considering the rain had wanted to spoil it.

Everybody just sat down where they were after that, properly collapsed, backs against sacks, legs stretched out in the dirt, nobody wanting to talk, just breathing. My cousin had ridden the bodaboda earlier to town for extra rope and had come back with rolexes wrapped in newspaper for everybody, and those disappeared in complete silence, nobody even joking anymore, just eating and watching the sky go purple over the hill.

But before anybody was let inside to wash properly and rest, the mother sent the last-born, who had barely sat down two minutes, to go and lock the chickens in before the dark finished swallowing the compound.

Mother: You reach there and back quickly, you hear, before that fox starts thinking he owns this place.

And off he went, running again like it was still morning, while the rest of us just sat there in the dark, too finished even to laugh at him properly.
