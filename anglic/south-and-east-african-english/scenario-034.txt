Most days I pass this stage about four times a week, and before I even reach the stall she's already cracking the eggs.

The vendor: Well done, well done. Our person is here, two eggs, tomato, no onion, wrapped tight, it's coming.

Me: Well done too. Eh but today make it one egg only, I'm busy trying to reduce, hey.

The vendor: Is it? You and this reducing, it will not stand, ah, but okay, one egg. Extend, you come and sit, let me finish now-now.

I lean on the wobbly stool she keeps for regulars and we start talking, slowly slowly, while the pan sizzles.

The vendor: So how's it there at work, is it moving?

Me: It's there. The boss is somehow these days, pushing us up and down, but we manage, hey.

The vendor: Shame, look how thin you've become with that stress. Me I was telling my sister the same, these bosses, they don't see.

Me: True. And business here, how is it? Just now I passed and the queue was long.

The vendor: Ah, it's up and down. Fuel went up, flour went up, even the onions themselves are proud now. And these council people, they came just now demanding their thing, as if they don't already eat enough money from us.

Me: Eh those ones. One day they'll tax even this smoke from your pan, I'm telling you.

The vendor: Don't joke, it will happen, this is Kampala.

We both laugh, and she rolls it fast, wraps the rolex, hands it over warm.

The vendor: There. Two thousand.

Me: Here. Borrow me a tissue quick, this one is dripping.

The vendor: Take, take. Call me when you reach.

Me: I will. See you Thursday, or is it Wednesday I'm passing.

The vendor: Whichever day, you'll find me here, hey.

I climb onto a bodaboda with the rolex warm in my hand, already thinking of the next time.
