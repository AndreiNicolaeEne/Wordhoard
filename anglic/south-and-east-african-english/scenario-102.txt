Karibu, come, mind that step, it drops more than you think and somebody is always catching their toe on it, even after all these years nobody has fixed it, si that step has outlived three different padlocks on this gate. Every single time I stood right here as a child my stomach would do that small thing, half excitement half fear, because you never knew who was inside, whether it was a good day in the house or whether some relative had come to collect an old debt. That gate has its own creak, a particular one, and even now if I heard it in a dream somewhere I think I would wake up already home.

This here, this is the main room. We called it the sitting room though there was more standing in it than sitting most evenings, aki, that room never rested. This is where anything that mattered enough for everyone to be looked at happened: the seven o'clock news on the radio that nobody was allowed to talk over, visitors who came unannounced and had to be given the good cups from the top shelf, homework spread flat on the floor because the table was for eating only and nothing else. Me I did most of my growing up right there on that floor, back against the sofa arm, listening to grown people arguing about matatu fares going up again, and about who exactly had eaten the money that was meant for the borehole. Imagine, three years of harambee contributions, everybody giving what little they had, and to this day there is no borehole, somebody somewhere is still eating that money slowly slowly and nobody has ever been made to answer for it.

Through here is the kitchen. Smell first, always smell before sight, smoke and paraffin and onions frying low, that smell followed you right out of the compound gate, you could tell whose house you were passing just by what was on the stove that evening. The one habit that always happened in there was the grandmother's tea. She boiled the leaves fresh every single time, never a reused pot, never that tea-bag business she called it with something close to disgust, and she'd stand right there stirring with her back to the door like the door itself didn't exist. If you walked in while she was doing that you waited, you didn't greet, you didn't ask for anything, you simply waited, sindiyo, that is just how it was done.

Sleeping was the parents in the one room and the rest of us packed into the other, three lengthwise on the one bed, two more on a mattress laid on the floor, the last born on a folded blanket that moved wherever there happened to be space that night. Nobody planned it out on paper, it simply arranged itself the way water finds the low ground on its own.

That corner there, by the window in the sitting room, that belonged to the grandfather and to nobody else, even when he wasn't sitting in it. His chair, his stack of old newspapers, his radio tuned to the one station and no other, and that whole corner kept his tobacco smell long after he had stopped being in the room at all. You would find yourself lowering your voice walking past it even now, aki, even now.

My own place was smaller and lower down, under the bed by the wall, behind a suitcase that nobody ever opened. My own small museum in there, bottle tops, torn pictures cut from old magazines, one broken watch with no strap. Somehow I was completely sure nobody else on this earth knew about that spot, imagine, I genuinely believed I had invented hiding.

Out the back door was the yard, cemented, a clothesline strung corner to corner, the sufuria washing area with its permanent grey ring on the concrete, and one lemon tree that never once produced a sweet lemon in its whole life, but we ate them anyway, sour face and everything, because a free lemon is a free lemon.

The neighbours were close enough that you heard their radio before your own woke up, close enough to hear their fights and their Sunday gospel music turned up full whether you yourself were saved or not, and there was a rooster on that side that nobody, not one soul, ever managed to catch off guard. If the sugar ran out at seven in the evening you sent a small one through the fence, "please borrow me a cup of sugar," and it always came back the following week doubled, because that is simply the law of it and everybody obeyed it without being told.

The street had its own clock, and the first hand of it was the makanga's voice calling the route before six every morning, before your eyes were properly open you already knew it was six by that voice alone, mbio mbio, and the diesel cough underneath it as the matatu pulled off. Same time, same sight too, an old man walking the same road at the same hour with his stick, rain or that baridi cold, nobody knew where exactly he was going and nobody ever asked, he was simply stitched into the morning the same way the sun was.

The tap outside was the thing that was always broken, or always about to be fixed, depending on who you asked that week. It ran fine now-now and then went completely dry for a week at a stretch, and the whole street had an opinion about whose job it was to sort it. It never got sorted, not properly, not once, in all the years I can account for.

The seasons changed the whole feel of the place. In the cold months the blankets doubled up and the hot water bottle went from person to person down the line, everybody complaining, nobody actually minding it that much. When the long rains came the street turned to red mud and the matatu would get stuck right at the corner and grown men would come out in flip-flops to push it free, laughing the whole time despite themselves. Come December half the street simply emptied out, everyone gone upcountry to shags, gates locked, the whole place gone quiet in a way that felt almost wrong, and then January brought it all back loud again, full of school-fees talk and new-uniform talk.

Out on the street itself was where the real gathering happened, evenings especially when the power cut and the stima went and there was nothing else to do but sit outside. Football with a ball made of tied plastic bags, a stone standing in for whatever else was missing, the bigger boys teaching the smaller ones how to fall without crying about it. The bodaboda rider parked at the corner most evenings, engine off, just talking, and somebody's radio carried the commentary from whatever match was on that Sunday to the whole street at once whether the street had asked for it or not.

Guest: Sasa!

The neighbour's child: Mambo.

Guest: Poa?

The neighbour's child: Poa poa.

That exchange happened maybe four times an hour on a good evening, nobody tired of it.

The mother, calling from the doorway when the light started going: You come, inside now, you reach the tap before you reach that ball, you hear me.

Me: I'm busy playing, just now.

The mother: Now-now, not just now. I said now.

The last clear memory I have of that place is an ordinary one, which is maybe why it stayed. Evening, power gone, everybody outside on the veranda because inside was too dark to bother with, the grandmother's radio going on its one station, somebody's phone flashing somebody else to say come out, and the smell of somebody's supper cooking somewhere close enough to make everyone hungry at once. Nothing happened that night. That's exactly why I remember it whole, start to end, when so many louder nights have gone somehow blurry in my head.

The place itself is not gone, not exactly, but it is not what it was either. New people live behind that same gate now, the lemon tree got cut down for reasons nobody explained to me, the tap I believe still runs the way it always did, badly. I passed it once from the matatu window and did not even ask the driver to slow down, I just looked, the way you look at somebody you used to know well who no longer knows you at all.

So that is the whole of it, my friend, room by room, the way I said I would give it to you. That is where I came from, more or less standing, more or less whole, sindiyo.
