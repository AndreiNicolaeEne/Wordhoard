My friend, that day did not go the way I had planned it, not even a small bit. Let me tell you the whole thing from the start, since you missed everything, hey.

We had agreed the truck comes at seven, seven sharp, so that by midday everything is already at the new place and we relax with a soda in the evening. That was the plan. I called the truck man the night before to confirm and he told me he's coming now. Now. Then by seven-thirty, no truck, I called again, and this time he told me he's coming now-now. Then by eight-thirty, still nothing, I called a third time, and this one told me the truck had just now left the stage, imagine. So already we are behind before the sun has even properly climbed.

First thing that went wrong, even before I had stepped out of the house, was the key. The key for the padlock on the store, the one with all the kitchen boxes, had gone missing. I was busy turning that whole bedroom upside down, under the mattress, in the handbag, in yesterday's trouser, when the mother came in and said,

Mother: Is it the key you are looking for? I put it on the nail behind the door, the way I always do.

Me: Eh, thank you, thank you. I was about to break that padlock with a hammer, I swear.

So that is how the morning started, already late before I had even locked the front gate properly.

Now the person who came without being called, that was the neighbour's boy, the one who is always around when there is work to be done. Nobody sent for him, nobody even told him we were moving that day. He just appeared at the gate before nine with his gumboots on, saying,

The neighbour's boy: I saw the truck outside, so I came to extend a hand.

Well done, well done, I told him, and that boy worked the whole day. He even borrows me his wheelbarrow without me asking twice, and carried more loads than some of the grown men there. Shame, that child, small as he is.

Now the thing that broke, it was the mother's big serving plate, the one from her own wedding, the one nobody is allowed to touch without washing their hands first. We had wrapped it well, or so we thought, but somewhere between the sitting room and the truck it slipped from between two cushions and hit the step, and the sound it made, eh! The whole compound heard the shout that went up. The mother came running from wherever she was, screaming,

Mother: Ah ah ah, my plate! My plate from that time!

She stood looking at the pieces for a long time, not talking. Nobody wanted to be the one to say anything. Then she just sighed and said ah shame, it had already served its years, hey, and that was that. But you could see it pained her the whole day.

Then there is the box we never found again. A small one, not even that big, but it had the extension cables, two of the good torches, and I think some of the old photographs. We loaded and loaded, boxes going up and down that truck the whole morning, and somewhere in all that moving that box just disappeared. We looked at the old house, we looked in the truck corners, we even went back in the evening to check under the veranda. Nothing. To now we don't know where that box went, my friend. Somebody's gain somewhere.

Now let me tell you about the fridge. That fridge nearly finished us. Me and the elder brother were meant to carry it, and immediately there was disagreement on how. He wanted it standing up the whole way, saying the gas inside will spoil if we lay it down. I was telling him no, standing up we cannot balance it on those stairs, we must tilt it small.

The elder brother: You lay that fridge down, you will kill it, I am telling you.

Me: And you carry it standing on these stairs alone then, let me watch you.

The boda rider who had come to collect something else just stood there laughing at us until we agreed, tilt it small small, not flat, not standing straight. You come first, the elder brother said finally, pointing me to the front, and slowly slowly we went with it.

But that doorway was another whole battle on its own. That fridge would not pass, no matter how we turned it. We turned it this side, the hinge caught. We turned it that side, the handle caught. We even lifted the door off, thinking that would help, still it was tight tight. In the end it is the neighbour's boy who said we remove the small shelf inside the door first, and just like that, small adjustment, and the thing slid through like it was greased. All that fighting for nothing.

By early afternoon the sky had started doing its own thing. It had been sunny sunny in the morning, hot even, and then those clouds came fast from the direction of the swamp, dark ones, and before we knew it the rain was pouring, proper pouring, the kind that does not ask permission first. We were caught with half the sitting room things still standing outside under the mango tree, mattresses getting wet, boxes going soft at the bottom. Everybody was running, covering things with polythene, with old bedsheets, with anything we could grab. We even lost time earlier at the roundabout, a traffic man wanted his something before he would let the truck pass, and if you don't give it he can delay you the whole day, these ones just eat money for nothing, that one ate our twenty minutes and some coins besides. Now with the rain the truck man was shouting for us to hurry, load, load, before the road becomes mud and he cannot drive out at all.

It is around that time, wet, tired, cold small small, that somebody's stomach started talking too loud to ignore. That is when the meal saved us. Somebody had sent the young one to the corner for rolexes, plain ones with extra onion, and a jerrican of tea, and we stood right there among the boxes and the wet mattresses, eating standing up, no plates, no table, just the rolex in its paper and the tea passed hand to hand in the one cup we could find. I am telling you, my friend, that was the best rolex I have eaten in my whole life, standing there in the rain-smell with everybody quiet and chewing.

After that meal is when the real tiredness came. Not small tiredness, the kind that sits in your legs and your shoulders and even in your eyes. The last few loads nearly finished us all. Every box felt heavier than the one before it, even the light ones. Nobody was talking much anymore, just lifting, walking, dropping, going back for the next one. The neighbour's boy was limping small by then but he did not stop.

When the first load reached the new place, I called the elder brother, who had stayed behind at the old house to finish with the last of it.

Me: You reach?

The elder brother: Not yet, still stuck at that jam near the roundabout.

Before we locked that old house for the last time, I walked through the rooms alone, just me. Empty rooms echo different, you know, your own footsteps sound like somebody else's. I stood in what used to be the bedroom, then the sitting room, then the kitchen where that plate had lived on its shelf for so many years, and it was somehow, standing there in all that emptiness after everything that had happened. I did not cry, but something sat heavy in my chest for a minute. Then I locked the door, tried it twice to be sure, and gave the key to the landlord's man who was waiting outside in the dark.

Me: Well done for waiting, sorry it has taken this long.

The landlord's man: No problem, no problem, these things reach where they reach.

By the time the last of it pulled up at the new place it was already dark, proper dark, streetlights dark, and we had less with us than we had started that morning expecting to still have, one plate broken, one box gone completely, a pair of gumboots left behind somewhere. We just carried the final things in by torchlight, the neighbour's boy still there helping even then, and somebody flashed the truck man to say we had arrived safe, and that was the day, my friend. That was the whole day.
