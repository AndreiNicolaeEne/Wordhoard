The gate was still opening when I heard footsteps running down the veranda, and by the time I had my bag off my shoulder the door was already thrown wide and the mother was standing there with her hand over her mouth, just staring, like she was trying to convince her own eyes.

The mother: Eh? Eh! Is it you? Is it really, really you?

She didn't even wait for an answer. She was across the yard before I could put the bag down properly, and she held me the way you hold something you thought was gone for good, long past where an ordinary hug ends, so long the father had to come and put a hand on both our shoulders before she let go even a little.

The mother: Come here, now now, let me hold you again. Let me look at you properly, ah. You have changed so much, so much, is it the food there, is it?

The father: (coming out slowly, wiping his hands on his trouser, busy fixing the gate hinge when the taxi came) I said to myself, who is this, and then I said, no, it can only be one person. Welcome, welcome, hey.

The eldest: (shouting from inside) Who has come, who has come, is it really... (appearing at the door, freezing) Wueh! It's true! Mother, it's really true, come and see!

The whole room, almost together: You've grown so lean! You've grown so tall! Look at the hair, when did you cut the hair! Are you eating there, are you eating properly! Why didn't you flash us from the airport, we were waiting and waiting!

The eldest: Si I told you the flight lands at ten, si, and you people were saying maybe he changed his mind again like last time.

The mother: Shame, look how small you were when you left, and now look at you, you have become a whole person, ah, shame.

I stood there in the middle of all of it, bags still by my feet where I had dropped them, everyone's hands on me at once, someone touching my face, someone touching my jacket like it was a strange thing, someone else already pulling my sleeve toward the sitting room.

Me: Me I don't even know who to answer first, you are all talking at once, let me breathe small.

The aunt: (standing a little back, blinking fast, her voice already going) I told myself I would not cry today, I told myself, I'll stop just now, just now... (she is not managing it at all, and nobody minds, and somebody hands her a corner of a kanga to wipe her face)

Somewhere behind the mother's skirt a small head was peeking out and pulling back again each time I looked over.

The little one: (whispered to the mother, not quite hiding) Who is that one?

The mother: That one is your own, don't you know your own, hey, come and greet, slowly slowly, nobody will eat you.

The little one did not move even one step closer, just watched from behind the skirt like the whole thing was a show being put on for other people, which, in a way, it was.

The eldest: Leave the little one, they don't even remember, it's fine, it's fine, come, come inside properly, why are we still standing at the door like visitors.

Me: (stepping in, and stopping) This house, it still smells exactly the same, you know. Dhania and the cooking fat and something else, I can't even say what, but it's the same, it hasn't moved an inch in eight years.

The mother: Is it? You still remember the smell of your own mother's kitchen, is it, hey, that's a good child.

Inside, the bags were forgotten completely by the door, nobody carried them in, nobody even looked at them again, because the news had already started coming, fast, one thing on top of another before I could even sit down properly.

The eldest: But wait, you don't even know, the one next door, the one you used to play with, she got married in December, big wedding, we even danced, and the other one, you remember the one who used to fight with you over the ball, he has moved to the coast now, working there, and this one here (pointing at the little one) has grown so much you won't even recognise them next year, and the grandmother next block, may she rest, she went two years back, we called you, remember.

The father: And the road, they've tarmacked the road now, all the way to the stage, no more mud in the rains, you'll see tomorrow.

Me: I saw, I saw from the taxi, and eh, the bodabodas, there are so many now, everywhere, they weren't even like this before. And that new building where the empty plot used to be, that place is somehow, I don't know, it doesn't sit right with the rest of the street.

The father: (laughing) You've been gone eight years and you're already complaining about our buildings, hey.

Me: But the jacaranda is still there, the one at the corner, I was looking for it the whole way, and when I saw it I nearly told the driver to stop the car.

The mother: That tree will bury all of us, it refuses even to fall in the storms.

The eldest: Imagine, we came to the airport at six in the morning for a flight that was landing at ten, imagine, sitting there the whole morning on those hard chairs, and when we asked them where the plane was they just kept saying now, now, it's coming now, and it wasn't coming at all.

The father: So. How long have they given you this time. Not one week again like the last time, is it? Because that one was not even a visit, that was just showing your face and running back.

Me: This time it's longer, I've arranged it properly.

The mother: Sindiyo? You're not lying to your own mother, sindiyo?

Me: Sindiyo, I promise, I'm not lying.

The aunt: (already halfway to the kitchen, calling back) Then we are not doing anything small for this one, we are calling everyone, the whole lot of them, we're doing a proper thing, this Sunday, I'm already thinking what to cook, we'll need to send someone for a chicken, maybe two.

The eldest: (grinning, the first joke of the day) Ah, look at this one, back only ten minutes and already the mzee of the house again, giving instructions with the eyes even before opening the mouth.

Nobody explained the name to anyone, and nobody needed to, it had been sitting there for years waiting for me to walk back through that door so it could be used again, and it landed exactly where it used to land, and the little one, watching all this from behind the skirt, looked confused at everyone laughing at a name that meant nothing to them yet, and that was fine too, that one would learn it soon enough.

A neighbour's child appeared at the open door, drawn by the noise, calling out before even stepping inside.

The neighbour's child: Mambo! Is it true he's come back?

Me: Poa, poa, I've come back, I've come back.

For a moment then, right in the middle of all that noise, everyone just stopped, all at once, like someone had switched something off, and they were only looking at me, all of them, the mother with her hand still resting on my arm as if she needed to keep checking I was solid, the aunt still holding the corner of the kanga to her eye, the father standing with his arms folded and his head tilted like he was still working out the sums of eight years on my face, the eldest just grinning without saying anything for once, and the little one, braver now, having come out from behind the skirt at last to stand at the very edge of the circle and just look, the way you look at something you were told about but never quite believed in.

Then somebody laughed, and somebody else said something about the tea going cold, and the bags finally got carried in, and slowly, slowly, the whole thing settled into something you could sit inside of, into ordinary talk, into a house with all its people back in it, at least for tonight.
