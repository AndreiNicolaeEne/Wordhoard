Let me tell you about the one year, my friend. I don't count it by any calendar, I count it by what it took from me, and even now if you ask me to put a date on it I will just look at you, because the body remembers a year like that by the weight of it, not by the number.

It started small, the way these things start. I was driving for a family in Kilimani, good people, steady pay, twenty-something thousand every month, school fees sorted, rent sorted, even a little left for myself. Then one day the man called me aside and said the car was being sold, thank you for your service, just like that, no warning, nothing. I was busy planning that whole month, busy thinking about the little one's uniform, and then in one conversation all of it was gone.

First to go were the small things, the comforts you don't even think of as comforts until they leave. I stopped taking sugar in my tea. Stopped buying meat on Sunday, we were doing sukuma and ugali most days and calling it enough. Stopped boarding a boda boda to town, I was walking, saving that fifty bob like it was gold. Small small things, but you feel each one leaving.

Then it went bigger, my friend. We pulled the firstborn out of the school she loved and put her in one that was, I won't lie to you, somehow, but it was a school, and a school was what we could afford. We moved out of the house we'd built a life in and into one room in a place I won't even describe to you. I sold my radio. I sold things I never in my life thought I would part with, and each time I told myself, it's just a thing, it's just a thing, but it doesn't feel like just a thing when you're the one carrying it out to sell.

The hardest part, harder even than the hunger, was going to ask. Me, I had never in my life borrowed from anybody, I was the one people came to. Now here I was standing at my workmate's gate at nine in the evening so the neighbours wouldn't see, asking him to borrow me something small, enough to carry us for two months. He gave it to me without even counting it in front of me, which I still remember to this day, but standing there asking, something in me bent, sindiyo, you know that feeling when your own pride goes quiet because it has to.

After that it was close to six months, my friend, six months of nothing steady. I was busy those months, don't ever let anyone tell you the unemployed man is lazy, I was busy every single day, carrying cement at a site for two days here, helping someone move furniture there, whatever came, and some weeks nothing came at all. I even went to the chief's office once for a letter to explain our situation for a fee waiver, and imagine, the man wanted his own something small first, eating from people who had nothing left to eat from, that's how those months were.

There is one day I still see clearly like it's in front of me. The lights were disconnected, and the little one asked me why it was dark, and I didn't have an answer for her, none, so I just went and sat outside on the step until it was properly dark outside too, so the dark inside the house and the dark outside were the same dark, and it didn't feel like it was only us drowning in it. That day was the very bottom for me. I sat there and I cried, a grown man, alone, on a step.

But eh, life has its moments, hey. Just when I had convinced myself there was nothing left to come, a woman I used to drive for years back, not even recently, years back, she called me out of nowhere and asked if I could drive for her sister for two weeks while their regular driver was away. Two weeks. That two weeks paid the light bill and bought us flour for a month. To this day I don't know how she even heard I was around, si Nairobi is a small place like that when it wants to be.

From there it was small small. A trip here, a delivery there, one week driving a matatu for a friend of a friend when their driver fell sick. Not steady, nothing like before, but it was something, and something is not nothing, my friend, remember that when you're in the middle of nothing.

It took close to eight months, slowly slowly, before I could say I had anything like steady again. Eight months of small jobs joining into bigger ones, of saying yes to work I used to think was beneath me and being grateful for it. Eight months before we moved back into a proper two rooms. Eight months before the firstborn went back into a school like the one she'd left, and when she did, ah, shame, she didn't even understand why I was the one who looked like he wanted to cry that time.

I learned something about people in those months. The ones I thought would show up, the ones I had helped before without counting it, some of them, when I called, they were somehow, somehow busy, somehow travelling, somehow broke themselves all of a sudden. And people I never expected, that workmate, that woman from years back, even a neighbour I used to just greet and pass, sasa, poa, nothing more, those were the ones who stood by me. You learn fast who is real and who was only ever around for the easy seasons.

And about myself, me I learned I could bend a long way without breaking all the way through. I learned pride is a small small thing when your child is looking at a dark house, it goes quiet fast, it doesn't even put up a fight. I learned I could carry more than I ever thought and still get up the next morning and go out and knock on another door.

It started to feel survivable around the sixth month of the climbing back, if I'm honest with you, the day I paid back a debt without anybody asking me for it, the day I walked to my workmate's gate again, not to borrow this time, just to sit with him and greet him properly. That was the day I knew we had turned the corner, sindiyo.

Now, my friend, I'm not telling you all this to talk about myself. I'm telling you because I know exactly where you are right now. This is your hard year, the one you'll count by what it cost you, not by any date on a calendar. I see it on you, I hear it when I ask how things are and you give me that "we are pushing" that isn't really an answer at all.

I won't tell you it's going to be easy, and I won't tell you exactly when it ends, because me I don't know that for anybody, not even for myself back then. But hear me on this one thing: you are not walking this one alone. My phone is on, day or night, flash me and I will call you back whatever the hour. When you reach, don't even knock, just come in, the door is not locked to you. If you need someone to sit with you on a step in the dark, I will come and sit. If you need someone to stand with you at a gate you're ashamed to be standing at, I will stand there with you so you're not doing it the way I did it, alone.

Whatever I have, small as it is these days, is there for you to borrow, no shame in asking, none, you hear me. We will get you through this one, slowly slowly, the same way I got through mine. You are not somehow, my friend. You are going to be alright. I am here.
