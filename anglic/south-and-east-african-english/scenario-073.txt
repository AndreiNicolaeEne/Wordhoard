Sit well, because I'm only going to tell this once, and after tonight you're supposed to know it like your own hand, is it.

It started almost ten years back now, when most of us were still in third year at Makerere, staying around Kikoni, broke the way students are broke, surviving on posho and beans and whatever kitty we could raise for a Friday. There used to be a rolex stand just past the gate, and after evening reading we'd all end up there at the same time, not because we planned it, just because that's where the queue was. It was the organizer who actually pulled us together properly, though, the one who used to insist a group discussion works better than reading alone, going round the hostel rooms telling everyone straight, "you come, we discuss together, then we go and eat." That's how it became a fixed thing, five of us at first, standing around that stand at night, and from there it just grew.

There's one among us who almost didn't stay, and I want you to hear that one properly because it explains a lot about how we are now. When she first started coming around she was quiet, hardly greeted anyone beyond the "well done" you give someone busy at their books, and some of us thought she was only there because her roommate dragged her. She nearly stopped coming altogether around second semester, felt she wasn't wanted, is it, the way new people sometimes convince themselves of a thing that isn't even true. Then her father passed, suddenly, back home, and we didn't even discuss it as a group, we just went, all of us, and stayed the whole week, cooked for the mourners, sat with her at night when the house had gone quiet. Shame, she was so small in that moment, but after that week nobody needed to ask if she belonged. She's the one who keeps peace among us to this day, actually, because she knows better than any of us what it costs to almost lose a place like this.

Early on, before even that, two of us fell out properly, over money, the way these things usually start. One had been holding the trip kitty and was slow refunding what was left over, and the other one, straight, accused him of eating it, the way you'd accuse an official who's eaten a road or a school. It got heated for about two weeks, nobody talking to who, everybody busy avoiding the other at gatherings. It got patched eventually, a proper apology was made, the money accounted for down to the last coin, but out of that whole thing came the joke that has followed us since: whenever anyone is slow to pay their share, somebody just says "ah, don't eat it o," and that's it, the whole table laughs, and the person pays up embarrassed. Ten years later and it still lands the same way, hey.

There's a friend who left the group properly, a few years in. The reason given at the time was that work had gotten demanding, odd hours, no time for weekend plans anymore. We didn't push it too hard, though we all somehow felt it was more that things had changed at home for him, a new person in his life who didn't sit well with how much time he spent with us. Either way, he drifted, the calls became fewer, and by the time anyone noticed properly, he was gone from the group in all but memory. We don't talk badly of it. That's just how that one ended, and we've left it there.

Later, someone joined who is now as core as anybody, and the way it happened was proper, not rushed. The peacemaker's cousin started coming around, vouched for from the very start, "he's serious, you can trust him with money," she told us, but we still tested him a good while before anything was official, small things, whether he'd show up when he said he would, whether he'd handle a contribution honestly and not be the type who borrows you a pen in first term and is still holding it at graduation. He passed all of it quietly, never made noise about being tested, and one day it was just understood, no ceremony required, that he was one of us properly.

The ordeal that bonded the rest of us harder than anything came a few years after that, when we all went upcountry together for what was meant to be a simple weekend. The vehicle we'd hired broke down halfway, and we ended up finishing the last stretch on bodabodas, in the dark, luggage balanced wherever it would sit, everyone somehow managing not to fall off. It rained the whole first night we camped, the fire wouldn't take, we cooked posho over a jiko that kept dying on us, and we were laughing the whole time because honestly what else was there to do. Nobody who was on that trip has ever fully explained it to anyone who wasn't there, and nobody needs to. We just know we came back different, closer, in a way that has held since.

One of the core five isn't around anymore, not because anything went wrong between us, just moved away, further studies abroad, and life caught up the way it does. We still feel that absence at every proper gathering, someone will say something funny and then look toward where that seat would be, is it, and there's a small quiet before somebody changes the subject. We call sometimes, when the time zones agree for once, and the first thing anyone asks is whether they've reached wherever they were going, but a call isn't the same as a seat filled at the table.

These days everyone has settled into their fixed part. The organizer still organizes, can't help himself, already telling us where and when for something the rest of us haven't even agreed to yet. There's one who is always late, has been late since the very beginning, we've learned to tell him a time a full hour before the real one and it still isn't enough, hey, we just greet him "well done" when he finally shows, since at least he tried. And the peacemaker, well, you've already heard what she does when things get tight between people.

We nearly broke apart for real once, a few years back, over a small savings scheme we'd started among ourselves. The one holding the books mismanaged it, whether on purpose or just badly we still don't fully agree, and it came out badly, accusations flying, people not speaking for close to a year, is it, real silence, not the joking kind. Imagine, some of us found out the box had been opened from a third person, not even from the one responsible. What brought it back together wasn't a meeting or an apology exactly, it was a funeral, another one of our own losing a parent, and everyone just showed up the way we always have, and somehow, standing there together at that one, none of the money mattered as much as it had felt like it did. What was left got sorted quietly after, paid back bit by bit, nobody made a thing of it. But that year taught us something about what actually holds a group like this together, and it isn't the money.

The one tradition that has never moved, not once in ten years, is the last night of the year. Wherever we all are, whatever else has happened that year, we find our way back to that same spot near the old rolex stand, midnight, and eat rolex together standing there the way we did as students, before any of us had proper jobs or proper problems weighing on us. It's a small thing, but it has outlasted every falling-out, every friend who left, every one of us moving house or job or country. It will still be there this year, now-now, in a few months, and you'll be standing there with the rest of us.

Which brings me to you. You've already done more than you think to belong here, you know. Nobody told you to, but you were the one who paid the kitty in full before anyone even asked, first month you were around, and you extended for the quiet one at that first proper dinner without anybody prompting you, moved your chair without a word so there was room for her to sit closer. That's not a small thing to us, even if it looked like nothing to you at the time. So that's the whole history, the way I'd tell it to nobody else but someone about to be part of it properly. Welcome, my friend. You're close as kin now, whether you like it or not.
