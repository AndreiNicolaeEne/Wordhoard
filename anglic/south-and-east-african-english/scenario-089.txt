Ah shamwari, let me tell you this whole story from the beginning because you were not there for any of it and it deserves to be told properly, not the short version.

So you know how it is, everyone around me learned this thing when they were small, five six years old, their parents just threw them in and that was that. Me I left it and left it until last year, until I was standing at the edge of the pool at the gym feeling like the only adult in the whole place who did not know what to do with their own arms. I had been saying for years I will learn one day, one day, and then one day just came and I was there with my costume on and my heart going somewhere it should not go for something so small.

First lesson, shamwari, I want to tell you it was hopeless. Completely hopeless. My body simply would not do what my mind was telling it. I would tell my legs kick and they would just sink, I would tell my head go under and breathe out slowly and instead I came up coughing like I was trying to bring up a lung. The instructor was patient, I will give her that, but she was not going to lie to me either. She looked at me straight and said,

The instructor: At your age the water does not forgive the same way it forgives a child. You will take long to get this. Longer than you think.

And is it, I said, because what else do you say to that. I did not want to hear it but hey, she was not wrong.

Within days, shamwari, within days, there were children in that same beginner class, small ones, six seven years, already doing what I could not do after a whole week. One little one was floating on her back like she was born knowing how, arms out, not a care. Shame, look how small she was, and already ahead of me by so much it was almost funny if it was not so painful to watch. That stung, I will not lie to you. I am a grown person, I have handled things in life, and here I was being shown up by someone who still needed help tying her own shoes.

That whole first week my body ached in places I did not know a body could ache. My shoulders, my neck, the backs of my legs, muscles that had been quiet for I do not know how many years suddenly making noise every time I sat down, stood up, turned in bed. I was busy telling myself it will pass, it will pass, but each morning it was a fresh surprise, ag man, this one too, and that one, yoh.

Then came the fall. I have to tell you about the fall properly because it is the part people always want to hear. I was getting into the shallow end from the side, not even trying anything clever, just getting in the way you get into a pool, and my foot went where it should not have gone on the wet tile and I went down. Flat. Hit the water on my front, the whole of me at once, and the breath was knocked clean out of me, gone, just like that. I lay there for a moment, in the water, not moving, and in that moment shamwari I want to tell you I was completely and totally finished with the whole idea. Swimming, learning, all of it. Finished.

Me: I am done. I am not doing this again.

My workmate, who used to come with me some evenings, she just looked at me and said,

Her workmate: Aikona. You are not stopping now, not after that. Come Thursday, one more time, then decide.

I did not want to. I said so plainly, I said I have had enough of being laughed at by people young enough to be my own child, enough of my whole body hurting for a thing that is supposed to be relaxing. But she talked me into Thursday, and Thursday turned into the week after, and somehow I just kept going.

After the fall, shamwari, progress was slow, and I mean slow, and often it was humiliating too. One small thing gotten right after weeks and weeks of getting it wrong, while a small crowd of parents sat pool-side waiting for their own children, some of them looking over kindly, some clearly enjoying the show. I would finally manage to float for three seconds and someone's father would look up from his phone just long enough to see it and go back to his phone, not impressed at all, and hey, fair enough, it was not much to see from where he was sitting.

The only thing that kept me sane was comparing myself only to myself. Not to the fast ones, never to them, that road leads nowhere good. Just, where was I a month ago, where am I now. A month ago I could not put my face in the water without panicking. Now I could, just that, and it was something.

Then, shamwari, around the fourth month, something happened that I still cannot explain properly in words. It was not one lesson where it clicked, it was somewhere in the middle of an ordinary Tuesday, kicking the same kick I had kicked a hundred times before, and suddenly my body just understood it. Not my head, my body. The water held me instead of fighting me. I do not know how else to say it. The instructor was standing at the edge and she just said,

The instructor: Sharp-sharp. Now you are swimming.

And is it, I said again, but this time it came out different, this time it came out like I finally believed her.

I never did catch the fast ones, shamwari, not the small ones and not the grown ones who picked it up in an afternoon like it was nothing. By the time the year was closing I had made my peace with that fully. I was not proud of catching up to anyone. I was proud of the things my own body could now do without even thinking, that same body that a year before could not keep its own head above water for longer than a breath.

Looking back on that whole season now, and yoh it really was a season, I do not look back on it sourly at all. The falls, the soreness every single morning, the small ones outswimming me, the fathers not even looking up, all of it sits fondly now, folded into something I am glad, properly glad, that I did. We even made a small braai of it after the last lesson, just the class and the instructor, nothing big, but it felt earned.

So that is the story, my friend. Late start, hard landing, slow climb, and somewhere around month four, the water finally let me in.
