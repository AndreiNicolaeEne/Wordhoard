Before this place there was the shamba up-country, and by the time my great-grandfather was a grown man that shamba could not hold one more mouth. It had been cut between so many brothers, generation after generation, that what came down to his portion was hardly two acres, and even that piece was tired, red soil that had already given its best long before he was born. Then the rains failed, two seasons running. Between the small ground and the dry sky there was nothing left to keep a young man there, is it. So he did what many were doing in those days, he decided to try the city.

He was the first of that whole family to go. Alone. No wife yet, no children yet, just himself and whatever he could carry, and generations later we are still telling it the way it was told to us.

The grandmother used to tell it sitting like this, hands busy shelling something, never stopping the work to talk, the two things going together:

The grandmother: Your great-grandfather left with one small bundle and a stick for the dogs on the road, that is all. He walked two full days before he even reached where the lorries pass. Imagine, two days, sleeping in whoever's cowshed would let him, eating what people gave out of pity, and even then the road itself was not simple, hey. At every stop there was some clerk wanting to see his kipande, and if the paper was not exactly right, the man would look at him long and slow until he understood he must find a coin from somewhere, because that is how those ones ate, off a poor man's paper.

He reached the city with close to nothing. No relative waiting with open arms, no room ready. What he had was a name of a family from back home who had gone before him, people his father used to greet at the market, and it was to their door he went first, and it was their floor he slept on those early months, borrowed, not his own, one corner of one room with three other men from the same area doing the same thing, all of them busy looking for work and finding none, day after day.

There was no steady thing at first. He would stand where the men stood, before the sun was even properly up, hoping someone building a wall or clearing a plot would point at him for the day. Some days yes, most days no. He carried loads at the market when there was nothing else, his back learning the city before his hands learned any trade. It was somehow, those months, truly somehow, but a man does not lie down and die because things are somehow.

Slowly slowly it changed. There was an old fundi near where he stayed, a carpenter, and this man needed someone to hold wood steady, to sweep the shavings, to carry the finished pieces to whoever had bought them. My great-grandfather took that, small pay, but steady, and he watched everything the old man's hands did. Within some time the old fundi was letting him cut the joints himself, and by the time that man was too old to hold a saw properly, my great-grandfather had the trade in his own hands, and he never put it down again. Carpentry became the family's thing from that day, right up to my own uncles.

Once there was work he began to save, coin by coin, and this is the part told with the most feeling, because it took him a little over two years before he had enough put aside to send for his wife and the children. Two years of a man alone in a city, and a woman alone up-country with small ones, each one telling the story from their own side now.

My mother: When my grandmother finally got the message that she should come, she did not travel light-light. She came with everything that could be carried, and with her, her own cousin, because a woman does not travel that road by herself with small children, sindiyo. That cousin never reached here. Somewhere on that same hard road, the one your great-grandfather himself had walked, the sickness took the cousin, and there was no proper burial that side of things could be done for, just what could be managed along the way.

That cousin is not spoken of by name in this family, not even now. Only my grandmother spoke of it at all, and even she only touched it lightly, the way you touch a burn to see if it still hurts.

The grandmother: Shame, that one did not even see this city she was travelling towards. Shame, all that way for nothing.

That is the whole of what is said. No name. Just "the cousin," said quiet, and the subject changed just after.

What did survive that journey, what is still in this house today, is a small wooden stool, low, three-legged, the wood gone dark and smooth with age. My great-grandfather's own father made it with his hands up-country long before any of this happened, and when my great-grandfather left the shamba the first time, alone, he carried that stool wrapped in cloth on top of his one bundle, and he brought it all the way to this city. People asked him why he was troubling himself with a stool when he had nothing else, and he would say only that a man should sit on something his own father made, wherever that man ends up. It sits in the good room to this day. Nobody sits on it carelessly. It comes out properly when there is an elder visiting, or when there is a death in the family and people are gathering, and everyone who has grown up in this house knows what that stool is, even the small ones who cannot yet say it properly.

Once the family was whole again, within some few years he had found a proper place to stay, a room and then two rooms in one of those estates built for African families in those days, and that became the family's place. It is where his own children were born, my grandmother among them, right there in the city his own father had never even seen. That is the part that finally made this place home, not the arriving, not even the reuniting, but the children coming and being born under that roof, belonging to no other ground but this one.

Me I was born generations after all that, in a different part of this same city, but the story never changed shape in the telling, only the teller. I heard it first from my grandmother, sitting exactly like her own mother used to sit, hands busy with some small work, never looking up while she talked, and me sitting on the floor because the stool was not for children to sit on.

Even now in this family there is a small joke about time that comes straight out of all that waiting those years ago. If someone says they are coming just now, we know to expect them properly later. If they say now-now, we start putting the food out. And if anyone ever says sasa hivi, we already know they are standing at the gate. Nobody explains this joke to a visitor, hey, you either grow up inside it or you do not.

So that is how I tell it, the way I was told it, generations passed hand to hand like that stool itself, and one day it will be told again by someone sitting where I am sitting now, hands busy with something, a small one on the floor listening, not yet understanding which parts of the story are the heavy parts and which are only the shape the telling has always taken.
