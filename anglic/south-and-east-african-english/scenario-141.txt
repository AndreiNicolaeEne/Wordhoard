I called them all together on purpose, on a Saturday when nobody had a wedding or a burial or a meeting to run to, and by the way they came in and sat themselves down without even arguing over who takes which chair, I could tell they already knew this was not going to be tea and small talk. The mother was busy folding and refolding a khanga in her lap the whole time we waited for the last ones to reach, not because it needed folding, just to have something for her hands to do. The eldest sat forward with the elbows on the knees, the way she sits when she is bracing for something. The auntie came in last, straight from the bodaboda, and I told her, you sit here, first, next to me, before anybody else could take that seat.

Me: Well done for coming, all of you, well done. I have called you here because there is something I have kept back for years now, and it is finally time I put it down.

Nobody answered me. Not even a hey. Not even an is it. The room went that kind of quiet where you can hear the fridge switching on in the kitchen and the posho water still boiling on the stove.

Me: You all remember the year we lost the land money. The money we had all put together, every single one of us, for grandmother's operation and for the plot out past Mukono that we were going to build the family house on. That money finished, just finished, and up to today nobody has ever properly explained to this family where it went.

The eldest shifted on the sofa. The mother stopped folding.

Me: You remember the anger that came after. How this whole family nearly scattered over that money. And you remember who took the blame for it. The auntie was the one holding the box that year, the one collecting from each of us on paydays, and when the money was found short, this family decided, just like that, that she had eaten it. Eaten it the way officials eat money that passes through their hands. Nobody asked her to explain herself twice. It was decided.

The auntie kept her eyes down. She has kept her eyes down about this for so long it has become how her face sits.

Me: For years now this family has looked at her somehow. You know the somehow I am talking of. The way people greet her a little too carefully at functions. The way her own children have had to hear it whispered at school, that their mother is the one who ate the family's money. She was never forgiven for it, not really, even the years everybody said it was forgotten, it was not forgotten, it was only quiet.

Me: I am telling you plainly today, in front of everyone, so there is no confusion after I have left this room: it was not her. She never touched that money.

Silence again, thicker than before.

Me: The one who took that money was the uncle.

The mother put the khanga down flat on her lap and did not touch it again.

Me: He took it that same year, to put into that bodaboda and matatu business he was starting with his friend from the garage, the one that finished within months and left him with nothing but a burnt engine and a debt he never told this family about. He borrowed from the box thinking he would return it before anybody counted, and then the business failed, and he was busy hiding from that failure instead of hiding the money, and by the time the counting happened it was already gone and it was too late to say anything without shaming himself in front of all of us. So he let it stand. He let this family decide it was the auntie, and he never once opened his mouth to correct it.

Me: I know this because I saw him carry that money out of the house myself, wrapped in a black polythene, that Thursday evening, when I was still young enough that nobody minded me sitting outside. I did not understand what I was looking at then. It was only later, when I was older, that he sat me down himself and told me the whole thing, in confidence, and asked me to carry it with him. And God forgive me, I carried it. All these years, I carried it.

The eldest: Is it? You are telling us this now, after all this time?

Me: I am certain. I am not confused about what I saw and what I was told. It was him, not her.

The mother's face had that look I knew was coming, the old anger from that year rising up fresh in her, the same anger she had folded away and never quite let go of.

Me: And before you say it, Mother, I know what you are thinking, that this family carried that anger once already and put it down at great cost, and now I am the one bringing it back up. I know. I am not doing it to open an old wound for the sake of opening it.

Me: I kept quiet because I was afraid of exactly this, this anger coming back to life in a room like it never left. I kept quiet because he begged me, and because for a long time I told myself I was protecting him, protecting all of us really, from the fighting starting again. And if I am honest, I also told myself it was not my place to be the one who caused more damage by speaking, when the damage was already done and could not be undone by my mouth. There was even a time, at the auntie's own daughter's introduction ceremony, years back now, when I nearly said it. I had the words in my mouth already. I looked at the auntie standing there being greeted stiffly by people who should have known better, and I nearly stood up and said the whole thing right there in front of the visitors. I pulled it back at the very last second. I have regretted pulling it back every day since.

Me: Carrying this has cost me more than I let any of you see. Watching her live under a name that was never hers to carry, watching her grow smaller and smaller at family functions, extending herself less and less room at the table because she had come to believe she deserved less of it, that has eaten at me. I am the one who ate something all these years, and it was not money. It was my own peace, hey.

The auntie lifted her head slowly. Her face was doing something complicated, something between relief and a fresh, sharper anger than the one she had lived with all this time.

The auntie: So all these years. All these years I have carried this. My own children have carried this. And you knew.

Me: I knew. I am sorry. There is no explaining that makes it smaller than it is. I should have told you long ago, and I did not, and that failure is mine alone to answer for now, not yours to carry one more day.

The uncle had gone very still where he was sitting, the kind of still that is its own confession. When he finally spoke his voice had none of its usual weight in it.

The uncle: I never meant for it to sit on her for this long. I meant to fix it. Then there was nothing to fix it with, and then there was no good time to say it, and then the years just kept passing and it became easier to let it stand than to bring it down.

The mother stood up then, and the anger that had gone quiet for years came back into that room exactly the way it had left it, only turned now, finally, in the right direction.

The mother: Easier for you. Easier. While your own sister was carrying your shame on her back like it was hers to carry.

Nobody stopped her from saying it. Nobody softened it for him either, and I did not try to.

Me: I am sorry, all of you, for how long this took. I am not saying it to be forgiven quickly. I only know that this family cannot keep standing around a story that was never true, and today at least we are standing on the true one, even if it is not a comfortable place to stand.

Nothing was fixed by the time everyone finally left that room. The auntie and the uncle did not embrace, did not even look at each other for long. The mother was still shaking her head at the door. But for the first time in all these years, the story sitting in the middle of this family was, at last, the real one, and whatever comes of it now, at least it will be built on that.
