Hello you,

I know, I know, it's been long, hey. You've been gone almost a year now and I keep saying to myself I'll sit and write a proper letter, not just those two-line messages, and then the year goes and goes and here I am only now, finally, busy sitting down with a pen to do this thing properly. Ag, forgive me. I owe you this one.

Let me start with the house because I know you'll want to hear it first. The gate motor, finally, finally, is it, after nearly two years of that thing groaning and sticking and the mother having to get out the car in the rain to push it by hand, the man came and fixed it properly this time. Not the cousin's friend who "fixed" it the last three times and left it worse. A proper electrician, took him one afternoon, and now it opens smooth, no noise, sharp-sharp. Of course, now-now, the very next day, the geyser started dripping in the roof space, just a slow drip drip into the bucket the mother put under it, so that's the next thing queued up. There's always something, hey, this house doesn't like to sit quiet for too long.

We put up the new fence along the side, the one the father was talking about since you were still at school here, if you can believe it finally happened. And I planted the lemon tree where the old broken swing used to stand, you remember that swing, the one that only the dog ever really used, God rest it. It's small still but it's got two lemons on it already, I check on it like it owes me money.

Now the big news, the one thing that changed everything this year. The mother left the shop. After all those years, can you believe it, she's not standing behind that counter anymore. The cousin, the one who works at the clinic, put in a good word and now the mother is doing the books there three days a week, sitting down, in the shade, with a proper chair, no more standing all day on that concrete floor that used to finish her legs by evening. It came about almost by accident, hey: the clinic needed someone urgent for two weeks to cover leave and the mother went in just to help, and by the end of it they asked her to stay on properly. She still can't quite believe her luck, she tells everyone who'll listen, at the braai, at church, at the shops, everyone. I'm happy for her, boet, really, she deserved that sitting-down job long ago.

You'll laugh at this one and I'm not even going to explain it, I'm just going to write it down and you'll know: the biscuit tin is still on top of the fridge, still empty, still exactly where you left it the last time you "borrowed" the last of the shortbread and swore blind it wasn't you. Some things don't change, hey.

The neighbors, let me tell you about the neighbors. The family two doors down, the ones with the loud gate, they've gone, moved out to the East Rand somewhere for the father's work, and it's actually quiet now which is strange after all these years of that gate banging at all hours. New people came in across the road instead, quiet so far, keep to themselves, we'll see, is it. And the one on the corner, her family's grown again, that's baby number three now, and honestly I don't know how she manages, shame, that woman doesn't sleep, you can see it on her.

Two people ask after you every single time I see them, without fail. The aunt, every time, first thing out of her mouth before even howzit properly:

The aunt: And that one of yours, still doing well over there, is it?

Me: Sharp, still doing well, still working hard, I tell her every time.

The aunt: Shame, all that way. Tell them we're all still here, hey.

And then the one at the corner shop, the older one who used to give you sweets when you were small and pretend the till was short so he could keep back a few for you, he still asks, every single time I go in for bread:

The one at the corner shop: Still there? Still working hard?

Me: Sharp, doing well, thanks for asking.

The one at the corner shop: Good, good. Tell them the shop still stands.

I'll confess something to you now that I could never say to your face, so I'm putting it here where it's safer, hey. Some evenings I still go and sit in your room for a bit, on the end of the bed, just to sit there, not really doing anything, and I don't tell anyone I do it because they'll think it's a strange thing to do. It's not sad exactly. I just miss having you down the passage. That's all. Don't make a big thing of it when you write back, I'll be embarrassed.

This past winter was a hard one on the place, I won't lie. Dry, dry, dry, the whole season, the lawn went completely brown and the water restrictions came in properly this time, not just talk. We were only allowed to water the garden certain days and even then only with a bucket, no hose. The lemon tree nearly didn't make it, I'll be honest, I was carrying dishwater out to it some evenings just to keep it going. But the rain's come back these last few weeks, proper rain, and already everything's greening up again, the grass, the neighbor's jacaranda, all of it, like the whole street breathed out at once.

Now you must tell me things too, because your letters, when they come, are too short, hey, always in a rush. How is the work treating you, is it what you thought it would be when you first went? And the food there, have you found anywhere that does something even close to a proper Sunday lunch, or are you still surviving on whatever you can throw together after work? And the cold, boet, how are you managing the cold, because I know you, you never dressed properly for winter even here, so I can't imagine how you're coping over there where it's supposed to be worse.

I'll close now because this letter is already longer than anything I've written in years and my hand is sore, hey, but I'm glad I finally sat down and did it. I'm going to send you that biltong I keep promising, the good stuff, not the garage-shop rubbish, I mean it properly this time, I'll post it this week before I lose the nerve. Write back soon, don't leave it a year like I did.
