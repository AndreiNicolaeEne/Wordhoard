Shamwari,

I said I would tell you properly once my head came right, so here it is, the whole thing, as far as I can even put it back together, because plenty of it I cannot.

You know how my cousin's birthday was supposed to be a small thing, just family, nothing big, that's what she was saying the whole week. Ah, it was not small at all, hey. By the time we got there the whole yard was full already, the braai going, the smoke everywhere, sadza on the fire in that big black pot, and the aunt was busy dishing before half the people had even sat down properly. We went by kombi, squashed right at the back, the hwindi hanging half out the door still shouting for more people even though there was no space left at all, and I remember laughing so much about that before we had even reached the gate.

The aunt kept dishing, and every time my plate was even half finished somebody was putting more on it, more meat, more sadza, more of that relish nobody can ever say no to. I told myself one more helping and then I'm done, and then it was one more after that one too, hey, you know how it goes at these things. Her murungu from work had even sent a hamper for her, drinks and everything, and the aunt was quick to put it out where everyone could see it, so the tots were not stopping either. The uncle stood up to give his speech with his beer already finished, and every two lines he was shouting are we together, and the whole yard was shouting it back at him louder than the music itself.

Somewhere before midnight the magetsi just went, whole street dark in one go, and I thought that's it, party's finished. But no, we made a plan, phone torches out, somebody found candles from somewhere, and if anything the whole thing got louder after that, not quieter. The last thing I can tell you for sure, properly sure, is the cake, everybody crowded round it, me singing off key right next to my cousin with somebody's arm round my shoulder and everybody clapping in the wrong rhythm on purpose. After that, shamwari, it is only pieces. A piece of dancing until my takkies were finished, proper finished, mud and who knows what else on them. A piece of the music still going and going. By the time we were leaving there were no more kombis running at all, so we had to make a plan and pack ourselves into a mshika-shika, four of us at the back in a seat built for three, the driver not even minding, and that is the last clear picture I have before the whole night just goes soft at the edges.

I woke up the next morning to the first wave of it proper, head banging like a drum on its own, and just the thought of food alone turned my stomach right over. The light in that room was too much, even with the curtain closed, and every small sound, a door, a spoon in a cup, was like something being hit right next to my ear. Sitting up alone took me a good five minutes, I am not joking.

The young one was already up and moving about like nothing at all had happened the night before, and of course could not resist.

The young one: Ah shame, look at you, hey. You were still going at midnight and now you can't even lift your head, is it?

Me: Don't start with me now.

The young one: I'm not starting anything, I'm just saying what I'm seeing. Boet, you look like something the cat dragged in.

Me: Eish, just bring me some tea, please.

The young one: Tea is coming now-now.

Now-now, shamwari, became almost midday by the time that tea actually came, the magetsi still not back so she had to boil the water on the gas, and I lay there the whole time not able to do one single thing about it. She'd been up since just now, the sun barely out, moving around fine like the beer from last night had never touched her at all, when we had both been at that same braai.

Lying there like that, not able to even turn my head properly, I made myself a promise right there and then, that I would never let a night run away with me like that again. Ag, and I know, I know, it is not the first time I have made that exact promise lying in that exact position, and it will not be the last either, but at that moment I meant it with my whole chest.

Small things that would take nothing at all on a normal day felt like too much that whole morning. Just walking to the gate and back to check if the magetsi meter needed anything, that alone finished me. Even folding the one blanket I'd used, a small thing, I had to sit down halfway through it. The young one offered me sadza first thing, thinking she was helping.

The young one: There's sadza from last night if you want, still warm.

Me: Aikona, no, please, just the tea.

By around midday I managed some plain bread with a bit of tea, nothing rich, nothing with oil in it, just enough to sit in my stomach without a fight, and I stayed quiet like that most of the day, not moving more than I had to, until the worst of it started lifting on its own.

And that is when the pieces started coming back properly, in flashes, one after the other, and one of them I keep turning over even now, days later, still embarrassed by it. Apparently the neighbour, the one two houses down, came to the gate well past midnight to ask us to bring the music down a little, and from what I am told I did not just talk to her, I told her, in that voice, that it is once a year and she must go back and sleep, and my cousin says I even added that she should count herself lucky people still greet her at all. Shamwari, I do not even know that woman well enough to be speaking to her like that, and every time that piece comes back to me my stomach turns worse than the beer ever managed. I owe her a proper apology, at her own gate, with something small in my hand, before I even pass her in the street again pretending it didn't happen.

By evening, hey, I was sharp again, properly, eating a full plate without my stomach arguing with me about it, thinking straight, even laughing again about the mshika-shika ride and the hwindi and the whole yard dancing in the dark after the magetsi went. The party itself was lekker, genuinely, right up to the point where my memory just stops being reliable, and that is the part I am sad about in a way, that a night that good has to end with me lying flat feeling like death and owing an apology to a woman I barely greet on an ordinary day.

So there it is, the whole thing, as much of it as I can honestly account for. I know myself well enough by now, shamwari, to know that this promise I have made will hold for a good while and then some other celebration will come along and pull me in completely again, one more helping, one more tot, one more song, and I will wake up in exactly that same state, swearing exactly the same thing all over again. Shame, that is just how it goes with us.
