Shamwari, let me tell you properly how that road went for me, because the first time I told it I made it sound like nothing, and it was not exactly nothing.

We were going down for the burial, my uncle's, and my father had made a plan the night before that we would leave before it was even properly light, because the magetsi had gone off again around nine and there was no sense being busy boiling water in the dark for tea we were not even going to enjoy. So we were at the rank early, dodging the mshika-shika that were already cutting in front of the proper kombis for passengers, and the hwindi was shouting the destination and pulling people by the arm the way they do, and we were packed in and moving before the sun was fully up.

Now, on that road, past the second growth point, there is a place where the police mount a roadblock, and it has been standing there some months now, because of the trucks that were going through with beasts that were not theirs, stolen cattle, so every vehicle on that road gets stopped and it is not only the driver's book that gets looked at, it is everybody's. The driver knew it was coming and he told us to have our IDs out in our hands already, not zipped away in a bag, because it moves faster that way, he said. But hey, nothing about that queue was fast that morning.

By the time we got there the line was long already, maybe fifteen or twenty people standing ahead of us with their bags down at their feet in that sun, and we waited there a good half an hour before it was even close to being my turn, everybody shuffling forward small small. Are we together so far. And I will tell you plainly, I was nervous, even though I had done nothing, even though my own papers were sharp and in order, because that is just how your stomach goes when a man in uniform is looking you up and down slowly and you don't know one thing about what is going on in his head.

Ahead of me in that same line was a young man, and when his turn came his papers took long, longer than anybody's. Another officer came over from the police bakkie parked at the side there and the two of them stood with him a little apart from the rest of us, going back and forth with a book of their own, asking him this and that. Nobody in that whole line said one word about it out loud. We were all watching from the side of our eyes the way you do, not wanting to look like you are looking, but shame, you could see he was young, not long out of school by the look of him, and something in me felt for him standing there like that even though I never opened my mouth about it, not then and not after.

When it was finally my turn I stepped up and gave my ID book across with both hands, the way you are taught to, and he opened it slowly and looked at the photo, then at me, then back at the photo.

The officer: Where are you travelling from and where to.

Me: From Harare, going to Mutoko, for a burial. Our uncle.

That one I answered plainly, no trouble in it, nothing to trip over. But then came the second one, and this is the kind you have to take slowly, because if you say it even a little bit wrong it starts to sound like you are hiding something, when you are not hiding one thing.

The officer: This address written here, your rural home. Is this the same place you are travelling to now.

Me: Yes. Same place. My father's home, same village.

I said it slowly, making sure my words matched exactly what was written on that page, because if it does not agree with the book they ask again, and the second time they ask it harder.

Then he went quiet and turned the pages one by one, and eish, that silence stretches long when your whole book is sitting in another man's hands and he is not saying anything at all. I stood there with my hands at my sides trying to look like a person with nothing on her mind, which is a hard thing to look like when your mind is very full.

Then, without looking up from the book, he asked me the name of the headman for that village, because it is written small on the back page and he wanted to hear it come out of my own mouth without me reading it off anything. And you know, shamwari, I nearly did not have it, that name, because at home we only ever say kumusha, we do not go around saying a headman's name every day of the week, but it came to me just in time, just before the silence got too long, and I said it, and he nodded and wrote something small next to it.

I kept my voice even that whole time and my face the same, because you learn quickly that if your voice starts to shake they hold you there longer just to find out why, so you make yourself sound plain and steady, even when what is underneath is not plain or steady at all.

The one small thing that happened, after he had finished turning the last page, he looked up at me properly for the first time and he said, not unkindly, that I could go back and sit in my seat now-now, no need to keep standing there in that sun while the rest of the line finished, and he passed the book back into my two hands the same way I had given it to him.

The officer: Sharp. You can go.

And that was the whole of it, that one word and a small tip of his head toward the bus, and I was released, just like that, after all that standing and all that slow turning of pages.

I went back to my seat and the bus sat there a good while longer taking the rest of them through, and then at last it pulled off, and I looked out the window at that flat dry bush going past on either side, my takkies still full of the red dust from standing so long at the roadside. The driver had said we would be moving again just now, and that just now had already stretched past forty minutes by the time we actually moved, the way it always does on that road.

It was only later, a good twenty minutes further down, past two more little villages, that I felt my shoulders actually come down from where they had been sitting up near my ears that whole morning. Not at the roadblock itself. Not even just after it. A good way down the road, when my legs under me went soft the way they do once you realise you had been holding them stiff the whole time without even knowing it.

That is the whole of it, and I am telling it to you now the same size it was, no bigger, because nothing bad happened to me there, nothing at all, I was let through same as everybody else whose papers were in order, hey. But it cost me something all the same that morning, that standing and answering plainly and answering carefully in their turns, keeping my face the same the whole way through, and that cost does not show if you shrink the story down to nothing, and it does not show either if you make it bigger than it was. So I leave it right there in the middle, shamwari, the same size it actually happened to me on that road, no more and no less.
