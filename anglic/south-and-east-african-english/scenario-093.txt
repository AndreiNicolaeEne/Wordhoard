Me: Howzit, sit down, all of you, hey. I'll tell you now-now, plainly, the way he'd have wanted it, no dressing it up.

It was just me and him, that last hour, everyone else gone for tea. The first thing he said, is it, wasn't about dying at all. He was busy looking out at the window, and then he asks me, quiet like that -

The father: Did they ever fix that robot on the corner? The one that's been out since Easter.

Me: Just like that, shame, like we were still sitting on the stoep some lekker Sunday. I said ja, Baba, they fixed it, don't worry about the robot now. He gave this small laugh, and then he got serious, proper serious.

The father: The bakkie. You sort out the papers on that bakkie this week, not next month, you hear me. Your brother needs it for the business. Don't let it sit there rotting.

Me: Eish, that's just how he was, hey, fixing things even flat on his back. I said sharp, I'll sort it, I promise you. He nodded, and then he called me boetie, one more time, same as always, since I was small -

The father: Ag, boetie.

Me: Just that. The nurse had looked in just now, said she'd give us the room. Then, yoh, he asked me something I wasn't ready for. He asked if I was scared. Me, not him! And I told him the truth, no lying to him at the end, I said ja, Baba, I'm scared, straight. He looked at me dead serious, like he needed me to mean it, and I did.

Then the last full thing he managed, I've kept it whole, word for word:

The father: You've done well, boetie. Sharp-sharp. Don't worry for me now.

Me: After that, hey, just quiet. I offered him water once, he just went aikona, hand up, that was it. My hand in his, I held on and held on, until he drifted, and then he was gone. That's it, that's all of it, plainly, the way he wanted it told.
