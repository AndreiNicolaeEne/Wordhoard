Eh, let me tell you the whole thing from the beginning because you missed everything that day, hey.

So this year it reached me to be the one hosting, the family said since I have the bigger house now it's my turn, and immediately the nervousness started, like properly, because you know how people watch what you serve, is it? I told myself let me not disappoint anybody, and that thinking alone is where the whole trouble started.

I sat with a paper and I was busy writing the menu for almost three days straight, because I wanted rice, and matooke, and posho, and a beef stew, and chicken, and greens, and even a salad because these days people are asking for salad too, and then I said let me add one whole fish for the elders, steamed with the sauce the way they like it. By the time I finished that list, my sister looked at it and said:

The sister: "Hey, who is going to eat all this food, is it a wedding you're planning?"

I said no no, it's fine, we'll manage, hey. That was mistake number two.

Then the numbers started climbing. First I had planned small small, maybe twelve people only. Then my workmate said she's coming with her husband and the two children. Then the aunt said she's extending the invitation to her friend from church who had nowhere to go for the holiday, and I said fine, we extend, there's always a seat, that's how it's done here. Then the neighbour heard there was cooking happening and said she's just passing by, and passing by became eating fully with everybody else. By the day itself we had reached almost twenty-two people, and I was there just holding my head.

Now the shopping. I went to the market early, got everything, the matooke, the beef, the chicken, the greens, the tomatoes, everything moving smoothly, but the one thing, the one single thing I could not find anywhere in that whole market was proper coconut for the fish sauce. I was going round and round, the women were telling me try this side, try that side, one even told me, you reach the far corner, there's a woman there who always has it. I reached, she had finished. I reached another one, finished also. In the end I sent a bodaboda all the way to a different market just for that coconut, paying the boy extra because he was complaining the traffic was bad, hey.

The night before, my friend, I did not sleep at all. I was busy peeling matooke until almost one in the morning, my hands were even paining by the end. Marinating the chicken, cutting the beef into pieces, preparing the sauce, deciding what stays in the fridge and what waits outside. I even wrote myself a proper timetable on paper: six o'clock start the fire for the beef, seven start the rice, eight the chicken, everything ready by ten before people start reaching around eleven. Very organized on paper, hey. That paper did not survive even one hour of the real morning, not now-now, not just now, nothing.

Because by six thirty I was still finishing the marinade I was supposed to have finished the night before, and already my phone was ringing off, this one flashing me because her line had finished credit, calling back to say she's leaving the house now, and me still standing there in my house clothes basically.

Then came the first real disaster. The rice. I don't know what happened, maybe too much water, or I left it there while I was busy sorting the chicken, but when I opened that pot the whole bottom had burnt completely, that smell had gone through the entire house. I nearly cried standing there looking at it, thinking what do I even tell twenty-two people now. I scraped off the top part that was still fine, set it aside, and quickly put on another smaller pot, cooking faster, just praying it finishes in time.

That's when the neighbour, the one who said she was just passing by, reached, and she reached early, before even half the food was ready. She found me standing over that burnt rice almost finished, and instead of sitting like a proper guest she tied a kanga round her waist and said:

The neighbour: "Well done, well done, but just show me where the greens are, I'm not going to sit and watch you suffer alone, hey."

She borrowed me her own kanga to tie since mine had stained already, and she stayed there chopping onions, stirring the stew, watching the second rice pot for me while I ran to check on the chicken. I don't know what I would have done that morning without her, honestly.

Then the stove itself became its own war, because now the beef stew wanted one side, the chicken wanted the other, the second rice pot needed its own space, the greens were waiting, and there were only four burners for all of it plus the oven wanting to bake something small for the children, everything shifting on and off, pots balancing on top of pots, it was proper chaos in that kitchen, hey. I was even telling the neighbour, you come, hold this one, you take that one to the side, just directing traffic like that.

But one thing, one single thing came out far better than I expected, and that was the fish. I had almost forgotten it with everything else burning around me, just steamed it plainly with onion and tomato and a bit of the rescued sauce, not even fussing over it because my mind was elsewhere entirely, and that fish came out so soft, so well balanced, I was surprised myself tasting a corner of it before serving.

So instead of starting at eleven like the paper said, we started properly serving around half past one, everybody already hungry by then, some of the small children already crying small small from waiting, but hey, that's how these things go.

First bites, and you could hear it round the table, this one saying eh, this stew, who cooked this, that one just going is it, the chicken is like this, people making those sounds you know, the mm and the eh and the is it. Then the aunt's friend from church, a very blunt woman, first time I was meeting her properly, she tasted the second rice and said straight, no decorating it:

The aunt's friend: "This rice is somehow, it's still a bit raw in the middle, shame, you were probably rushing it."

I just laughed and told her yes, I was rushing, hey, she wasn't wrong at all.

But that fish, ah, that fish disappeared first, finished before even the meat plates were halfway, people were asking is there more, someone even joked I should be selling it on the street next to the rolex seller, everyone praising it, loudest praise of the whole day by far. My workmate even said:

The workmate: "You should be hosting every month if it's coming out like this one, hey."

By the end, sitting there among all those empty plates and pots stacked everywhere, someone asked me will I ever host again next year, and I said, eh, ask me again in one year, maybe by then I'll have forgotten how tired I am today. My sister just laughed and said:

The sister: "You'll forget by next week even, you always do."

And honestly, she's probably right, hey.
