Let me tell you how that night went, because up to now I am still not believing it myself, hour by hour, the way it actually happened.

I reached at eight and already at the gate the guard was telling me two of us have not come. Two, before the shift has even started, is it? The one on the filler and the one on crates, both absent, no message, nothing. The supervisor was busy on the phone trying this one and that one, and getting nothing back from anybody.

We started anyway because the orders were sitting there waiting and the trucks come for loading by six whether we are ready or not.

By nine everything was moving, the line running, bottles going round and round, but there was a sound from the compressor that was somehow. Not loud, not something you'd stop the line for, just a knocking, like something inside was tired.

Me: This compressor, do you hear it?

Workmate: Hear what. It's running.

Me I kept my ear on it anyway, because a machine does not knock for nothing.

By half past ten the filler jammed. Not just jammed, it seized, and syrup was pouring out where it should not be pouring, all over the floor, sticky, and the bottles behind it backing up one on top of the other. That was the first real trouble of the night.

We tried to patch it ourselves first, because you don't stop a whole line for one machine if you can help it, the other stations were still needing bottles fed to them. My workmate and I were busy clearing the jam by hand while the rest kept the far end moving, but it was like fighting water with your hands.

I called the technician. It rang and rang. I flashed him after, thinking he'll see the missed call and ring me back, nothing. Twenty minutes, half an hour, still nothing. The floor was slippery with the syrup by then and one of the newer ones nearly went down carrying a crate, caught himself on the rail. Shame, he was shaking after that one.

Eventually the supervisor called the maintenance man direct, at his house, past eleven at night.

Supervisor: I know it's late, but the filler has seized, we're losing product.

Maintenance man: On a Tuesday night you're calling me for this? Couldn't it wait for morning shift?

Imagine, a man they are paying to be on call for exactly this kind of thing, grumbling like we called him for nothing. He came anyway, but the whole time he was fixing it he was saying this and that, how he's not officially on call tonight, how they should train more people on the machine, how he was already sleeping. Nobody was answering him back, we just wanted the line moving.

Supervisor: You bring the light this side. You hold it steady there.

By midnight he had it patched, not fixed properly, patched, enough to run slow. We were relieved for maybe one hour.

Then at half past twelve the power went. Full blackout, not even the small flicker before it goes, just gone, and the whole factory dark at once. The backup generator was supposed to catch it automatic but it didn't catch, it just sat there quiet. Somebody had to go and start it by hand in the dark, and that generator house is not a place you want to be groping around in at night.

Guard: Borrow me your torch, mine is finishing.

I gave him mine, and even then the generator refused two, three times before it caught. Later we heard the fuel had been low, and there was talk that the money for topping it up had been eaten somewhere along the line, nobody wanted to say by who exactly.

While the generator was down, one of the loaders, moving crates in the dark without proper light, cut his hand on a broken bottle. Not deep, but enough blood that we had to stop and bandage it properly before anyone else touched anything. Shame, the poor boy, he kept saying he's fine, he's fine, but his hand was shaking the whole time.

That is when the supervisor made the call to shut the crating side completely and only keep the filling line running, slow as it was going. He said better we finish half the order properly than push the whole floor broken in the dark and somebody gets seriously hurt this time. Nobody argued with that one.

For about an hour, maybe from two to three, it actually looked like it was turning. The generator was holding, the filler was going even if slow, the loader's hand was bandaged and he was sitting resting by the wall. We found a small table near the generator house and ate posho and beans standing around it, nobody had the appetite to sit down properly. My workmate pulled the bench over.

Workmate: Extend there, let me sit small.

Workmate: See, it's coming right now-now.

Me: Don't talk too soon, this night is not finished with us yet.

And true to that, at almost half three the delivery truck that was supposed to be loading from midnight, the one that had been sitting outside the whole time waiting for us to catch up, refused to start when the driver finally tried it. Battery flat, or something with the starter, he wasn't even sure himself.

Driver: This one is not starting, I've tried it now three times.

By then I was not even surprised. Me I just laughed, because what more.

So now we had product finished slower than planned, a truck that can't move it, and a crating side sitting idle with crates half stacked from before the blackout. The tally by four in the morning was bad. Two crates of bottles smashed from the scramble in the dark. A whole batch from the jam that had to be pulled aside because the syrup ratio came out wrong once it started running again, spoiled, can't sell that one. Hours lost that no amount of patching was getting back.

We pushed through anyway because that's the job, four to six is always the hardest part of any night shift, your body is telling you it should be sleeping and your hands are moving on their own by then. Someone found another driver, a boda was sent to go and wake him up, and by five he came, and at least the truck already loaded could finally go.

Supervisor: When you reach the depot, call the driver there, tell him we're running late.

The supervisor was on the phone again by then too, this time to the office, explaining what got lost and why, and you could hear in his voice he was tired of explaining.

By six the sky was already going that grey-blue at the edges, and we were still on the floor, cleaning the syrup off, stacking what crates could be saved, counting the broken bottles into one pile like counting them was going to make sense of the night, hey.

The day shift started arriving around half six, fresh, greeting us as they came in.

Day worker: Well done. How was the night?

Me: Ah, don't ask.

I walked out past seven, the sun properly up by then, and there was a vendor already setting up outside with the charcoal going, so I bought a rolex off him right there at the roadside, too tired even to sit down for it, eating it slowly slowly on my way to the stage for a boda. The whole ride home I kept turning the night over hour by hour in my head, still not believing all of that happened in one shift. I should have felt relieved reaching home. I didn't feel anything yet, just tired, the kind that sits behind your eyes and won't move.
