Let me tell you this whole story from the beginning, my friend, because you were not around for any of it and people think I am just boasting when I say eleven months, but eleven months it was, start to finish, and six different pairs of hands came through that yard before it was done.

It started because the house was just too small, hey. We were five in three rooms and then my wife's young sister came to stay to help with the baby, and then my own young brother finished his form four and had nowhere else to go, so now we were seven. You are busy stepping over somebody every time you want to reach the kitchen. My wife was busy sleeping the baby in the same room we were eating in. It could not continue like that, so we sat down, myself and her, and we said, this house must grow another room and a proper veranda, and while we are at it we fix that lean where the old wall was pulling away from the roof.

At the start we reckoned it small in our minds. I said to her, four people, maybe five months, we will manage. Is it? she said, because she did not believe me even then, and shame, she was right not to. In this country when you say five months you must double it in your heart and then add some, because that is how it goes.

The first wrong turn came almost before we had started. We dug the foundation trench on the line the neighbour's brother-in-law showed us, a fellow who does building work here and there, and only two weeks in did the real builder come past and look at it and just shake his head. He said the whole trench was off, not lined square to the old wall, and if we built on that the new room would sit crooked against the house forever. Eish. We had to break out what little foundation we had poured and start again from the string lines, and that cost us close to two weeks of work we had already done, gone, just like that. My wife did not say much that day. She just looked at the broken concrete and went inside.

Then there was the day the extra hands came without anybody calling them. I woke up and there were three men from the church standing at the gate with their own trowels, saying they heard we were building and they had come to help, no charge, just help a brother. I did not even ask them. Shame, look how small a thing like that can make you feel, in a good way. We laid block the whole day, and in the evening we did a small braai for them, just chicken and some sadza, and everybody sat outside until late.

The weather turned on us in the fourth month, the rains coming early and hard, and for the better part of a week nothing moved. The trench filled with water, the sand we had was turning to mud, the cement bags we had left uncovered by mistake went hard in two of them. You just sit those days, watching the yard turn to a dam, drinking tea, waiting.

Materials ran short around month six. We had budgeted the bricks wrong somehow, and the money for the next load was not there yet, so we made a plan, as we always do here. We pulled down an old outbuilding at the back that nobody was using and cleaned the bricks off it one by one, and we mixed the last of the cement thinner than it should have been for the inside partition wall, because that wall was not carrying weight. Not proper, but it held, and it is holding today.

Somewhere in there one of the men fell off the scaffold planks at the gable end, maybe three metres, and my heart nearly stopped. He landed on the sand pile, thank God for that sand pile, and he stood up holding his wrist and swearing and then laughing, because nothing was broken, just bruised bad and shaken. We knocked off early that day. Everybody needed a minute.

Then came the argument, and it was a proper one, over the roof pitch. My young brother wanted it steeper, said the rains here demand it, and the builder wanted it lower to save on the timber we did not have money to buy more of.

The brother: This roof will leak in one season if you make it that flat, I am telling you.

The builder: And where do you want me to find the extra trusses, boet? You think timber grows at the robot?

The brother: Better we wait one more month and do it right than fix a leaking roof every rainy season for ten years.

Me: Are we together on this or not, because I am the one who must sleep under it.

In the end we split the difference, went a bit steeper than the builder wanted but not as steep as my brother, and honestly it has held fine through two rainy seasons now.

The worst blow came when the mason doing the gable wall discovered, almost by accident, leaning a level against it one morning, that the whole thing was out of plumb, leaning out by more than it should, because somebody had rushed the coursing while I was away at work one week. There was nothing for it. That wall had to come down completely and go up again from the footing, brick by brick, this time with the string checked every second course. Ag, it was painful watching good brickwork go into rubble, but a leaning wall is not a small thing, it is a wall that falls on somebody's child one day.

The small win, the one that still makes me smile, was the day the frame finally stood, the trusses up and the wall plates level and the whole shape of the room there against the sky for the first time. We just stood in the yard looking at it, not even talking much, and somebody said lekker, and that was enough.

The last four nights before we finished, we worked past dark, wiring up the last of the magetsi points and fixing the ceiling boards by the light of a bakkie's headlamps pointed in through the window, because the man doing the electrics could only come those evenings. Sharp-sharp, we said to each other, keep going, almost there.

And then the day it stood finished, painted, doors hung, veranda swept, we called the same people back, the church men, the builder, the mason, my brother, everybody, and we had a proper braai, and the whole street could smell it, and people were coming past on their takkies from the kombi rank asking who is getting married, is it a wedding, and we just laughed and said no, no wedding, just a house.

When we sat down after and counted it up, it was eleven months from that first trench to the last coat of paint, and six different people who had put in real work on it besides myself and my wife, not counting the church men who only came the once but whose one day still counted for something.

Now when I stand inside that room, in the evening, with the baby who is not a baby anymore running around it, I still sometimes put my hand flat against the new wall, the one we rebuilt twice, and I think of all of that, the wasted weeks and the rain and the fall and the shouting over the roof, and honestly, my friend, it feels like the house knows what it cost. It stands quiet now. It stands straight. That is all I ever wanted from it.
