Eh, my friend, let me tell you how that thing finished today, because my legs are still paining me.

You know that drainage that passes behind our plots, the one that has been closing up since the rains started, until every time it poured the water was coming right into the compounds, even reaching the kitchen at the corner house. It had reached a level where one household alone could not touch it, the channel is too long, and the mud inside is now like porridge, somehow. So the chairman, he is the one who called it, he did not even wait for the council, because you know how those people, once money is released for such things, they just eat that money and nothing reaches the ground. So on Sunday after church the chairman was already going up and down with his little megaphone, saying tomorrow morning, everybody who has hands, come out, bulungi bwansi, bring your own hoe if you have one. And that WhatsApp group of the street, eh, it was ringing the whole night, message after message, until even people who don't usually greet each other in the morning were writing there. The few without WhatsApp, my friend, we just flashed them, one ring and drop, and when they called back we told them, just come, we are starting.

By seven in the morning we were expecting maybe the usual ten, twelve who always come for such things. But my friend, more than thirty people had come out, extra people we were not even counting, from the far end of the street, from the plots behind, even two men who normally just pass and salute from a distance, and by the time they reached, they had already picked up a hoe on their own. Some people were shouting they are coming now-now, others were saying they'll come just now, meaning who knows when, and the chairman was there from six sharp, alone with his own hoe, not moving.

Everybody was busy sorting who does what before the chairman even opened his mouth properly, some going for the hoes and pangas, some for the wheelbarrow, the children being sent for water. Me I was just given the wheelbarrow, going back and forth with the mud the whole day, nothing heavy like the mechanic, but my hands were paining by evening all the same. The chairman then divided it, right there: you people start from the main road side, you people start from the swamp side, we meet in the middle. And it is the mechanic who took the heaviest part, that man, because where the channel had completely collapsed near the culvert, somebody strong had to go down into that mud up to the knee and lift it out bucket by bucket, and it was him, he did not even complain, he was busy digging there the whole morning like it was nothing.

Now the work was going slowly slowly, everybody finding their rhythm, greeting each new person as they arrived, well done, well done, sorry for the work, before they even picked up anything. Then around midday, imagine, the bank where we had already cleared, the one near the culvert, it just gave way and slid all that cleared mud straight back into the channel, right on top of where the mechanic had been standing, and covered nearly two hours of work in one go. Eish. Some people were even laughing at first because of how the mechanic looked, but he himself was not finding it funny at all that time, hey.

That is also around when the argument started, because after that collapse, some were saying we should just cut a new short line straight to the main channel to save time, instead of following the old bend.

The mechanic: This mud is too much. Let us just cut straight here, why are we following this old bend for nothing.

The old man: Is it? You cut straight there, you will be sending all that water straight into the compound of the one with small children. That old channel bends for a reason, it was made by people who knew.

The chairman: Ehh, stop that, we are not going to fight over mud. We cut the short way for the stones only, the water itself follows the old line. Everybody happy?

And that settled it, more or less, though the mechanic went back down still shaking his head.

By early afternoon the households were bringing things out on their own, nobody had organised it, that is just how it happens. One woman brought a whole flask of tea and a plate of mandazi, another sent a boda for rolexes off the street, enough for everyone to grab one, and the neighbour borrowed us her big sufuria for the posho since ours was too small, while somebody's wife came with beans besides. We were still finishing that same posho in the evening, sitting over it long after the sun had gone down.

The children were not just standing looking, they were given their own small work, picking out all that kaveera, those plastic bags that had buried themselves in the mud over the months, filling sack after sack and competing among themselves over who fills faster. Shame, look how small those hands looked next to a full sack of that rubbish, but not one of them wanted to stop before the others did.

And the oldest woman on the street, that grandmother, she was not going to just sit on the veranda watching, no. She came out with her own small stool and her own broom, saying she will sweep the loose grass and stones off the finished part so that nobody slips. When the chairman tried to tell her to rest, she just looked at him, so he told the young ones near the shade, extend there, make room for her, and left her to it, because you cannot argue with that one, hey.

The one moment that had everybody laughing together, though, that was the goat. You know that goat that is always wandering on this street, belongs to nobody knows who exactly. That thing came from somewhere, walked straight along the cleared channel like it owns it, and fell right into the deepest part where it was still wet, and came out the other side looking like a moving heap of mud, then ran straight through where the rolexes were laid out, scattering everybody, some jumping over hoes, the old lady's stool nearly going with them. My friend, even the mechanic, mud and all, was holding his stomach laughing. That goat finished the day looking better dressed than half of us.

We had told ourselves we would be done by four, but that job only finished around seven, a good three hours past what anybody had said in the morning. By then we had cleared the whole channel from the main road side right down to the swamp, filled in those two bad holes on the road with murram, cut back all that overgrown grass on both banks, and collected maybe ten full sacks of kaveera and rubbish that we burned at the end. When that water finally ran clean and fast through the whole length of it, somebody just let out a shout, and then it was everybody, clapping, some of the women ululating, the children running along the bank following the water like it was a race.

And that evening, my friend, this street was somehow a different street, even the ones who usually just greet from their gate were sitting outside talking, comparing who did what, laughing again about that goat. It will not stay like that forever, these things never do, but for that one evening, hey, it was something.
