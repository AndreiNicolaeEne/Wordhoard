The house had been standing empty three years now, ever since the grandmother could no longer manage the steps and went to stay with the eldest in town, and last month the family finally let it go, sold to a man who wants his flats up before the rains reach. So on the Saturday everybody came, not only the closest ones but the aunties from the village side, an uncle who had not set foot near that gate in maybe ten years, a cousin's workmate with a pickup for the heavy things, even the neighbour who used to keep an eye on the compound whenever it was standing empty. The land office people had already eaten enough money out of this family over that title, nobody wanted to argue with the buyer over one more week, so we were clearing that day, finish.

We started in the sitting room. The sofas had gone soft with damp, the curtains grey from nobody opening a window in so long. The uncle stood in the doorway only looking, not touching anything, and after some time he said,

The uncle: This house has seen us, eh. This house has seen everything.

Nobody answered him properly, we were busy carrying the sofas out to the veranda one by one, everything to the veranda first, sorted after, that was the plan. Somebody was already shouting from outside for people to extend, extend, make room there, the load was coming.

In the main bedroom the auntie opened the old suitcase under the bed and found the grandmother's wedding gomesi folded inside newspaper gone brown with age, and she just sat down on the bare mattress holding it, quiet for a long minute.

The auntie: This is the one she wore, you know. I remember her in this one, at the introduction, I was small small, look how small I even was.

She wanted to keep it aside straightaway, nobody argued that one, everybody understood.

Then in the same room, in a drawer under some old bedsheets, the mother found a small tin box with foreign coins inside it and one photograph, black and white, of a woman none of us could place. Not the grandmother, not any auntie anybody could name.

The mother: Who is this one? Is it somebody from her side, the mother's side?

The eldest: I don't know that face, me. Maybe it is somebody from before, before even our time.

Nobody could explain it, so the photograph went into a bag marked "ask the grandmother," though everybody suspected she herself would not remember either.

The grandfather's reading chair caused the first real talk. It was in the corner of that same room, wooden, the arms worn smooth, and both the uncle and a cousin wanted it, each one saying it should go to his house.

The uncle: That chair is mine by right, I am the firstborn son remaining.

The cousin: But I am the one who used to sit with him there every evening, boet, he used to call me next to him on that very chair.

It was the eldest who settled it in the end, saying the chair would go to the uncle's house since he is the head now, but the cousin could come and sit in it whenever he wanted, the door was not closing on him. Everybody accepted that, hey, and moved on.

The old Singer sewing machine near the window brought its own disagreement, though, and this one took longer. Some were saying it was rusted through, good for nothing but scrap, throw it, while the mother was refusing outright.

The mother: That machine fed us. You don't know what that machine did for this family, all our uniforms came off that machine, me I am not letting it go to some scrap man.

The youngest: But it doesn't even work, it has been sitting there since I was born.

In the end it stayed with the mother, somehow nobody had the heart to keep pushing her on it.

It was under the same mattress the auntie had knelt on for the gomesi that somebody's hand found, folded flat between the springs and the frame, an old brown envelope of money, not much by today's standards but clearly hidden there on purpose, and with it a folded letter in the grandfather's own handwriting that nobody had ever seen before. The room went quiet properly then.

The uncle: Imagine, all these years, and it was sitting right there under where we were all sleeping when we visited as children.

That envelope and letter were set aside for the eldest to open properly with the grandmother present, nobody wanted to read a dead man's letter standing in an empty bedroom.

The store room at the back of the house was the one that swallowed the whole afternoon. Old school files, broken radios, a mountain of shoes nobody remembered wearing, boxes of things that had been packed "for now" maybe fifteen years back and never touched again. Every few minutes somebody was calling out from in there.

The cousin: You come, help me lift this one, it's heavy somehow.

We were carrying things out of that room slowly slowly, and still by the time the other rooms were long finished, that store room was only half done.

That was when the break came, everybody stopping to eat together in the shade of the veranda, posho and beans somebody's workmate had cooked at home and brought in a basin, and a boy sent on a bodaboda to the trading centre for rolexes for those who wanted something else, coming back balancing a stack of them wrapped in paper on his lap the whole ride.

The neighbour arrived just then, having heard the noise, and greeted everybody properly before sitting to eat.

The neighbour: Well done, well done, you people have been busy since morning I can tell.

Somebody flashed the cousin's workmate to bring the pickup round after the meal, and he came back just now, meaning not immediately but soon enough, in his own time.

The old wardrobe from the second bedroom was the piece nobody in the end wanted, cracked down one side, too heavy for any of the doorways it needed to pass through without a fight, and after much discussion about who might take it, everybody quietly agreed it was going to whoever wanted to come collect it from outside the gate before evening. The uncle and the workmate carried it out between them, complaining the whole way, and left it standing by the road for whoever would take it, and by the time we were locking up somebody already had, hey, gone, just like that.

The family Bible, though, with all the names written inside the front cover going back generations, nobody argued over at all. The eldest simply picked it up and everybody nodded, that one was staying with the eldest, no discussion needed, is it, and that was that.

The main bedroom, once the mattress was finally out and the suitcase gone and the drawers emptied, stood the longest of all the finished rooms, completely bare, just the bare walls and the window with no curtain, and the mother stood in the doorway of it for a while before anyone called her to come help with the last bits.

Whatever small things were left after all the rooms were cleared got shared out there on the veranda, plates, cups, a few chairs nobody had claimed earlier, some tools from the store room, everybody taking a little something to remember the place by, the aunties dividing the kitchen things quietly among themselves without much fuss.

By the time the sun was going down, the house stood completely empty, every room echoing when you spoke into it. The uncle went round checking each window was properly shut, and the eldest locked the front door last of all, standing there a moment with the key in the hand before putting it away for good.

The eldest: That's it then. That's the house finished.

Nobody said much after that, we just started walking to the vehicles, carrying our small shares of the place with us, the compound behind us going dark and quiet the way it would stay from then on.
