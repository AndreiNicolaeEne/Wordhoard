Mam, sit down a minute wi' us, I want to tell you summat and I want to tell it proper this time, not skirt round it like I've been doing for over twenty year now.

You know th' scar. Course you know it, you're the one as put th' plaster on it first, and you've heard us tell the tale of it more times than I've had hot dinners. Off us bike, I've allus said. Skidded on the corner by th' ginnel, went over th' handlebars, opened us leg on th' pedal. Our lass asked us about it on the beach when she were little, pointed right at it and said "what's that then," and I told her the bike story an' all, same as I told you, same as I've told everyone from th' doctor's to the lads at work. It were never true. Not one word of it. And I'm done saying it now, so give us a minute and let us get it out.

What really happened were this.

There were three or four of us ran about together that summer, me and our kid and the eldest lad off further down, and the little lass from across the way who could out-climb the lot of us if she'd a mind to. We had us spot down past the ginnel where the water pooled up under th' big tree, and there were a dare going round that whole summer that if you went in off the low branch you were nowt, everybody'd been in off the low branch, that were for babbies. You had to go higher to mean owt.

I'd backed out of it once before, earlier that summer, stood up on the branch and just couldn't make us legs do it, and climbed back down while the rest of them watched. They never let us forget it. Our kid especially.

Our kid: "Chicken again, are you? Same as last time?"

The eldest: "Leave him, he'll do it when he's ready."

Our kid: "He weren't ready last time either. He'll never be ready."

So that day, when it come round again, I went up past where I'd stood before and kept going, further than I'd ever meant to go, because I weren't having it said again that I'd backed out. The eldest were shouting up summat about mithering on about it all summer, and I just wanted them to give over, so I went right up to the top branch I could reach, higher than any of them had gone that year, and I stood there a minute with us heart going like the clappers.

The eldest: "Tha's never going that high."

Our kid: "Go on then, if tha's going, go."

And I jumped.

I misjudged it summat rotten. I don't know if it were the height or just that I weren't looking proper at the water, but I come down wrong, and it knocked all the wind clean out of us, and then there were this pain in us leg, sharp, like nowt I'd felt before, and I knew straight off there were summat under that water I'd never seen coming, a branch or a bit of old fencing or I still don't rightly know what, and it had opened us leg right up.

I couldn't get us breath to even skrike proper at first, just sort of gasped, and then when I did start skriking the others come in after us panicking an' all, and between the lot of us we got us out onto the bank, and I were bleeding summat shocking down us leg into the grass, and everybody were talking at once and nobody knew what to do.

And right there, still soaked through, cold, shaking, us leg pouring, we agreed it between us. I remember it clear as owt, clearer than I remember most of that whole summer.

The eldest: "Say tha fell off us bike. Say nowt about th' tree, or we'll all get in bother, every one of us, and we'll none of us be let down here again."

Our kid: "Aye, say bike. Everybody falls off their bike, nobody'll think owt of it."

The little lass: "Say the pedal did it. That's how you'll have the mark on you like that."

And I said aye, alright, bike, and we all shook on it there on the bank like it were some proper grown-up business deal, and I limped home with our kid holding us up on one side, and I told you the bike story before I'd even got us breath fully back, stood there dripping on th' kitchen floor.

You said put wood in th'ole, you're letting all the cold in, and then you took one look at us leg and forgot all about the door.

You: "Give it here, chuck, let's have a look at that."

And you cleaned it and you fussed and you took us up the doctor's and it got seen to and stitched and you never once doubted a word of it, why would you, it were a gradely enough story, it happened to half the kids on the street one way or another. And it healed into the scar it is now, and every single time anybody's asked us about it since, I've told them bike, right up to us own kids asking, right up to you asking us not three month back when we were sat having us brew and you caught sight of it under us trouser leg and said, do you remember that, weren't you a state that day.

I said aye, I were a right state, and let it go same as ever.

But I've been sat with it more than usual lately, Mam, and appen it's because our youngest has took to climbing everything that'll hold her, and I stood at the bottom of a tree in the park not long since watching her go up further than I liked, and us heart were in us mouth the whole time, proper frightened, the kind of frightened where you can't hardly breathe till they're down safe. And I stood there thinking, that's what you must have felt an' all, all them years, only you thought it were a bike and a pedal, a daft little accident, nowt more than that. You never got to be frightened of the actual thing. I took that off you without you ever knowing there were owt to be frightened of.

And that's sat wrong with us for weeks now, that you were made up it were summat so ordinary, and it were never ordinary at all, it were us stood up a tree too high because I couldn't bear being called chicken one more time, and it were nearly a lot worse than a scar, and none of us said owt true about it, not then and not since, not to you nor to nobody.

The others still keep to it an' all, far as I know. Our kid brought it up at Christmas, laughing, "remember when he came off his bike," and I just laughed along same as always, and I've give over doing that now. I don't want the lie living in this family no longer. It's bobbins, the whole story, always were, and it were bobbins of us to let you carry it round for twenty odd year thinking it were nowt but a bike.

So there it is. That's th' truth of the scar. It were a dare I couldn't walk away from twice, and a jump I never should have took, and something under that water none of us ever saw coming, and a story we made up between us shivering on a riverbank because we were more frightened of getting in bother than owt else.

I'm sorry, Mam. I'm sorry I let you believe it, and I'm sorry it's took us this long to tell you proper, when you'd every right to know what your own lad actually did that day, and what it actually cost. You've asked us about that scar for twenty year and got the same false answer every time, and you deserved better than that off us, you always did.

Ta for hearing us out. I know it's a lot to land on you now, after all this while, but I couldn't carry the other version no longer, not since I stood watching our youngest up that tree.

Ta-ra for now, Mam. I'll ring you Sunday same as ever.
