Reet, I'll tell it thee proper, the way my nan telled it me, sat by th' fire wi' a brew going cold in her hand cos she never could tell it wi'out stopping to have a skrike halfway through. She'd start off matter of fact and end up dabbing her eyes wi' her pinny, every time, and we'd all sit dead quiet an' wait for her to give over so she could carry on.

It starts back home, and by home I mean the owd place, over the water, where there weren't nowt for anybody by the finish. The land had failed 'em more than once and the rent still wanted paying same as if it hadn't, and there were more mouths than there were spuds to fill 'em, if tha knows what I mean. Nobody wanted to go, mind. It weren't some grand adventure. It were "stop and starve or go and appen not," and put like that it's no choice at all really, is it.

So it were the great-great-grandad went first, on his own, and he were nobbut a young fella, no wife wi' him nor nowt, just what he could carry on his back and a bit o' money sewn in his coat that his own mother had put by for him, God rest her. Nan allus said he never spoke much of the crossing, and what he did say he said only the once, and it were this. That it were weeks, not days, in the bottom of a boat wi' more folk crammed in than were decent, and there were them that didn't come off it at t'other end. He'd only ever say that much and then he'd go quiet, cos his own cousin were one of 'em that didn't make it, took poorly on the crossing and were gone inside a week, buried at sea like nowt, like she'd never been. He never gave a name to it neither, our great-great-grandad, not once in his whole life, just "the cousin," said soft, like the name itself were too heavy a thing to lift. So that's all any of us ever had of her. The cousin. That's her whole memory, passed down four generations, and I'm passing it thee now the same road, wi' no name on it, cos there never were one give.

He landed wi' next to nowt. A bag, the coat off his back, and whatever were left o' the money once he'd paid his passage, which weren't much. No work waiting, no family here, nowt. First while he were lodging off another family entire, not a place of his own at all, just a corner of somebody else's house that took him in cos he were owt but idle and could pay a bit toward the rent when he had it. That family were good to him and he never forgot it, Nan said, spoke of 'em kindly his whole life though I don't reckon anybody remembers their name now either, if we ever had it.

Work were the hardest part after the crossing itself, appen harder in its own way, cos at least the crossing had an end you could see coming. There were nowt steady for him at first. A day's carting here, a day humping sacks down at the wharf there, whatever a fella could get that didn't ask too many questions of where he'd come from. Some days there were nowt going at all and he'd come back to that borrowed corner wi' empty hands and go to bed on an empty belly, and that were that.

Then he got taken on proper, down at one o' th' mills, and that were the making of him. Started as nowt much, sweeping and fetching, but he learned the looms quick and he were good wi' his hands, and inside a year or two he were weaving hisself, and weaving's what he stopped at for the rest of his working life. Every lad that come after him in that family went the same road, my own grandad an' all, and my dad after that on the same sort o' work near enough, so it stuck, that trade, it stuck for good.

Once he'd summat put by, and it took him the best part o' two year, he sent word back and sent for the rest of 'em, his wife and the little 'uns that had stopped behind waiting on him. Two year and a bit, Nan allus said, near enough two and a half by the time they were all under one roof again, and I've thought on that since, what that must've been for the wife left behind all that while not knowing day to day how he were getting on, only a letter now and then if a letter come at all.

Grandmother: "He were near broke when they landed, love, but he were made up, made up beyond owt, first time he clapped eyes on 'em on that dock. Cried like a babby, he did, big grown man."

Me: "Did he say owt to 'em?"

Grandmother: "Not much as needed saying. He just put his arms round the lot of 'em at once, best he could manage, and telled 'em, 'reet, that's done wi' now, we're all here.' That were it. That were the whole speech."

There were one thing come over the water wi' him and it's still in this family now, kept careful, passed hand to hand down the years, a little tin box, dented down one side, that had been his own mother's, wi' a bit o' lace folded inside it that were hers an' all. It's nowt to look at, honest, a body'd walk past it in a shop and never give it a second glance, but it's the one thing that made it the whole way and it's sat on somebody's mantel in this family every year since, and it'll go on to whoever's next when I'm gone, and they'll know where it come from cos I'll have telled 'em, same as this.

They found a house, a proper terrace off one o' th' ginnels near the mill, within the first three or four year of being back together, and that were theirs, rented but theirs, and that's the house the children were born in, my own grandad among 'em. And that's the thing our nan allus said made this place home for good, not the landing, not the work, not even the house itself really, but the babbies being born in it. She'd say, "You can live somewhere and still be from somewhere else in your heart, love, but once there's a babby born under your own roof, that's it, that's home, no more looking back over your shoulder after that." I've thought that were about the truest thing anybody ever said to me.

Course, by the time it were my nan telling us, sat round wi' our brews going cold and a plate o' butties going spare in the middle, it were her own grandad she were remembering, and now it's me telling my own, and one day it'll be our kid's grandchildren getting it off somebody, appen changed a bit in the telling the way these things allus do, a detail lost here, a bit polished up there, but the bones of it stopping the same. The going, the crossing, the cousin wi' no name, the tin box, the looms, the two year apart, the house off the ginnel, the babbies. That's the whole of it.

Mither me all you like, our kid used to say when we were little and wanted it telled again for the hundredth time, go on then, tell us about the boat again, and Nan never once said no, bless her, not once, however tired she were. She'd just settle back in, have a sip o' cold tea, and start it off the same road every time. Reet then. It starts back home...

And that's me lot, chuck. Ta for listening. Put wood in th'ole on your way out, there's a right owd draught got up. Ta-ra.
