Right, I'll tell thee the whole of it, start to finish, cos everyone just sees th' end result now and thinks it went up in a fortnight. It didn't. Eleven month, near enough, and six different pairs of hands in and out of that yard before it were done.

It started cos we'd simply outgrown the place. Four of us crammed in where there were only room for three, our kid sleeping on a put-up bed in what were meant to be a box room, and the mother forever saying she couldn't swing a cat in that kitchen never mind cook a Sunday dinner in it. So we sat round th' table one night, brew each, and had a proper reckoning of it. Reckoned it'd take four of us, on and off, and maybe four month if we got a shift on. Four month. Eh, we were that far off it weren't even funny, looking back.

First wrong turn come within a fortnight, and it were a right one an' all. Set the footings out wrong, measured off the wrong wall by a whisker, and nobody clocked it till the block layer come to lay the second course and said summat weren't reet. Had to dig the lot back out, re-peg it, start again. Best part of two weeks' work, gone, just like that, for nowt. I could've skriked, honest, standing there looking at a hole where a wall should've been.

Funny thing were, not long after, one Saturday morning, we're out there having a brew and a butty, feeling sorry for ourselves over the setback, and up th' ginnel comes the brother-in-law with two of his mates off the site he worked, all in their boots, nobody'd asked them, nobody'd even mentioned it to them as far as I know. Just turned up.

The brother-in-law: Heard you'd had a bit of bother. Thought we'd give thee a hand for th' day.

Me: Give over, you didn't have to do that.

The brother-in-law: Aye well, we have now, so put wood in th'ole and mash us a brew, it's parky out here.

That day put us right back on track and a bit besides, three extra pairs of hands shifting block and mixing muck all day, and it were made up we were, honest, dead made up. That's the sort of thing that keeps thee going through a job like that.

Course, then the weather had its say. End of a wet spell that turned into best part of a week we couldn't do a thing, rain coming sideways, site like a bog, mother saying appen we should've waited till spring to start it all. We just sat in, drank tea, moaned at the sky, went and did owt else that wanted doing round the house instead. There's nowt you can do about weather, you just wait it out and get mithered by it same as everyone does.

Ran short of block about halfway through an' all, the merchant's order held up, and rather than sit idle again we made do with what were in the yard, mixed and matched a couple of courses with some reclaimed stuff off next door's old shed that were coming down anyroad. Looks a bit patchy under the render if you know where to look but nobody'd ever spot it now, and it kept us moving, which mattered more than owt else at that point.

Had a scare an' all, up on the scaffold doing the gable end, one of the lads went over, missed his footing on a board that hadn't been pinned proper. Dropped a good height, landed in the muck heap thank God, knocked the wind clean out of him and give us all the fright of our lives, but he were up on his feet within the minute, dusting himself down, calling himself all sorts for not checking the board first.

Him: I'm reet, I'm reet, stop fussing.

Me: Tha's not reet, sit thi sen down a minute, you daft beggar.

Nowt broken, thank God, just bruised summat rotten and shook up for a day or two, but it put the fear in all of us for a bit, and we were a sight more careful on that scaffold after.

There were an argument too, mid-way through, over how the roof should be tied in, whether to do it the way the joiner reckoned or the way the brother-in-law's mate said it were always done on the sites he'd worked. Proper barney it were, out in the yard, voices raised, the pair of them going at it over rafters and hangers like it were a matter of life and death.

The joiner: I've done it this way thirty year, it's the reet way.

His mate: Aye, and I've done it different for twenty and it's never fell down yet.

In the end we split the difference, more or less did it the joiner's way cos he were the one with his name on the job, and the other fella grumbled about it for a week but got over it, as you do.

The real setback though were the side wall. Once the inspector come and had a proper look, turned out it were out of true, built up crooked from early on, nobody'd caught it, and there were nowt for it but to take the whole thing down to the footings and start it fresh. That were a low point, I'll not lie, standing there of an evening looking at a pile of rubble where three weeks' graft had been. But you just get on with it, don't you. No use skriking over it.

Small mercies though, cos the day the frame finally went up and stood there, square and proper, roof timbers all sat on top looking like an actual house at last instead of a hole in the ground, that were a gradely day. Stood back with a brew in me hand and just looked at it a good while. First time in months it felt like summat rather than nowt.

Last stretch of it, the final four nights, we were out there working on past dark with a couple of lamps rigged up off an extension lead, finishing the last bits, snagging round doors and skirting, mother bringing scran out to us in the cold cos none of us would come in and eat proper. Ta to her for that, cos we'd have starved else. Everyone dead tired, hands cut to bits, but nobody wanted to stop that close to the end.

And then the day it stood finished, actual finished, doors hung, windows in, not a scaffold pole in sight, we just stood in the yard looking at it not saying much at all. Somebody said it were belting, and for once that weren't an exaggeration.

Tallied it up after, cos you always do, don't you, count the cost of it once your legs have stopped shaking. Eleven month top to bottom, near enough three times what we'd reckoned at the start round that table with our brews. Six different folk in and out helping over the course of it, not counting the two mates who only come the once. One wall built twice. A fortnight of footings dug out and redone. Best part of a week lost to rain. One lad in the muck heap. One roof argument settled by compromise. And a barm cake and a brew shared out on more days than I could ever count, cos that's how owt gets done round here, in't it, one brew and one butty at a time.

Standing inside it now, it's just home, you stop noticing the walls after a bit. But every so often I'll put me hand flat on that side wall, the one we rebuilt from nowt, and think, aye, that were a job and a half, that were. Ta-ra to anyone who thinks it went up easy. It didn't. But it stood, and it's ours, and that'll do.
