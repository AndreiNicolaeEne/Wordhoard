Right then, I'll tell thee how it went, start to finish, because it were a right year on that plot, up and down like owt.

Back end of February I were sat in th' shed with a brew going cold on the bench, trying to work out what to put in. There's only so much room down there, and I weren't going to waste it. In th' end I went for spuds down one end, a row of onions, broad beans, some carrots if the slugs'd let me, and a load of runner beans up th' canes at the back because our kid loves a runner bean and I'm not buying them off a stall when I've got ground going spare. Cabbages an' all, for scran through the back end of the year. Nowt fancy. I'm not one for growing summat you can't eat.

Weren't going in the ground yet though. You put owt in too early round here and a late frost'll have it, so I sat on my hands a fortnight longer than I wanted, mithering meself daft looking at the forecast every morning. Soon as the cold had properly gone, first week of April or thereabouts, I were down there of a Saturday with the fork and got the lot in. And do you know what, we had a right run of weather after that. Three, four weeks of it mild and soft, sun most days, and everything come up like it were made up to see us. The beans were away up the canes before I'd hardly blinked, spuds pushing through lovely and green. I remember stood at the gate one evening thinking, this is gradely, this is, if it keeps on like this I'll have a plot worth summat come summer.

Course it never keeps on like that, does it.

First thing that showed up were on the cabbages. Little white flies, clouds of the buggers, soon as you touched a leaf they'd all lift off at once and settle back down when you'd gone. Whitefly, the fella on the next plot but one called it.

The fella on the next plot but one: Tha wants to get some of that soapy water on, spray it of a morning, keeps the numbers down.

So I mixed up washing-up liquid and water in an old bottle and were out there most mornings before work giving 'em a going-over. Did summat, I'll give it that, kept the worst of 'em off, but they never fully cleared off the whole year, just simmered down to a nuisance instead of a plague. I lost a couple of the cabbages outright, rest come on but they were bobbins compared to what they should've been, all holes and half-ate leaves.

Then July came and it just stopped raining. Stopped dead. Three, four week with nowt out the sky at all, ground gone hard as the path, and I were down there every single evening with two cans, back and forth from the tap, sloshing it on the spuds and the beans because if you don't they just give up and sit there sulking. My arms were done in by the end of it, honest, up and down th' ginnel with the cans banging me legs, but you can't let it go, not after all that work getting it in.

And that's where the bother started, because the tap's shared, and there's a rota pinned up in the shed nobody much looks at till there's a drought on, and then everybody's looking at it. Fella two plots down reckoned it were his turn of an evening and I'd had it three nights running.

Him: Tha's had that tap every night this week, I've a runner bean plant gone over dying for want of it.

Me: I've spuds in the ground, they'll not wait till Thursday, I'll be quick, five minutes.

Him: Five minutes every night's not five minutes, is it.

It went on a bit like that, back and forth over the fence, neither of us giving over, both of us hot and short-tempered because the weather were doing our heads in as much as the plants'. In th' end the woman who does the secretary job for the association come down and sorted a proper timed rota, half six to seven mine, seven to half seven his, chalked up on the shed door so there were no more argument over it. Bit daft it come to that over a tap, but there you go, water does summat to people when there's not enough of it.

Best thing all year, no contest, were them spuds. I don't know if it were the early start or the good spring or what, but I dug the first row up end of July and they were smashing, proper size on 'em, not a scab or a slug hole on the lot. Filled two trugs off one row. Everyone walking past said the same.

The woman with the plot opposite: Eh, them's gradely spuds, what'd tha do to 'em?

Me: Nowt special, just got 'em in early and kept the water on.

While that were going champion, the runner beans I'd been so pleased about early on started going rotten on the vine, this soft brown mush setting in where the pods touched each other, especially after any damp spell. Lost a fair few of 'em before I twigged and started spacing the picking out and taking off owt that looked iffy straight away. Never fully sorted it, just kept on top of it, same as the whitefly.

Then end of August we had that storm come through, one night of it, wind and rain like nowt on earth, and I went down next morning to find half the bean canes over on their side, whole row of 'em flat in the mud, and the sweetcorn I'd not even mentioned yet laid down like somebody'd walked through it. Proper heartbreaking sight first thing, standing there in me wellies looking at it.

Spent the best part of a Sunday putting it right. Straightened the canes, drove 'em back in deeper this time, tied the beans back up best I could, propped what corn I could save with bits of bamboo and string. Lost a bit of it, snapped clean, nowt to be done for that, but most come back once it were stood up straight again and got a bit more sun on it.

Come the back end of the year I sat down with it all tallied up. Spuds were the star of the show by a mile, more than I could eat meself, so I swapped a good sackful with the fella two plots down, the one I'd had the barney with over the tap, for a load of his rhubarb, because I fancied doing some crumbles and he'd had a glut of it he didn't know what to do with. Funny how that goes, falling out over a tap in July and swapping veg like old pals by October. He brought us a barm cake and all one morning, said his missus had made too many, and I'll tell thee, a proper barm cake, none of yer cob or bread roll nonsense, split and buttered with a bit of the ham off, best butty I'd had in weeks, ate it down the ginnel on me way to sort the last of the beans.

Cabbages limped on, beans did all right in the end once I'd kept the rot down, corn come good despite the storm, onions were nowt special either way. Put the last of it away, forked the empty beds over, chucked some manure on for the winter and pulled the canes down and stacked 'em in the shed, put wood in th'ole and locked up for the year.

Looking back on the whole of it, I'd call it a gradely year, all told. Bit of everything in it, good and bad, same as any year on there I suppose. You get your storm, you get your dry spell, you get your barney over the tap that sorts itself out in the end, and then you get spuds like that and it's all worth it. Give over moaning about the whitefly, I says to meself, look what else tha got. Ta for listening, any road, I could go on for hours about that plot. Ta-ra for now, I'm off to put kettle on.
