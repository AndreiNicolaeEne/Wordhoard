Eh, come here, chuck, sit thi sen down a minute. I know what road you're on right now and I'm not going to sit here and tell thee it'll sort itself, because that's not owt anybody wants to hear when they're in it. But I'll tell thee about my year, the proper bad one, because appen there's summat in it'll help thee hold on.

I don't need to say which year it were, it's not about the date on it, it's about what it were. It were the year everything went from under me. Work went first, sudden like, not a slow fade, just gone, one Friday I were in and the next Monday there were no job to go to. No warning worth owt, just "we've to let thee go" and that were that. I stood outside after and I thought, right, I'll have summat else by the month end. I were made up wrong about that, I can tell thee.

First thing to go were the daft little comforts, the things you don't think of as spending till you've none of it coming in. No more brew and a bit of cake down the caff of a Saturday morning, no more chippy of a Friday, none of that. I stopped buying the barm cakes from the good baker's and got the cheap sliced loaf instead, and don't let anyone tell thee that's a small thing, because when you've had a barm cake your whole life and you're eating summat that's not even fit to call bread, it gets to thee. Stopped the little bits, the magazine, the odd pint after work, all of it, one by one, like pulling threads off a jumper till there's nowt left of the pattern.

Then it were the bigger things, when the small stuff wasn't enough on its own. Give up the car for a bit, walked everywhere, through the ginnel and back, rain or not, because bus fare were bus fare and I hadn't it spare. Turned the heating right down and told myself I were hardy, put another jumper on, told our kid it were bracing not freezing. Gave up seeing folk and all, not because I didn't want them, but because you can't keep saying no to a round or a run out without it costing thee summat different, your pride, and that's a currency and all, love, don't let anybody tell thee different.

And then there were the asking. Eh, that were the hardest part of the lot, harder than any of the giving up. I'd never in my life had to ask anybody for owt. Proud that way, always had been, always paid my own road. But it got to where I hadn't the choice, and I rang round and asked, and it were like pulling my own teeth out to do it.

My workmate: "Tha should've said summat sooner, I'd have sorted thee."

Me: "I know, I know, I just didn't want to be a bother."

My workmate: "Give over, that's what folk are for."

She lent us enough to see me through a couple of month, and I paid every penny back the minute I could, but I'll tell thee, taking it were worse than owt else that year, worse than the cold, worse than the walking. It sits on thee different, borrowing off someone you know.

Then there were the stretch, near enough six month, where there were just nowt doing. Applied for everything, went for interviews that came to nowt, sat by the phone that didn't ring. Some days I'd not speak to a soul from morning to night bar the one at the counter down the shop, and even that were just "ta" and "ta-ra" and out again. That's a long dark stretch, that is, when the days start running into one another and you can't tell a Tuesday from a Thursday because nowt marks one out from t'other.

There were one day in the thick of it that were the very bottom, and I'll not forget it. Stood in the kitchen with the leccy meter beeping at us and about four pound left in the account and I just sat on the floor, back against the cupboard, and skriked. Proper skriked, the sort you can't stop once it's started. My mother rang right in the middle of it, and I nearly didn't answer, but I did.

The mother: "What's up with thee, love, you sound rough."

Me: "I'm alright, just a bit low."

The mother: "Don't gi'e me that. What's happened."

And I told her, in the end, all of it, and she went quiet a minute and then she said summat that's stuck with me since.

The mother: "You put wood in th'ole and you sit tight, and it'll turn, it always turns."

It weren't advice exactly, but it were summat to hold, and I held it.

Then there were a stroke of luck and all, small but it mattered. A fella I'd not spoke to in years, from an old job, rang us out the blue asking did I fancy a few days' work, covering for someone poorly. It weren't much, few days here and there, but it were summat, and it kept us from going right under when I were closest to it. Funny how it's never the big grand things that save thee, it's some daft little bit of luck landing at the exact right minute.

After that it started, slow, proper slow mind, coming back. A few days here, a bit more there, and it took the best part of eight month before I'd owt like steady work again, and I mean proper steady, not just scraping. Every little bit built on the last, and I remember the first time I bought a barm cake from the good baker's again and near enough cried over it, daft as that sounds. It's the little things that tell thee you're climbing, not the big ones.

What I learned, and I'll tell thee this plain because it's the truth of it: it's not always the folk you'd have put money on that show up. Some I thought were close never asked how I were, not once, and some I'd hardly count as close turned up with a bag of scran and no fuss made about it. You learn quick who's just there for the good times and who's there full stop. And about my sen, I learned I were harder than I thought, and softer and all, that I could go without and still stand, and that asking for help doesn't make thee lesser, whatever it feels like at the time. It only feels like that because you've never had to.

It started feeling survivable somewhere in that climb back, not one single day, more a slow turn, like when you're stood at the top of a hill and you look back down and you can see how far up you've come without noticing the climbing of it. One day you catch yourself laughing at summat daft and you think, eh, I'm alright, I'm actually alright.

So that's my year, chuck, the reet bad one, and I'm telling thee it not to make out mine were worse or owt daft like that, every hard year is its own hard year and yours is no less for not looking like mine. But I know you're in the thick of it now, I can see it on thee, and I'm not going to give thee some belting speech and walk off. What I'm saying is I'm here, proper here, not just saying it. You need someone to sit with, ring us, any hour, I mean that. You need a hand with owt, the shopping, a lift somewhere, someone to sit and say nowt with, I'm good for it. Don't mither about being a bother, because you're not, and I'll not have thee going through it on your own the way I near enough did. Ta for listening, love. We'll get thee through this an' all.
