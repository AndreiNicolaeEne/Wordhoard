Eh, I'll tell thee summat now, and give over laughing before I've even started, because it in't funny, love, it were flippin' murder at the time, even if it makes a gradely tale of it now.

I were forty-odd before I ever put a blade to th' ice, and don't ask me why I picked that of all things to take up, I couldn't rightly tell thee sen. Everyone round me had learnt as a kid, dragged down some rink by their mam of a Saturday morning while I were sat at home doing nowt of the sort. So I thought, reet, I'll have a go now, better late than never. I told our kid what I were planning and she near fell off her chair laughing.

Our kid: Tha's joking me. At thy age?

Me: I am not joking thee. I've paid for six weeks of lessons and I'm not backing out now.

Our kid: Well, don't come crying to me when tha's flat on thy back.

She were right an' all, though I'd never tell her that to her face, not in a month of Sundays.

First lesson were an absolute disaster, I'll be honest with thee. The instructor stood theer, patient as owt, but he didn't mince his words neither, and I appen needed hearing it plain.

The instructor: Tha's not going to pick this up quick at thy age, tha knows. Takes longer when tha's older. Body forgets how to learn summat new.

I didn't want to hear that, but he weren't being unkind, just straight with me. My legs wouldn't do a thing my head were telling them to do. I'd tell my ankle to hold firm and it'd go every which way but firm. There were kiddies half my age, seven, eight year old, skating rings round me inside three days flat, laughing not unkindly but laughing all same, and I felt daft as a brush stood theer clinging to th' barrier like it were the only solid thing left in the whole world.

That first week my body ached in places I'd forgot a body could ache. Backs of my legs, sides of my ribs where I'd been twisting funny trying to keep upright, muscles under my arms I didn't know existed till they were screaming blue murder at me every time I moved. I were walking like an old crock all week, and our kid mithering me summat rotten about it every time I saw her.

Our kid: Tha's walking like tha's had an accident.

Me: I have had an accident. Several. Give over.

And then there were the fall. I'll tell thee about the fall proper, love, because it were the worst of it by a country mile. I'd got a bit cocky, thought I were getting somewhere at last, tried summat I'd no business trying that early, and down I went, hard, flat on my back, and it knocked every last bit of breath clean out of me. I lay theer on that ice looking up at the lights, couldn't get a breath in for what felt like an age though it were only seconds really, and I could've skriked right theer in front of everyone, I were that close to it. I thought, that's it, that's me done, I'm too old for this carry on and I'm going home and I'll not be back.

The instructor: Tha alreet down theer?

Me: I'm packing it in. This in't for me.

The instructor: Get thi breath back first, then decide owt.

I did get my breath back, and I still wanted to pack it in, and I said as much to the friend who'd talked me into this whole business in first place.

My friend: Tha's not quitting after one fall.

Me: It weren't one fall, it were the whole week, and I've had enough of it.

My friend: Come once more. Just the once. If tha still wants to give over after that, I'll not say another word to thee about it.

So I went once more, mostly to shut her up if I'm honest, and I'm made up now that I did, though I'd not have admitted that at the time for love nor money.

After that it were slow, chuck, slower than owt I'd tried before in my life. Weeks and weeks of getting one small thing reet after getting it wrong over and over, while a little crowd gathered by th' barrier of a Saturday afternoon, some of them encouraging, shouting me on, and some of them plain enjoying the spectacle, I'll not lie to thee about that.

The one at the barrier: Go on, tha's nearly got it, keep going.

Another at the barrier: Eeh, watch this, she's off again, look.

I learnt quick not to measure my sen against the little ones flying past like it were nowt to them at all. That road led nowhere but misery and mithering my own head. The only comparison ever worth owt were against my own sen a month back. Was I better than I were at start of last month? Aye, a bit. Reet, that'll do then, keep going.

Then, about fourth month in, summat happened that I still can't rightly put proper words to for thee. It weren't owt I understood in my head first, it were more a feeling that came up through my legs of its own accord, like my body had been listening all along and just hadn't bothered saying owt about it to me. All of a sudden the thing that had been flat impossible for months were only difficult, which after what I'd been through felt like nowt at all by comparison.

The instructor: Now then. Tha's got it.

Me: Have I?

The instructor: Aye. Don't ask me how tha did it. Just don't stop doing it now.

That first proper lap without going down felt belting, I'll tell thee, better than owt I'd felt in years.

I never did catch them fast little ones up, and by the end I'd made my peace with that proper. There's some things tha's not meant to win at, only to do, and I'd rather do a thing gradely at my own pace than be first and sloppy about it. I could do things by the end I'd never have believed I could do at the start, plain and ordinary to look at maybe, nowt spectacular, but mine, won fair, every last one of them.

Looking back on the whole season now I can't help but smile at it, chuck, the whole daft humiliating business of it, the falls and the aches and being laughed at gentle-like by an audience most Saturdays. It were bobbins for a good long while there, proper bobbins, and now it sits gradely in my memory, folded in with a brew and a butty after every session, walking home down the ginnel with my legs like jelly, our kid's mithering, my friend not letting me give over when I wanted to most.

We'd always stop at the caff after, and it were a barm cake every time, mind, never a cob and never a daft roll, a proper barm, hot from under the cloth, with the scran steaming and the brew going cold while we talked over the day's falls.

So that's thy answer, love, when tha asks why I did it and why I don't regret one bit of the pain that came with it. Appen it were daft starting so late. Appen I looked a right state most of that first month, clinging to that barrier like grim death. But I'd do it again the same road, every fall included, ta very much, and that's me told thee the whole of it now. Put wood in th'ole on thy way out, I'm parched and I'm off to put kettle on. Ta-ra for now.
