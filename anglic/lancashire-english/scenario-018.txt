Right, give over shuffling about and siddown, I'll tell it proper since he's stood there grinning like a cat wi' a tin o' cream and saying nowt. He never does an' all, leaves the talking to me, always has, tonight included.

So. How we met. Nowt grand, no big to-do. He come into the bakery where I were working of a Saturday, this were donkeys back now, and he stood there dithering over the tray same as everyone does of a morning, and then he says to me, right there at th' till, bold as brass...

Him: I'll have a cob then, love, wi' cheese in.

Me: A what?

Him: A cob.

Me: There's no cobs here, chuck, it's a barm. Tha's not from round here then, is tha.

And he just looked at me, dead serious, and says he'd ask for whatever he liked, and I told him he could ask all he wanted but he'd get nowt till he called it right. He stood there a good minute working out whether I were being funny or whether I meant it. I meant it. That's stuck wi' us thirty-odd year now, he still can't order owt in that shop without somebody behind the counter correcting him, and it's usually me.

I'll be honest wi' you all, I weren't sure at first. Not because of him exactly, he seemed reet enough, bit full of himself maybe, but I'd had me fingers burnt not long before that, some other fella who said all the right things and meant none of 'em, and I weren't in a rush to get mithered again by another one just the same. So I give him nowt for weeks. Served him his barm, took his money, said ta, and that were it. But he kept coming, same time every Saturday, rain or shine, and he never pushed, never got funny about it, just turned up steady as owt. That's what wore me down in the end, if I'm honest, not owt he said, just that he showed up. He said he'd be there and he were there. Appen that don't sound like much, but I'd not known that in a fella before.

So he finally asked us out proper, and I said aye go on then, and we went for a drink down th' local, nowt fancy, and it were grand, it were. But we'd only just got started when his work sent him off, he were a fitter then and they'd a big contract on over Barrow way, months of it, and there were nowt he could do about it, work's work. So it were phone calls and him coming back weekends when he could get the coach, and me sat waiting of a Friday not knowing if he'd make it through wi' the roads. It weren't romantic, whatever anybody tells you. It were hard going and lonesome wi' it, living off scran from the freezer and phone calls that cut off halfway through, and there were times I thought give it up, it's not worth the mither. But we just kept at it plain and simple, he come back when he said he would, I waited when I said I would, and eventually the job finished and he were home for good.

Now he never come back from one of them weekends without summat daft in his pocket for us, off the arcades on the front, tuppence machines, and one week he won us this horrible little troll thing wi' the hair stood up, ugliest thing you ever saw. And I've had it on me key ring ever since, some of you've clocked it, it's still there now, been on every set of keys I've owned for thirty year, gone bald in patches. He still calls it "the ambassador." Don't ask me why, he's never once explained it and I've stopped asking.

I knew it were serious when I caught myself putting his brew on before he'd even asked for one. Just automatic, like I'd always done it. That were it for me, quiet as that, nowt dramatic. He found out different, mind. I'd told our kid down the phone it were getting serious, thinking he were out the room, and our kid, being our kid, couldn't keep her gob shut and told him straight to his face next time she saw him.

Our kid: I hear it's serious then.

And he come round to ours after, looking dead pleased wi' himself, and I had to own up I'd said owt at all.

The asking now, he'll tell you himself it were nowt like the pictures. No down on one knee in some fancy spot. We were out the back in the ginnel putting bins out, freezing, chips going cold in me hand, and he just come out wi' it, matter of fact, like he were asking did I want salt and vinegar.

Him: Go on then. Shall we get wed or what.

Me: Is that it? Is that the whole speech?

Him: Aye. Well? You going to eat them chips or what.

That were the proposal. I said aye. We ate the chips.

About a year after that we were wed, give or take. Me mam were made up straight off, had him fed and watered and part of the furniture within a fortnight, no messing. His mam took a bit longer to come round, mind, she'd her own ideas about who he should've settled wi', and it were a good few months of frosty dinners before she thawed. But she got there. Come the day itself she were crying harder than anyone in the room.

And the wedding day, you can have all the photos in the world, and there's one thing off that day none of them ever caught, which is our kid, stood outside the register office doorway before we went in, absolutely skriking her eyes out, mascara everywhere, trying to tell me it were happy tears while hiccupping summat rotten. Nobody got a picture of that. I see it clear as owt every time I think back on it, clearer than any photo on the wall at home.

We've had our stretch since an' all, same as anybody does. There were a bad year a few year back, when he were laid off and money were bobbins for the best part of it, proper hard going, and I won't go on about it because you all know, and it's done now. What carried us through it were exactly what got us through the Barrow days if I'm honest, he turned up, every day, didn't run off, didn't skulk about feeling sorry for himself, just put wood in th'ole behind him and got on wi' it beside us. That's the whole of it really. Nowt fancier than that.

And what's stayed exactly the same since that first Saturday morning wi' the cob that were really a barm, he's still full of himself, I'm still telling him what for, and he still can't order a butty right to save his life. Thirty-odd year and he's the exact same daft article he were then, and I wouldn't have him any other road, gradely or not.

So. Get your glasses up, the lot of you. To him, a belting lad underneath it all, whatever he says he ordered, and to us. Ta for listening. Ta-ra, I'm done, I'm sat back down before I start skriking an' all.
