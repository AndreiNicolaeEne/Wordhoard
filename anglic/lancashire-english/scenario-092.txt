By the time it got to gone midnight the house had thinned right down, coats going off the bannister one by one, folk letting themselves out down th' ginnel same as they'd come in, till it were just the five of us left in the front room with the gas fire hissing and the brew gone cold and stewed on the hearth because nobody could be bothered getting up to see to it again.

The mother had her cardigan pulled tight round her even though it were warm enough in there, and she were the one started it off, quiet, like she were only thinking out loud.

The mother: D'you remember he used to stand at that window there of a morning, watching for the milk float, like it were the only thing worth getting up for.

The eldest: It were the paper he were watching for, not the milk. He'd stand there till he saw the lad on the bike and then he'd be straight out the door in his slippers, rain or no rain.

The mother: Were it the paper? Aye. Aye, you're reet, it were the paper. I always get that bit wrong, don't I.

She laughed a bit at herself then, proper laughed, and it set our kid off laughing too, one of them laughs you don't mean to let out at all, and nobody minded it, it were nice hearing it in the room if anything.

The neighbour: He'd shout it down the hall an' all, soon as he'd got it off the step. "Papers here. Wood in th'ole after." Same every morning of his life, that were.

Me: He did say that. Word for word, he did, every single morning.

The neighbour: "Papers here. Wood in th'ole." (saying it exact, in his voice, the way he used to say it)

Everyone went quiet a second at that, like they could properly hear him stood there saying it, and then our kid said summat none of the rest of them had heard before.

Our kid: D'you know he used to slip the lad on the bike an extra pound most Fridays. Never said owt about it to any of us his whole life. Found his little book after, where he'd wrote it down every week for years.

The mother: He never!

Our kid: He did. It's in the drawer, I'll show you after.

Nobody said owt for a minute after that, the room just sat with it a while, and then the neighbour got up to see to the food again, more for summat to do than because anyone were that hungry.

The neighbour: Right, who's having another butty, there's ham left, and there's barm cakes still under that tea towel if any of you can manage a bit more scran.

Our kid: Ta. Go on then, just a small one.

The eldest: (to the mother, half under her breath) You never did tell me why you two fell out over that caravan business.

The mother: Give over, not tonight.

The eldest: I'm only saying.

The mother: I know what you're only saying. Not tonight, chuck, eh.

It dropped there, the way these things do, neither of them pressing it any further, and the neighbour came back round with the plate and everyone took summat off it without really looking, just to have summat in their hand.

Me: D'you remember the barm cake business though. That were a proper carry-on, that were.

The eldest: Oh, don't start.

Me: He wouldn't have a cob in the house. Wouldn't have it at any price. Our nan brought a bag of cobs round one Christmas thinking she were doing everyone a favour, and he wouldn't touch his dinner till somebody went up th' road and got proper barm cakes in.

The mother: He said a cob were nowt but a roll wearing a different coat, and he weren't having it in his house.

The eldest: And he said that every single time an' all, not just the once. Every Christmas, same speech.

Our kid: He were that stubborn over nowt. A barm cake. That's all it ever were.

The neighbour: Gradely fella though, whatever else you say about the barm cakes.

That got a proper laugh, and it settled something in the room, because that were him all over, weren't it, digging his heels in over the daft little things and being made up with himself after for winning. Nobody needed to say it out loud but you could tell they'd all landed on the same thought at once, that that were the thing they'd remember him by longest, not the big stuff at all, just that.

It were quiet a bit then, only the fire going, and it were our kid started skriking, not loud, just quiet, tears running while she still had her cup in her hand, and nobody stopped talking or made a fuss over it, the mother just put a hand on her knee and carried on same as before.

The mother: He were a belting dad, whatever else. Bobbins at singing, mind, you all remember that.

The eldest: Don't remind us. His singing were shocking.

The neighbour: Absolutely shocking, God rest him, shocking.

They were all smiling through it now, our kid an' all, still with the tears on her, laughing a bit too, the two things going at once the way they do at a time like this.

After a while it turned to the practical, the way it does when everyone's tired and there's nowt left worth saying that won't set somebody off again.

The eldest: I'll see to the flowers in the morning, and I'll ring round about the cars.

The mother: I'll do the order of service with the vicar, he wants a word Thursday, appen about the hymns.

Me: I'll sort the buffet for after, I know a woman does it reasonable enough.

Our kid: I'll do the announcement for the paper, first thing.

The mother: Ta, all of you. I don't know what I'd do else, honest.

Nobody argued over who should do what, it just fell out plain between them, and that were that settled, no more said.

It got to gone two and the neighbour were the only one still properly awake, the rest of them gone heavy-eyed and quiet in their chairs, our kid still sniffing a bit but calmer now, and he stood up with what were left in his glass, not much, and held it up without any ceremony about it at all.

The neighbour: Go on then. To him. Gradely fella, daft over barm cakes, couldn't sing a note to save his life, and we're all sat here tonight because of him one way or another. To him.

The mother: To him.

Our kid: To him. Ta-ra, love.

And they drank what were left and sat back down, and nobody said owt else for a good while after that, just let the fire do its thing while the house settled round them, quiet, and the brew stopped mattering at all.
