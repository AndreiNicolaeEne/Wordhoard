Me: Go on then, give us it proper this time. Th' butter pie. I'm sick o' guessing at it.

The aunt: Eh, give over, I've told thee twice a'ready. Put wood in th'ole first, it's parky in here. Right. Sit thissen down and don't mither me while I'm thinking.

Me: I'm not mithering, I'm listening.

The aunt: Taters first, that's your main one. Good three pound of 'em, big floury sort, none o' them waxy ones our kid brings back from t' big shop, they go to mush wrong way. Peel 'em, slice 'em not too thin, not too thick neither.

And onion - oh, I nearly left that out an' all, there's an onion in it, one big 'un, sliced fine. I always forget to say that bit and then folk wonder why it's got nowt to it. Put it back in, aye, the onion's in, that's not right without it.

Me: Right. Taters, onion. What order then?

The aunt: Taters int' pan first, parboiled, not cooked through mind, just softened - and that's the bit her mother, my nan, always went on about summat fierce. Never rush them taters, she'd say, stood right over thee. Rush it and they go to slop int' oven, and then it's bobbins, ruined, the lot.

Layer it up int' dish then, tater, onion, tater, onion, salt and pepper through it, dot o' butter on top - and honest, chuck, I've never weighed that butter in me life, I just go till it looks enough. Judge it by eye, always have.

Me: How d'you know when it's done, if there's no clock on it?

The aunt: You don't want no clock. It's done when th' pastry's gone gradely golden and th' smell's right through th' house, out int' ginnel and all. That's when.

Me: Could I use margarine 'stead o' butter?

The aunt: Appen you could, at a push, it'll do. But it's not reet, not proper belting like hers were. Ta, love, kettle's boiled, get us a brew and I'll do us a barm cake while you're at it.

Me: Ta-ra then, I'm off to write it down before I lose it.
