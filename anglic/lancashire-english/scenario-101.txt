The wife: Eh, you should've seen it, love. Went in and nearly walked straight past one of 'em, I did, the one who used to sit next to me in form two. Great big beard on him now, gone grey an' all. I says to him, "is that you?" and he says, "aye, thirty-odd year'll do that to a fella."

The husband: And then there's the other one, exactly the same, not changed a bit. Same laugh, same daft jokes, still wearing that same sort o' jacket he'd have had on at sixteen.

The wife: Reet, and think what she were like at school, quiet as owt, wouldn't say boo to a goose. Well now she's stood up in front of a room full of folk running the whole do, gob on her like nobody's business. Couldn't credit it were the same woman.

The husband: Somebody said the one from down our row weren't coming, mind. His hip's bad, can't manage the stairs to th' club no more.

The daughter: Aw, that's a shame, that.

The wife: And you'll never guess, word is the lad who left school with nowt, no exams, nothing, he's gone and set up his own building firm, doing reet well an' all, three vans on th' road now.

The husband: Made up for him, I were, when I heard that.

The wife: Us two though, chuck, we've not changed that much, have we?

The husband: Give over, course we have, you still mither me same as ever, and I still can't hear a word over th' telly.

The wife: Aye, appen. But we scrubbed up alright for it, all told. Gradely night, that were, brew and a butty after and everything. Buffet were a bit bobbins, mind, but nobody minded.

The husband: Ta for sitting there listening to us bang on about it, love. We'll not leave it another twenty year, eh, do it again next year, before we're past knowing who's who.

The wife: Ta-ra now, love. Put kettle on.
