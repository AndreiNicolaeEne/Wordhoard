Eh, chuck, sit thee down and I'll tell thee the whole of it, cos tha missed a right day and no mistake.

We were meant to be off by eight, van booked for half eight, and I were still in bed at quarter to nine. Don't ask me how. I'd set two alarms and all, and I just switched the pair of 'em off in me sleep like a woman possessed. Woke up and it were proper daylight coming through the curtains and I thought, oh no, no no no.

And that were before I'd even got out th' door. First thing, dead first thing, I went to put me key in the front door to lock up behind us and it snapped off in the lock. Just snapped, clean off, half of it still stuck in there. Stood on the step in me pyjama top swearing summat rotten while the van driver were sat outside beeping like I'd forgot he existed.

So we're behind before we've started, van driver mithering us to get a shift on, and who turns up, not asked, not rung, nowt, but my friend's husband, in his overalls with a flask under his arm like he'd heard it on the wind. Never said owt about it after either. Just started carrying boxes like it were his own house he were moving. I could've kissed him. Didn't, obviously. But I could've.

Now the thing that broke. I'd wrapped the dresser mirror, the big one off me nan, wrapped it in three blankets and bubble wrap and told everyone twice, twice, mind the mirror, and it were my own cousin, of all people, stood it up against the wall in the hall to answer his phone and it went straight through the bannister spindles and cracked corner to corner. The shout that went up. Honest, the whole street must've heard it. My mother went "OH NO" like somebody'd died, proper hand over the mouth and everything, and I just stood there looking at it not able to say a word. Belting mirror an' all, that were, and now it's bobbins, in two pieces wrapped up in the shed waiting for me to decide what to do wi' it.

And then there's the box. One box, just the one, had all sorts in, the good crockery and some of me dad's old records, and it went from the front room to the van and it never came out the other end. Never. We looked everywhere, rung the driver a fortnight after, looked under seats, in the loft of the new place thinking someone had put it up there daft, nothing. Gone. Vanished off the face of the earth. I still think about that box.

Then there were the argument. Oh, the argument. The wardrobe, the big oak one, weighed a ton, and my brother, our kid, wanted to carry it flat, said it'd never go through upright, and the friend's husband said no, on its end, has to go on its end or you'll never get round the turn on the stairs. Proper stood there in the hallway shouting at each other over the top of this wardrobe like two men deciding a war.

Our kid: It'll not go flat through that door, tha daft article, it wants to go on end.

The friend's husband: On end it'll not clear the ceiling on the landing, we tried that with the last one.

Our kid: Well it's not going flat either, look, it's wedged.

And it were wedged. Proper wedged, halfway up the stairs, at that turn where the stairwell goes narrow, and no matter how they twisted it, tipped it, swore at it, it would not go through. Appen half an hour we were at it, taking drawers out, taking the door off it, and even then it caught on the newel post and took a right chunk out the paintwork going past. My mother stood at the bottom going "mind the wall, mind the wall" the whole time which helped nobody.

By dinner it started chucking it down. Proper Preston rain, not a shower, the kind that comes sideways, and half the boxes were still stood in the front garden waiting to go on the second run. We were running back and forth with bin bags over us heads like a right shower of idiots, and the cardboard on the bottom boxes started going soft, you could hear it, that sound when a box starts to give.

And that's when the scran saved us, I'll tell thee that for nowt. My mother had gone to the shop earlier, before all this kicked off, and got barm cakes, proper barm cakes mind, not cobs, not rolls, she'd have had summat to say if anyone'd brought a cob within a mile of that house, and ham and a bit of cheese, and she'd made a brew in the one kettle we'd not packed yet. We stood there in the kitchen, empty units, boxes everywhere, rain hammering the window, eating butties off the worktop cos there were nowhere to sit, and honest, chuck, it were the best butty I've had in me life. Gradely, that were. Proper gradely.

By the last few loads I were done in. Legs like jelly, hands rubbed raw off the tape and the box edges, and every stair felt like ten. My friend's husband were still going strong, don't know where he got it from, kept saying "one more, one more" and there were always one more. I were past talking by then, just grunting and pointing.

And then it were just me, on me own, before the van went for the last time, walking through the rooms one last go round. Every room empty, marks on the carpet where the sofa'd stood for fifteen year, the pale bit of wall where the mirror should've hung, our kid's old room with the wallpaper he'd picked when he were seven still on it under the paint. Stood in the doorway of what were the kitchen a long minute, not doing owt, just looking. Give over, I told meself, tha's not one for skriking over a house, but I'll not lie, I had a right good go of it, on me own, nobody there to see. Then I put the lights off, one by one, and put wood in th'ole behind us for the very last time.

Van finally pulled up outside the new place gone eight at night, pitch black, rain still going, and we'd less than half what we started th' morning with, no mirror, no box, a chunk out the wallpaper, paint scraped off the wall we'd left behind us. Neighbours must've thought we were mad, unloading a van by torchlight at that hour. But we got the kettle on before owt else went in, and my mother said, right, this'll do, this'll do us gradely, and that were that.

So aye. That were the day. Ta for listening to the whole tale of it, love, cos I've not told it proper to anyone yet. Ta-ra now, I'm off for a lie down.
