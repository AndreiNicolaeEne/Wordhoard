Sit thi down properly, our kid, get thi a barm cake off the side if thee's hungry, and give over mithering. I'll tell it thee once, right through, and then that's yer lot for tonight. It always comes up the same road anyroad. Some soul new to the street asks why nob'dy cuts down past th' ginnel by th' old lodge once dark's properly drawn in, and everybody goes quiet over their brew a minute, and then it falls to whoever's oldest in the room to say it out loud, which tonight is me, so give over fidgeting and listen proper, because I'll not say it twice.

Nob'dy's ever seen the thing right, not properly, not so as they could tell thee what it looks like, because it in't really a looking thing at all, not first off. It's a hearing thing. It comes at thee through yer ears before it's owt to yer eyes, if it's ever owt to yer eyes at all, some say never, that it's voice and nowt else, all the way through, and I'm not one to argue different. What it does, and does every time it's ever done owt, is call. And it dun't call in some voice thee'd not know, some stranger's voice, that'd be nowt, thee'd just walk on past. It calls in the voice of whoever it is thee loves best in the world, dead on, note for note, reet down to the way only that person calls thee and nob'dy else ever could. And it's not calling thee for company. It's calling thee off the path, away from the road where somebody might hear thee shout back, out towards where there's nob'dy at all.

It dun't come whenever it likes, mind, and that's summat folk forget to say when they're telling it quick. It's after midnight only, never before, and it's on them nights when there's no moon up at all, not a sliver, not owt, reet black as th' inside of a coal hole. Any other night thee can walk past th' ginnel whistling if thee's a mind to, and nowt'll come of it.

Round here it's tied to one place and one place only, and I'll not have thee thinking it's owt bigger than that, because it in't, it's that stretch down by th' lodge, where th' ginnel comes out along the water afore it bends back up towards the road. That's it. That's the whole of it. I've heard folk two towns over claim their own version, their own spot, and appen they have, but that's theirs, this is ours, and I'll only ever vouch for this one bend of this one water.

I were nobbut little mesen when I first heard it telled, sat where thee's sat now, and it were our grandmother as telled it, on a night much like this one, wood on th' fire and the door shut against the cold...

The grandmother: Put wood in th'ole, our kid, and I'll tell thee about th' ginnel, but only if thee sits still and dun't skrike when it gets to the bit that wants skriking at.

, and she telled it dead straight, mind, reet plain, not a flicker on her, same as she'd tell thee the price of a loaf, and that were somehow worse than if she'd laughed, because a laugh thee can shrug off and plain fact thee can't.

First thing anybody'll show thee for proof, if thee goes down there in daylight when it's safe to look, is the ground. There's a patch right by where the ginnel meets the water, no bigger than a rug, and nowt grows on it, never has, not grass, not nettle, not owt anybody's ever tried planting there out of spite or curiosity, and round here we've tried plenty of both. Bare as a scrubbed table, right in the middle of everything else growing wild.

Second thing, and this one always comes secondhand, the way these things do, our neighbour's brother, who I never met mesen, swore his dog wouldn't go within twenty yard of that bend, not for scran, not for owt, just planted its feet and howled and near pulled his arm off getting away, and that's a dog, which dun't know owt about stories and in't got the sense to be frightened of a tale it's never been telled.

But the one that gets telled in full every time, word for word, same as it always has, is about an uncle of mine, years back now. He'd had his butty and a couple at the pub, same as most nights, and were walking home past th' ginnel of a moonless night, nowt unusual in that, he'd done it a hundred times, and he heard his own name called off to the side, away from the path, in a voice he'd have known anywhere in this world, and he'd gone three, four steps towards it before summat stopped him dead and turned him round instead, he could never rightly say what.

And what he swears to this day, ta or no ta whether folk believe him, is this...

The uncle: I heard mi own mother call mi name, plain as owt, same as she'd call me in for mi tea, and I'd have followed that voice into the water and never thought twice, only summat in mi legs knew afore mi head did that summat were wrong, and it stopped me.

Now here's the bit that makes even them as dun't believe a word of it go quiet a second, that man were a gradely sort, straightest, dullest, most matter-of-fact soul thee could meet in a month of Sundays, wouldn't tell thee a lie about the weather let alone owt like this. And here's the other bit, just as true, that gives the doubters their opening to laugh it off anyway soon as they've had their quiet second, like I say, he'd had a couple that night, same as most nights, and it were pouring down, and there's an owl nests along that water thee can hear cry summat pitiful in the wet, and appen that's all it ever were.

I'll tell thee straight, it changes a bit dependin' who's telling it, our aunt tells it belting, that gentle thee'd think it were nowt but a lullaby, and mi own father used to tell it hard, that grim it'd have thee not sleeping, and thee'll get bobbins versions off some others an' all, half-remembered and wrong in the details, and I've never minded the difference one bit, because it's still the same bend of water underneath whichever voice is carrying it.

What we do because of it, all of us, and nob'dy round here'll tell thee different if thee asks straight, is we dun't answer, not once, if us name gets called from somewhere dark, not till us has looked with us own two eyes and knows for certain who's doing the calling. Sounds daft written down plain like that. I'll tell thee summat else plain an' all, our kid, since it's only thee and me and the fire. I don't believe it, not properly, not in daylight, not most nights, and I've been made up with mesen more than once for saying so out loud, but I've never once broken that rule mesen, not once in mi whole life, and I in't about to start now. Ta-ra, that's yer lot, off to bed. Ta for sitting so still, an' all.
