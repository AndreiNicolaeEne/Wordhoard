Now then, our kid,

Right, before owt else. I'm sorry. I am. You'll have thought I'd fell off the face of the earth, and it's been the best part of a year now and me only just sat down proper to write it, brew gone cold beside me because I keep stopping to think how to say things. Our mam's been on at me about it near enough every Sunday, same as clockwork:

The mother: Have you written to our kid yet.
Me: Appen next week.
The mother: You said that last week.
Me: Aye, well, I mean it this week.

So give over thinking I've forgot you, chuck, because I haven't, not for a day.

Right then, the house. You'll remember the guttering round the back, the bit that were held on with nowt but hope and a length of wire our dad twisted round it that time it first went. Well it's done. Proper done, new brackets and all, though it took the fella three goes to get here, first time it were chucking it down and he weren't stood on a ladder in that for love nor money, second time he'd double-booked himself same as usual. But it's up now and it doesn't sound like somebody's dragging a chain across the roof every time it rains, which if you remember is what it were doing for years, and none of us thought owt of it because you get used to a noise.

Mind you, no sooner is that sorted than the fence at the side's gone. Not fallen down exactly, more leant over of an evening like it were tired, and now it just rests itself against the wall looking embarrassed about the whole business. That's next on the list, appen come spring if there's owt left in the pot by then.

We've put a rose in where the old shed used to stand, one of them that's meant to climb, though at the minute it's about as tall as a brew mug and looking none too made up about its situation. Give it a year or two, our mam says. She says that about most things these days.

Now the big news, and you'll want to sit down for this one, the eldest has moved on from the warehouse. After all them years, all that moaning about the shifts and the cold in there come winter, they've gone and got set on at the depot the other side of town, proper days, proper money, and a canteen that does a decent butty by all accounts. It come about because a fella they used to work alongside put a word in for them, out of the blue, hadn't spoke to him in donkeys, and one phone call later it were sorted. Everyone were made up for them. Our dad went round telling anyone who'd stand still long enough.

Don't tell it the pigeon story, mind.

Right, the street. You'll not believe who's gone, the ones at the end, the corner house, they upped and moved out back in the spring, and it's a young couple in now, keep themselves to themselves mostly, though he does wave if you catch his eye over the wall. And two doors down, the family's grown an' all, another little one arrived just after Whitsun, and there's prams and bikes and all sorts cluttering their ginnel now where before it were just the one bike propped against the bin.

Her at the corner shop still asks after you every single time I'm in. Goes summat like this:

The one at the counter: How's your kid getting on then, are they still over there?
Me: Aye, still there.
The one at the counter: And are they keeping well?
Me: Think so. You know more than me some days, the rate I hear from them.

So you'll have to fill me in and give me summat proper to tell her next time, save me stood there looking daft. And the fella off the allotments, the one who used to lend our dad the strimmer, asked after you an' all, said to tell you he's still got that packet of seeds you gave him and hasn't a clue what they are but he's planted them anyway out of stubbornness, says it's summat to look forward to not knowing.

Now, summat I'll write down because I'll never say it to your face. I do miss you something rotten, our kid. More than I let on when we're on the phone and I'm going on about nowt in particular just to fill the quiet. The house is quieter than it used to be, and I don't mean that as a complaint about the racket you used to make, though you did make a fair racket. I mean it proper. Right, said it now, don't go making a thing of it when you write back.

Summer's been decent to the place actually, better than owt we've had in years, dry for weeks on end, everyone's washing out on the line every day, the park gone that scorched brown it goes when it hasn't rained, and the old fella two streets over complaining it's too hot for his liking, which is his complaint whatever the weather's doing so nobody pays it much mind. Then it broke about a fortnight back, proper thunder, and the ginnel flooded same as it always does, and we were all stood at the back door watching it like it were entertainment laid on special.

Right, questions, and I want proper answers this time, none of your two-line ones. What's the scran like over there, is it owt to write home about or are you living on nowt but toast same as when you were here. Have you found somewhere decent for a brew yet, or is it all fancy stuff in daft little cups with no proper mug in sight. And are you keeping warm, because our mam worries, she'll not say it outright but she does, asks me every time have I heard from you, are you eating right, and I tell her aye though half the time I'm guessing.

I'll send the photos, our kid. I know I've said it every letter and every phone call for months and you're probably sick to the back teeth of hearing it, but I mean it this time. I've got them printed and everything, sat on the side by the door so I don't forget them again, and if I don't send them within the fortnight you've my full permission to mither me about it till I do.

Right, I'll give over now before this turns into a book. Look after yourself over there. Ta for putting up with me being such a bad letter-writer all this while, and ta-ra for now, chuck.
