Eleven days it went on for, no break in it at all, not one afternoon where the sun come out and give us owt back. First morning we knew it were reet bad, colder than owt they'd said on the forecast, and it never let up from there, not once.

Had th' stove going round the clock and the fire banked up of a night so it wouldn't go out on us. Kept telling our kid, put wood in th'ole, shut that door, we're not made of coal here. Someone up every couple of hour feeding it, taking it in turns, appen it'll ease off by morning we kept saying, and it never did, not once in eleven days.

Then the pipe out the back went. Never froze in all the years we'd been there, and this time it went solid and split clean through, and we'd no water out the tap for two days till it were sorted. Our kid up the ginnel with a bucket in each hand, borrowing off next door like summat off the telly.

The eldest: I've seen nowt like it, not since I were a girl. That winter everyone goes on about never held on this long.

Me: You reckon this one's beat it then.

The eldest: Beat it, aye. Beat the lot of 'em. Get thi coat on, chuck, tha'll catch thi death stood there.

Worst night were the seventh, or the eighth, hard to keep count by then, when the cold got right into the walls and the ground went tight outside like it were cracking apart. Lost the two hens that night, found them stiff in the morning and nowt to be done for it, and that hurt more than the pipe did.

By the fourth day owt were bobbins, nowt were belting except the fire, and our kid still off up the shop for barm cakes and scran because nobody were mithering with cooking proper by then, just brews going cold before you'd had two mouthfuls.

Eleven days it held, then it broke, just like that, dripping off every roof by dinnertime. Stood out the front in it like fools, made up, letting the wet soak right through us. Give over, someone said, laughing, and nobody did. Gradely, that's what it were. Ta.
