Right, I'll tell it how it went, because I've told it three times already since I got in and every time somebody says give over, that can't all be true, and it were, it all were, every last bit of it.

Clocked on for ten and straight off you could feel it were going to be one of them shifts, because we were two short before we'd even put an apron on. The one on proving had rung in poorly and the other lad, the young one, just never showed and never rang neither, so the gaffer's stood there at quarter past ten going, right, who's doing what, and we're already behind before a single tray's gone anywhere.

The mother, well she's not my mother, she's the older one on the ovens, everyone calls her that because she mithers you about your coat if it's chucking it down, she says to us, we'll manage, we always manage, and I thought aye, we will, because you do, don't you.

Half ten, eleven, nowt much doing, just the usual, mixing going, first batch proving, and it were quiet enough that our kid, who's on with me most nights, says to us on us break, this is dead easy tonight in't it, and I should've known not to say owt like that back because it's asking for it.

First sign summat were off were about half eleven. The big mixer started making a noise it doesn't normally make, a sort of grinding under the hum, not loud, just there if you were listening, and I said to the mother, that don't sound reet to me, and she says, appen it's nowt, leave it running, we've a full night ahead. So we left it running. Should've stopped it there and then, looking back, but you don't know that at half eleven, do you, you only know it after.

By half midnight it weren't nowt. It seized right up mid mix, dough half done, motor screaming, and then dead quiet, which is worse. That's the first proper failure of the night, and everything after followed on from that, one thing dragging the next behind it like a chain.

We tried to patch it ourselves first because you don't want to be stopping the whole line for one machine if you can help it, there's barm cakes need to be out for six and vans coming for half seven and you can't just down tools. So we're in there with our sleeves up trying to free the dough out of it, and our kid's on the phone to maintenance, and nowt, just ringing out, ringing out, twenty minutes of ringing out while the queue behind us backs up.

Me: Has tha got through yet.

Our kid: Not a dicky bird. It's just ringing.

Me: Try the gaffer's mobile and all.

In the end it were gone one before anybody answered owt, and it were the shift manager they had to pull out of his bed for it, and you could hear it in his voice down the phone before he even got there, proper narked at being called out for a machine at that hour.

Shift manager: This had better be summat and not nowt, because I were asleep.

He got there for half one looking like he'd dressed in the dark, which he had, and fair enough it were a right mess, dough gone hard in the workings, and he's under it with a torch going, who left this running when it were making a noise, and nobody answered that one straight off.

While he's under the mixer, that's when the second thing landed on top, because the proving room, which had been fine all this while, decided its heater were playing up and dropping, and if that goes too low the whole next batch is stood there instead of proving, so now we've got two things wanting fixing and three pairs of hands to do it with, us being two down from the off remember.

That's when our kid went over on the wet floor by the racks, rushing trays through with one hand and the door with the other, went down proper hard on the hip, and for a minute you think oh God, because it's a bad sound when somebody goes down like that. Nowt broken though, thank God, just bruised right through and shook up, but the mother made her sit down for ten minutes with a brew whether she wanted it or not, and would not hear a word otherwise.

Mother: Tha's sitting there till I say, love, I don't care what's proving.

With one down sat with an ice pack and the mixer still not right, the shift manager made the call to stop the second line altogether rather than run it half broken, said better half the order than a batch we can't stand behind, and that were the right call even though it hurt to hear it, watching a whole line go dark like that mid shift.

For a bit after that, maybe half three onward, it looked like it were turning. Mixer patched enough to limp on, proving room heater coaxed back up, our kid back on her feet insisting she were reet, made up with ourselves if I'm honest, thinking we'd got away with it, mother saying this is more like it now, gradely, we'll get there.

And then the delivery van for the flour top up, the one due at four for the early batch, never showed. Rang the depot and got told it'd broken down itself on the motorway, no flour till gone six if we were lucky, and that were the third thing, right when we thought we were through the worst of it, landing just as we'd let our shoulders down.

After that it were just grinding on. Counting up what we'd lost, and it kept climbing all night, first one full tray gone to the bin because it caught while everyone's attention were elsewhere, then a second batch that never proved right and come out flat as owt, bobbins the lot of it, then having to tell the early van driver half the usual order weren't there and watching his face at that. Every hour summat else got chalked onto the list of what were ruined or short, and you stop even reacting to it after a while, you just note it and keep going because there's nowt else for it.

Last couple of hours before light we were running on fumes and butties eaten standing up, nobody talking much, just put wood in th'ole and keep at it, the ovens going, the last trays through, everyone moving slower than they started but still moving. The mother didn't stop once the whole night, I'll say that for her, up and down like nothing had happened to her legs at all.

By the time the sky started going that grey colour over the yard, not even proper light yet, just less dark, we'd got the order out, most of it, and that were that. Shift manager off home to try and get back to sleep, none too pleased with his night either, our kid limping a bit but laughing about it by then, the mother locking up behind us same as always.

Walked out into it just stood there a minute, too tired for it to feel like owt yet, no relief, nowt, just cold air and legs that don't feel like they're mine. Ta-ra then, I said, see thee tonight, and our kid just nodded, because there weren't the energy left in either of us to say more than that.
