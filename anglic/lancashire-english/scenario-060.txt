Right, I'll tell thee the whole of it start to finish, because it were a proper long day and no mistake, and by the end of it none of us had owt left in us.

Nan's hip's been bad since back end of last year and she's not managed them stairs since, so she's settled now over at the home in Fulwood, made up as owt with her own little telly and her window onto the garden, and there were no sense in the house stood empty another winter with the pipes to worry over. It's sold now, contracts exchanged, completion end of the month, so it were down to us, the whole lot of us, to get it cleared in a day. Not just our lot either, the auntie and the uncle come over, the cousins, the eldest brought her husband to shift the heavy stuff, even the little one come along though she were more bother than help, bless her.

We were all stood in the ginnel at the side waiting on our kid to find the key.

The mother: Tha's had that key in thy bag since Tuesday, give over faffing and find it.

Our kid: I am finding it, mither me and it'll never turn up, will it.

It turned up. In she went first, and it were that quiet in there with nowt on the walls moved yet, just the smell of the place, which nobody says owt about but everybody notices, that particular smell that's only ever in a house that's been one family's house for fifty year.

Front room first, because that's where you start, isn't it. The auntie went straight to the sideboard and had the drawer open before her coat were even off, and there were the box of dominoes, the proper bone ones gone yellow, and she just stood there with it in her two hands.

The auntie: Eeh, Sunday afternoons. Your grandad'd have that lot out before the pots were even washed. He'd not let thee win an' all, not even when tha were six.

She were made up finding that, proper quiet a minute, and nobody rushed her.

Second thing that turned up in that same sideboard nobody could place at all: a little brass key, not much bigger than thy thumbnail, no label on it, fits nowt in the house that anyone could find, not the clock, not the tea caddy, not owt in the desk. We tried it in everything. The uncle reckoned it were off an old jewellery box long since gone, our kid reckoned it were a bike lock key from donkey's years back, nobody agreed, and in the end it went in a pocket to be forgot about, same as these things always are.

Sideboard itself caused a right to-do. The eldest wanted it, said nan had told her years back she could have it, and our kid said she'd been told the very same thing, and for a bit there were proper handbags about it in the front room, until the mother put a stop to it.

The mother: She told half of Preston they could have that sideboard, it were her way of being nice, it means nowt. Whoever's got room for it in their car today has it, and that's an end.

Turned out to be the eldest's husband had the estate car, so that were that, and nobody skriked over it for long.

Then there were the rocking chair, broke rocker on one side, been like it years, and the uncle wanted it skipping straight off.

The uncle: That's firewood, that is, it's not sat right in thirty year.

The eldest: Tha's not skipping that, that's her chair, she sat in that every night of her life.

Give over, said the uncle, but she wouldn't, and in the end it went in the eldest's car boot an' all, rocker and all, to be mended appen, or not, but kept regardless.

Kitchen were quicker, though behind the built-in cupboard, when the uncle pulled it out from the wall to get at the skirting, down fell an old biscuit tin nobody knew were there at all, and inside it a wodge of ration books and a photo of men in uniform none of us could name, and a few old pound notes gone soft as cloth. Nobody expected that, not in a month of Sundays. The mother went very quiet over that one and put it straight in her bag to look at proper later, at home, not stood in a cleared-out kitchen with everyone mithering her about it.

It were the box room took the whole blessed afternoon on its own, longer than every other room put together, I reckon. You've never seen the like. Carrier bags of carrier bags. Wrapping paper ironed flat and kept, every jar that had ever held owt, curtains from three houses back, a whole shelf of birthday cards going back donkeys. Our kid near enough gave up on it twice.

Our kid: There's no bottom to this room, I swear it's bigger on the inside.

Round one o'clock the mother put wood in th'ole, meaning shut the kitchen door to keep the heat in while the little one had gone to run about the yard letting the cold straight through, and called us all down for scran. Barm cakes she'd fetched in, proper ones mind, none of your rolls or your cobs, ham and a bit of tomato, and a big brew going round in whatever mugs weren't packed yet. We all sat on boxes in the kitchen eating and it were the first time all day anybody laughed properly, the uncle doing his usual daft turns, the little one getting butter on her nose.

After that it were the three-piece suite nobody in the end wanted, not one of us, not for love nor money, gradely as it once were in its day. Too big for any of our houses, pattern gone out of fashion forty year since, and every one of us stood round it a minute like it were owt more than a settee, before the uncle and the eldest's husband got it out the front door between them and it went straight on the house clearance van and that were that, no ceremony to it beyond a look passed between the mother and the auntie.

One thing went without a single word said against it: nan's sewing machine, the treadle one, straight to the auntie, because she's the only one of us ever sews owt, and nobody so much as blinked at it going her way. Reet, that's yours, said the mother, and that were the whole conversation.

Last room to stand empty were nan's own room, and it took no time at all once we were in it, being as it were mostly just the bed and the wardrobe and a chair, but stood in it after, bare mattress, dents in the carpet where the bed legs had sat thirty year, nobody said much. The auntie just stood in the doorway a minute.

The auntie: Well. That's that then.

Whatever were left after, the odds and sods nobody had claimed, went spread across the kitchen table and we all just took what we fancied, no fuss to it by then, everyone tired past arguing, the little one got the dominoes in the end, which felt reet somehow.

Last thing, the mother went round every room checking nowt were left, switched off at the mains, and stood in the hall a minute with the key in her hand before she locked it.

The mother: Ta-ra then, love.

Said it to the house and all, which none of us thought owt daft about, and out we went into the ginnel one after another, and that were the house done, and locked, and gone.
