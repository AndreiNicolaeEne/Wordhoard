The mother sat the two of them down at th' table, close in, hand on each, and waited till they were both looking at her proper before she said owt at all.

Then, quiet like: their grandad had died.

The eldest: What does that even mean though. Like where's he gone.

The mother: He's not gone anywhere as such, love. His body's stopped and his heart's stopped and he won't be waking up again. It's not like sleeping, it's not a story where he comes back round. He's dead, and we won't get to see him no more, and that's the hard bit of it, but I'll not tell thee owt different just to make it easier, because it wouldn't be true.

The younger: Will he still have his slippers on?

The mother: He will, chuck. They'll have looked after him nice and proper, he'll be comfortable, nowt to fret over there.

She pulled the pair of them in tight then, an arm round each.

The mother: Come here, chuck, come on, that's it.

The younger: Will we see him again though?

The mother: No, love. Not like we used, not popping round for a brew and a butty of a Sunday. We'll not see him again that way, and I'll not dodge it, because thee asked me straight and thee deserves it straight back. But we'll talk about him plenty, and that keeps him close an' all.

Before she let them go back to their scran she told them plain: there'd be folk in and out the house next few days, aunties and uncles down the ginnel and in the kitchen, appen a bit of crying and no shame in it, and then a day they'd all go together and say a proper goodbye. Nowt to come at them sideways later.

The mother: Reet. Off you go now. Ta, both of you, for asking me straight.
