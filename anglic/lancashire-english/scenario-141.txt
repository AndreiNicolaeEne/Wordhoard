Me: Sit down, all on you. Go on, our kid, put wood in th' ole, there's a right draught coming under that door and we'll be here a bit.

Right. I've got everybody here today for summat, and I'm not mithering on wi' small talk first neither, though there's a brew made if anybody wants one, and scran after if you can still eat when I've said what I've got to say. I've kept this back thirty-odd year. Longer, if I'm honest wi' misen. And I'm done keeping it. It's time it were said, plain, so I'll say it plain.

You all remember th' chip shop. Course you do. Nobody in this family's ever not remembered it, it were spoke of that much, one road or another, every Sunday dinner since. What happened that night, and what it cost us, and who copped for it. I were there. I know what it did to this family, the row that blew up after, and I know it never really went quiet, not proper, it just went underground, and it followed our kid about ever since like a smell that won't wash out of a coat.

Everybody sat quiet now, nobody taking their turn, just looking. Good. Let me get through it.

That night, when th' fryer went up and near took the whole place with it, and we lost thousands we didn't have, and the insurance men came round asking questions about who were meant to be watching what, this family needed somebody to answer for it. And our kid answered for it. Got blamed there and then, and never stopped being blamed since, not really, not underneath. I've heard it said in this very room, more than once, dressed up as a joke, "aye, don't let him near a fryer," and every time it were said our kid laughed along same as anybody, and I sat there knowing.

Mother: Knowing what.

Me: Let me finish, Mam. Please.

It weren't our kid. It never were. I'm saying that plain and I'm not softening it: our kid never left that fryer, never touched it that night. It were the auntie.

A stunned quiet fell over the room, the kind you could nearly hear, and I made myself keep looking at them while it sat there.

Me: I know how that sounds, coming out of nowhere after all this time, but I'm not making it up and I'm not confused about it. I were stood in th' back doing the washing up when it happened. I watched the auntie go out down th' ginnel for a fag while that pan were still going, and I watched her come back in when it were already catching, and I watched her not say a word when everybody started shouting for our kid.

Mother: No. No, tha's got that wrong, love, tha must have. That's not, she'd never have let him carry that.

Me: I'm certain, Mam. I were there. I saw it with my own eyes, not once, twice over in my head every year since, and I'm certain.

Mother: But thirty year...

Me: I know. I know what thirty year is. Let me get to that an' all.

I could see summat rising in the auntie already, before she'd even opened her mouth, that old look she gets when she thinks she's about to be had a go at again, the one that says here we go, blame the one who's allus been the family's bad penny. So I said it before she could.

Me: And before anybody starts, I'm not saying this to have a go at thee. I'm not dragging this out to settle owt. I've sat on it this long because I didn't want to be the one causing more damage, not because I've been waiting to land it on thee in front of everybody. I want that understood before another word's said.

I looked then at our kid, because that were the face I most needed to see, and it were complicated, the way I'd always feared it would be if this day ever came. There were relief in it, real relief, the kind that loosens somebody's shoulders you didn't even realise had been up round their ears for thirty year. But there were anger under it and after it too, fresh as owt, because relief don't undo the years, it just shows you how heavy they were.

Our kid: Thirty year. Thirty year I've had that follow me. Every job I lost the nerve to try for, every time our mam looked at me sideways in that kitchen, every barm cake I made with my hands shaking because I thought I'd burn the place down again, thirty year, and it were never even me.

Mother: Love...

Our kid: No, let me. I'm made up it's out, I am, I'm not saying I'm not. But I'm reet angry an' all, and I don't know which one to feel first.

Me: I know. I know you've every right to both.

The auntie hadn't said a word yet. I watched her go white, proper white, the colour just dropping out of her, and she didn't deny it, not one word, and that not-denying were its own confession sat right there in the room. When she did speak it come out thin, nowt like her usual self.

The auntie: I were only gone a minute. I only went for one fag. I never thought...

Mother: A minute. A minute, and you let him carry it thirty year, you let my own lad...

The auntie: I didn't know what to say, after. Everybody were shouting, and it were already his name in their mouths, and I...

Mother: You said nowt. You stood there and said nowt while this family tore itself apart over him and you said nowt.

And there it were, the old anger, same anger from that very night, still fully alive after all these years, only now it were pointed the right road at last, straight at the one who'd earned it, and it come out of Mam like it had been folded away in a drawer this whole time waiting for the right hand to open it.

Mother: All them years I couldn't look at him proper, all them years I let mysen think he were careless, feckless, that he'd cost us everything through his own daftness, and it were you, sat at my table every Christmas, letting me think it...

The auntie: I know. I know what I did.

Me: And I've to answer for my part in it an' all. I should've said summat that first year, when it were still fresh, before it set hard into who everybody thought our kid were. I've thought about telling this so many times I've lost count, but there were one time in particular, at our kid's wedding of all places, somebody made that same tired joke about the fryer and I had it right on the end of my tongue, I'd had a few and it were nearly out, and then I looked at our kid laughing along same as ever and I give over, I couldn't do it, not that day. I told mysen I were protecting him from more upset on his own day. I told mysen a lot of things over the years to make the not-telling feel like summat other than cowardice. I'm not going to stand here now and pretend it were owt gradely of me. It weren't. It were me being scared, plain and simple, scared of you two never speaking again, scared of being the one who broke this family open a second time. And I let our kid carry it instead. I'm sorry. Not as an excuse. As summat I owe, and it's overdue.

Nobody said owt for a bit after that. Just sat with it, the tea gone cold in front of most of us, untouched.

Me: I'm not stood here saying this fixes owt. It doesn't. Our kid's still lost them years, and no amount of saying sorry now buys them back. This family's not going to walk out of this room tidy, all forgiven and forgotten, I know that. But we're standing on what actually happened now, finally, instead of stood round it, pretending, same as we've been doing this whole time. That's not nowt. It's not everything either. But it's true, and true's more than we've had in this house in thirty year, and I'd rather have that, even like this, even today, than go on the other road any longer.

Our kid looked at the auntie a long while, and the auntie couldn't hold his eye, and Mam had gone quiet in that way that meant the shouting weren't done, only paused. Nobody moved to put the kettle back on. Nobody said ta-ra either, because nobody were going anywhere yet, not for a good while.
