Come on then, chavo, mind that step, it's up on one corner and always has been, nobody's ever got round to it. Right, this is it. This is the door.

You'd come up the path and the first thing was always the smell off the step, whoever'd been round with the mop, and the door itself, that heavy old thing that stuck in the frame come winter so you had to give it a shoulder. Coming in always felt like coming in out of something, even on a kushti summer's day, like the whole world outside was noise and this was where it went quiet. You'd hear the telly going low, or the kettle, before you even saw a soul, and that was the feeling of it, that you were back, that whatever you'd been rokkering about out there with the gorjas at school or down the yard didn't matter no more the second that door went.

Straight through was the front room and that was where everything happened that mattered. Nan's chair by the fire, always. The rest of us moved about, sat here, sat there, but that chair was hers the way the sky's the sky, nobody questioned it, not even the littlest of the chavvies would go near it when she was up making tea. Christmas, birthdays, a wake, an argument, the football on with half the street's men crammed in of an evening, it all went off in that room. There was a settee that took three grown men and swallowed a fourth, and a mantel with more photos on it than there was family left to put names to half of them. That's where the rokkering happened proper, the real talk, the stories about the road, about who'd had a result at the fair, who'd had words with a gavver over nothing, who'd shod what horse for who. You could sit in the corner as a chavo and just take it in, half of it you didn't understand and the other half you'd repeat wrong at school and get a look for it.

Kitchen was through the back and it smelled of fried bread most mornings of my life, that and strong tea stewed near black. That was Nan's habit, first thing, kettle on before her coat was even off some days, and you knew the day had started when you heard that gas ring catch. Whoever came through that door, first thing said to them was sit down, you'll have a cup, and there was no arguing it, mush or mort, family or not, you sat and you had one.

Sleeping was three to a room some years, the boys top and bottom of one, the girl of the family in with Nan when Granddad was away, and whoever was staying, aunt or cousin passing through for a week that turned into a season, they went on the floor of the front room on a mattress that came out each night and went back behind the settee each morning like it had never been there.

The good room, the back bedroom, that was always spoke of as Granddad's, even them years he wasn't there half the time, off with the grai to one fair or another. Nobody else's things went in there. It had the one good wardrobe in the house and inside it, hanging, his good coat for weddings and funerals, and that room kept his smell in it long after he'd have said different himself.

My own spot was out past the kitchen, a lean-to bit that had been meant for coal and never was no more, and I'd got a crate and an old bit of carpet in there and called it mine, and nobody argued that either, not even the dog, though the dog tried.

Out the back was a yard, more concrete than garden, and that was where the real living got done half the year, where the men would be over an engine or over a bit of harness, where washing went up on a line strung corner to corner, where a fire'd go in a drum some evenings and you'd sit round it till you were sent in.

The neighbours either side, gorja families both, and decent enough, you'd hear them through the wall plain as anything, their radio, their rows, their baby crying half the night some weeks. One of them kept chickens and you'd hear them starting up before it was even light.

Same time every morning you'd get the milk float, that whine and rattle of it coming up the road, and not long after, old boy two doors down leading his horse out to graze the verge, same hour near enough every divvus of your life, rain or shine, and you'd watch him go by the front window with your hobben half in your mouth.

The back gate was always off one hinge, always, my whole life that gate was being fixed and never stayed fixed, and it was a running joke, somebody'd say we'll get that gate sorted and everyone would just about fall about, because it never was, not once.

Summer the door stood open all day, the street full of chavvies out till it went properly dark, marbles up the pavement, football against somebody's wall till a gorja came out shouting, and the older ones out on the step of an evening with a box going, somebody's uncle with a squeezebox more often than not. Winter it drew right in, the fire never let go out, coats stayed on indoors half the time, and the road went quiet and dark by four and you felt the cold coming up through that concrete yard something rotten.

There was a night I still see plain as anything, everyone out in that yard round the drum fire, Nan telling one of her long ones about the road before any of us was born, half the street's mush and mort both in and out the gate all evening, and one of the little ones asleep on somebody's lap, and I remember thinking even then, young as I was, hold onto this one, and I have.

House still stands, far as I know, though it's had that much done to it by them that bought it off us, new front, new everything, you'd not know it now walking past, and the yard's got a proper garden laid where the drum fire went, decking and all sorts. Street's still there. Half the families gone though, scattered to wherever work or the road took them, and them that's left, you'd hardly know to nod to now.

Anyway, that's your lot, chavo. That's where I come from, room by room, same as I've just walked you through it. Kettle's on if you want a cup before you jal off home.
