You know what it was, right, it was that big ash what come down across the top of the lane in the storm, the one what leant over the ditch for years and everybody said one day she'll go and she went, right across the only way in and out the tan. And that en't a job for one household, not a tree that size, not with the roots half out the bank and all. My old man was the one clocked it first, gone out at first light to see to the grai and come back up the row bangin on the trailer doors, shoutin there's a tree down and we're shut in, who's about. And I'm tellin you, divvus hadn't hardly started and word was up and down that row faster than owt I seen, door to door to door, somebody's kid runnin barefoot to holler at the far end before the kettle was even on.

We reckoned maybe eight, ten of us'd turn to. Come dinner time there was near on twenty-five stood round that tree, mushes and morts and all, more than anybody called for, more than anybody had hobben laid on for and that sorted itself out quick enough as you'll hear. Everybody just fell into a bit, nobody stood about waitin to be told, that's not how it goes. My cousin's fella, big lump of a mush, he took the heaviest end of it, up on the trunk with the saw most the divvus, arms like hams by the finish, everybody give him a wide dick when he was goin at it case a branch sprung back on him. Rest of us was on the brash, draggin it clear, stackin it, the young lads haulin rope round to the far side so the grai could pull the big limbs off once they was cut free.

Dick this though. About midday the whole thing near went wrong on us. They'd got the main trunk near cut through and somebody'd chained the grai to drag the top half clear, and the chain weren't set right, come loose sudden and that log rolled a good way down the bank quicker than anybody thought, mud flyin, everybody scarperin out the way, and my old man's boot got caught under the edge of it for a second, half a second, before he had it out again. No harm done in the end, thank God, but there was some shoutin over that, I don't mind sayin. My uncle reckoned they should've chained it lower, nearer the root end, and the fella with the saw reckoned he was talkin like a dinlo and knew nothing about it, and for a good ten minutes them two was at it proper, rokkerin away over who done what wrong, and the rest of us just kept on with the brash and let 'em go at it, cause that's family for you, blows over quick enough. And it did. Somebody put the kettle back in his hand and that was the end of it.

By early afternoon there was hobben comin out proper, my nan brought a great pan of stewed meat out and set it on a crate, and further up the row another household had bread and a big tin of tea goin round all divvus, cups passed hand to hand, nobody keepin count whose was whose. The chavvies had their own job an all, my sister's lot had 'em gatherin the little sticks and kindlin off the brash pile, stackin it proper neat in a corner so it wouldn't go to waste come winter, and they took to it like it was the best game goin, proud as owt every time they filled a barrow.

My old gran, she's the oldest on that whole tan, she weren't havin none of sittin in the trailer out the way either. She come out with her stick and stood over the fire we'd got goin for the tea and said she'd mind that and nobody was to move her off it, and mind it she did, all divvus, wouldn't let a soul else near the kettle. Somebody tried to take it off her hand once and she gave him such a look he never tried it again.

Funniest bit though, and everybody was creased up over this one, the juk, my brother's juk, he got himself tangled right up in the loose end of that rope while everybody's back was turned, and he's runnin round in circles yelpin like he's been chored by the devil himself, draggin half the coil after him, and he runs straight through the middle of where the tea was set out and knocks the whole lot flyin, cups everywhere, and my nan's shoutin at him and he's still runnin, and by the time somebody caught hold of him the whole row was doubled over, even them two that had been arguin over the chain a couple hours before, laughin fit to bust. Set the tone proper different after that, whole afternoon felt lighter.

Thought we'd be done by mid-afternoon, tell you the truth, thought a few hours and the lane'd be clear and that'd be that. Come out a good three hour past what anybody reckoned, sun near gone by the time the last of it was hauled off the bank. But by the finish we'd got the whole trunk cut and cleared, all the brash stacked for burnin and kindlin both, the root end dug out the bank so it wouldn't just come down again next blow, and the ruts in the lane packed with what hardcore we had lyin about so a lorry could get through without sinkin to the axle. Somebody let a cheer go up when the last bit was off the road, and then it was everybody, the whole row of us, hollerin and clappin like we'd won summat, and in a way I suppose we had.

That evening the whole tan felt different, I couldn't tell you exact how, just different, everybody's fires lit close together, folk sat out longer than usual, more rokkerin back and forth between trailers than there'd been in weeks, like the whole street had remembered it was one thing and not just a row of separate doors. Didn't last, mind, few days and everybody's back in their own business same as ever. But that divvus, sat out with the fire going and the lane clear behind us, it was a good one. Kushti divvus, that.
