Right, mush, I'll give you the whole of it start to finish, because you never saw a second of it and it still gets brought up every time we're all sat round together, so you're owed the full tale proper, not just the bit people shout out over each other.

It started for nothing. Me and my cousin was sat on the step of the vardo of a evening, cards going between us, just three-card brag for nothing at first, for the good of it, because there weren't nothing else doing and half the atchin tan had come and stood round watching same as they always do when cards come out. Somebody's chavvies was chasing about behind us, a juk barking at nothing like they do, and it was just a laugh, that's all it was meant to be.

Then my cousin says, half joking:

Cousin: Go on then, put summat on it if you're that kushti at it.

And I says fine, a fiver, why not, and that should have been the end of the deciding but the ones stood round wouldn't leave it be. That's how it always goes. Nobody stood watching a card game wants it to stay small.

The uncle: A fiver's nothing, that's not even a bet, that's pocket money.

The eldest: Make it a proper sum or don't bother laying it at all.

So it went up and up between the pair of them arguing it more than we was, tenner, fifteen, till somebody's aunt settled it flat out, twenty, and that was locked in before either me or my cousin had even said aye to it ourselves. Twenty quid, small enough it wouldn't sting bad whoever lost it, agreed and done before a single card had turned. Nobody had even said yet what happened if you lost, mind, that bit got skipped clean over in all the shouting, everyone just wanted the number settled.

So we played it. And it went back and forth a good while, proper back and forth, I had a good hand then he had a good hand, folk stood round groaning and laughing depending whose side they favoured, somebody's mort leaning over her chavo's head trying to dick my cards without me clocking her. Nip and tuck the whole way, neither one of us clear ahead, till it turned. And when it turned it turned hard, all at once, one bad card and I could see it going before the hand was even out, before it was even finished I knew, sat there watching my cousin's face go from worried to certain in about two seconds.

When it was done, well and truly done, I just groaned right out loud and put my hands over my face and said it plain, no good pretending otherwise in front of that many people watching.

Me: Alright, alright, you got me, fair's fair.

And that should have been that, twenty out my pocket, everyone has a laugh and it's forgotten by next divvus. Only it never happened that way at all.

Because before I could even get my hand near my pocket, the eldest stands up and says no, hold up, we're not having money for this one, and the whole crowd sort of turned on it together like they'd all been thinking the same thing and just waiting for somebody to say it first.

The eldest: Twenty quid's nothing, that's gone in a divvus and forgot. We want summat we can rokker about after.

And then they went and decided it there and then, the whole lot of them talking over one another, what exactly it had to be, and they didn't leave a single bit of it vague either, they named it out proper, every part of it, so there'd be no wriggling later. I won't say what it was yet, I'll come to it, but I heard it laid out and my stomach went right down through the floor of the vardo.

I tried it on once, mind, tried to buy my way out plain and simple.

Me: I'll just give you the twenty, straight up, and we'll call it done, eh?

Laughing at me before I'd even finished the sentence.

The uncle: Not a chance, dinlo, you agreed it, you're doing it, twenty quid don't come near it now.

No way out of it, and everyone laughing so hard at the state of my face they had to set a divvus for it there and then before I could think up another way to talk myself clear. Saturday, they said, up by where the horses stand, in front of whoever was about, and no getting out of it on the day either.

So Saturday come, and I'll tell you, they turned it into a proper occasion, chairs dragged round, somebody's aunt with her arms folded like she'd paid to get in. What they'd named was this: I had to get down proper on one knee in front of the whole crowd and serenade the ugliest, oldest juk on the whole site, sing to it, a proper love song, none of your mumbling either, full voice, and finish it off asking it to marry me, down on one knee, hand out and all, in front of everyone gathered to watch. And somebody had gone and dug out the aunt's old headscarf for me to wear over it, just to finish me off good and proper.

So there I am, down on my knee in the mud, this old bandy-legged juk looking at me like I'd lost the run of myself entirely, and I start into it, singing away, dying inside but singing it out loud all the same because there weren't no way through it but through it. Whole crowd near falling off their chairs already just watching me get down there. And I get right to the end, get to the actual asking bit, hand held out to the juk, dead serious face on me, and I says it, will you marry me, and the whole atchin tan's gone dead quiet waiting on it.

And that juk, I swear to you, right on the very word, throws its head back and lets out this long mournful howl, right at me, like it was giving me its proper answer and the answer was no. Didn't even look at me while it done it. Just sat there howling at the sky like I'd broke its heart asking. The whole crowd went up like nothing you ever heard, somebody's uncle nearly went off his chair backwards, the chavvies was rolling about in the grass, even the mort who'd been watching all stern-faced was bent double. That howl, right on cue like the juk had been rehearsing it, that's the bit nobody's ever let go of.

And that's the thing, see, that's outlived the twenty quid ten times over. Nobody remembers now who even had the better cards that night. But get the family together any divvus you like, round a fire, round a table, don't matter, someone only has to start with "d'you remember when he asked that juk to marry him" and off it goes again, and every single time it's told the story's grown a bit more theater on it than it had the time before, till by now you'd think I sang three verses and it howled the whole thing back at me in tune.
