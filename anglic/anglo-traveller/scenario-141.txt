I got everyone sat down of a divvus when the fair was quiet and no one had nowhere else to be, told the chavvies to jal on out and play though the two eldest of them lingered by the door pretending not to listen, and before I'd said one word I could see it on the lot of them, that grown stillness that comes over a family when they know summat's coming. No one took a turn of their own. They just sat and waited on us.

Me: Right. There's summat I've carried a long while now, years and years, and I'm putting it down tonight, so hear us out the whole way before anyone says owt.

I let that sit a minute. Then I took them back to it, the way you'd have to, because half the young ones in that room weren't even walking yet when it happened. The grai, our good grai, the one the whole family's living half hung off back then, got out of the atchin tan of a night and was killed dead on the road before light. And the family near broke over it, not just the money of it, though God knows there was no wonga to speak of that winter, but the loss of the thing itself, the shame of a family that couldn't keep its own grai safe.

Me: And you all remember whose door that got laid at. It got laid at the uncle's door, and it stopped there ever since. Fifteen years of "him and his drink," fifteen years of "you can't trust him with owt," and it followed him about like a smell he couldn't wash off, into every row this family's ever had since, whatever it was actually about.

The mother's face had already gone tight, because she knew where this had to be going, though she couldn't have said how yet.

Me: It wasn't him. It was never him. I've known the whole time who left that gate swinging, and it's time I said the name out loud in this room. It was the eldest.

There was a stillness then you could've cut with a knife. Nobody rokkered a word.

Me: I seen it meself. I was a chavo, up in the night for nothing much, and I dicked out the vardo window and there was the eldest slipping through that gate, off to see a mort three atchin tans over, and never so much as turned round to shut it behind. I heard the grai go a good half hour after. I've had that sound in us ever since.

The mother: You're having us on.

Me: I'm not having anyone on. I'm dead certain. I was there, I seen it with me own eyes, and there's no mistaking what I seen.

My sister had gone very still and very red, the old grievance climbing up in her plain as day, all them years she'd sided hard with the mother against the uncle, called him dinlo to his face more than once, and I could see it rising in her before she'd even opened her mouth to let it out.

Me: And before anyone starts on why I never said, I'll tell you why. Because the anger over that grai near split this family down the middle the first time round, and it took years to go quiet, and I was afraid of what saying it would wake back up. And because the eldest begged us, that once, not to say, and I kept that confidence a lot longer than I'd any right to. I told meself I was protecting somebody. Half the time I think I was only protecting meself from being the one who set it all off again.

I looked round at them all.

Me: There was a Christmas, years back now, when it nearly come out of us. We were all sat same as this, and the uncle made some joke against himself about the grai, laughing at his own expense the way he'd learned to, and I nearly stood up right there and told the lot of you. I got as far as opening me mouth. Then I looked at his face, laughing along with a joke that wasn't even his to carry, and I lost me nerve, and I sat back down, and I've regretted it going on ten years now.

Me: It's took summat out of us, carrying it. Watching him get called dinlo at every gathering, watching him half believe it of himself in the end, when all he ever did wrong that night was be asleep and easy to blame. That's a heavy hobben to sit with, watching a man eat his own bad name at every table this family's ever set.

The uncle hadn't said a word the whole time, just sat with his jaw working. When he did speak it come out rough.

The uncle: You're certain. Say it again so I know I heard you right.

Me: I'm certain. It wasn't you. It was never you.

Something went through him then that I couldn't put one name to, some of the weight coming off his shoulders right there in front of us, you could nearly see it lift, but hard on the back of it come a fresh anger, worse than relief in the end, at every year he'd spent carrying a name that was never rightly his, every gavver joke, every "there he goes again," fifteen years of a reputation he'd done nothing to earn.

The uncle: Fifteen year. Fifteen year I've ate that.

The eldest had gone white and then gone still, the way a person goes when there's nowhere left to stand. No arguing it, no denying it, just sat there with the truth landed square on their chest at last, after carrying it alone even longer than I had.

The eldest: I was going to say it meself one day. I kept telling meself that. One day.

My sister found her voice then, and it come out shaking, all that old grievance she'd carried on the uncle's account now with nowhere honest left to go but where it belonged.

My sister: All them years I called you dinlo to your face. All them years.

The uncle: Aye. You did.

My sister: And you knew the whole time, didn't you. All them years you let him carry it.

The eldest didn't answer that one, just sat there and took it, and I reckon that was answer enough.

Me: I'm sorry an' all. Not making excuses for meself, saying it plain: I should've said it long since, and I let it run on years past where it should've stopped, and that's mine to answer for same as anyone's is theirs.

Nobody rushed to say it was fine, because it wasn't fine, not yet, maybe not for a long while. The old anger was up and moving round that room again, only pointed the right way at last, at the one who'd earned it instead of the one who never had. There'll be more said before this settles, I know that much, there's rows still owed and years still owed, the uncle's got every right to them and more besides.

But we're not stood round the truth no more, talking about it sideways, letting a good man carry another's shame like it was his own to wear. We're stood on it now, plain and hard as it is, and however long it takes this family to find its feet on that ground, at least it's the actual ground we're stood on, at long, long last.
