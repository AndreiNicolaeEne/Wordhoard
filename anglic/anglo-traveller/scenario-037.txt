Up before the light again, same as every divvus of the picking. We'd been on that atchin tan the best part of a week already, and Da had the fire going before any of us properly stirred. He was the one give the first job out, quiet, not even proper awake himself yet.

Da: Get the kettle on and get the little ones up, we're wanted down the field for six.

The youngest was up first anyway, always is, too excited to sleep right the whole week the picking's on. Da give him the easiest of it, carrying the empty baskets down and setting them out along the rows, nothing heavy in it, just legs young enough to run it twice over before the rest of us even had our tea drunk.

Da: Go on then, chavo, jal on and get them baskets down, one every few yards, don't go setting them all in one place like you did last divvus.

He was off like a juk let off the lead, glad of a job that felt like a proper job even if it weren't much of one.

The heaviest of it fell to my uncle same as it always does, being the biggest and strongest of the lot of us, and nobody argued it, it just was how it went. He'd be the one hauling the full sacks up onto the trailer once they were weighed, one under each arm like they weighed nothing at all, though you could see it in the way he stood by dinner time every year.

Uncle: Load us up then, don't stand there dicking about, this lot won't carry itself up there.

We started down the first section while the light was still grey and cold enough to see your breath, my aunt working the row next to mine, a hard grafting mort if ever there was one, and for a good while there weren't much talk at all, just the work, hands going, baskets filling, the odd cough down the line. Once that section was cleared we all shifted along together to the next bit, the whole gang of us moving like one thing, baskets and all, nobody needing to be told where to go.

My cousin was falling behind before the sun was even proper up, not from want of trying, just her hands weren't quick as the rest of us that morning, stiff with the cold. Nobody said a word about it, no telling off nor nothing, my mother just come round quiet and worked the end of her row alongside her a while till she caught up, and that were that, no fuss made of it.

Mother: You alright there, love?

My cousin: Just me fingers is cold is all.

Mother: Don't mind it, we'll get this bit between us.

Come mid-morning the hobben was brought out, bread and a bit of bacon and the tea in the big can, and everybody stopped where they stood and sat down on turned-over baskets, glad of the sit-down near as much as the food. That's when the talk started up proper, and the joking with it.

Uncle: Look at the state of your rows, mush, picking slower than my old juk this morning.

Da: Slower than your juk and still got more done than you, so hold your rokker.

The eldest: He's right though, you have been dragging it a bit.

Uncle: Dragging it, I've done twice what any of you dinlos have done, don't you come it.

It went on like that a good while, back and forth, nobody meaning nothing by it, just passing the tea round and passing the time, the chavvies laughing at the grown ones carrying on same as they always did.

Weren't long after we was back at it that the sky started turning, gone that flat grey that means it's coming, and Da was up dicking the state of it before anyone else even noticed.

Da: That's coming in fast. We want this next bit under cover before it's on us. Leave the far section, jal on over onto what's nearest the trailer first.

So the whole plan changed there and then, everybody shifting over to the rows closest in so whatever got picked could get covered quick if the rain come down proper. For a spell it looked like it might blow over anyway, the sky lightening a touch, the wind dropping off, so we pushed on through the afternoon glad of it holding, working the harder for knowing we'd got lucky with it.

Then the trouble come. One of the old baskets give way clean at the bottom right when it was near full, the whole lot of it spilling back down between the rows, half of it spoiled in the dirt.

The eldest: Ah now look at that, there's a basket's worth of wonga gone for nothing.

Uncle: Don't stand there looking at it, mush, get it picked back up. What's clean can go back in, the rest we'll leave to the birds.

We all come over and got down and sorted through it quick, salvaging what could still go in and leaving what couldn't, and it cost us a bit of time but not so much once everybody had a hand in it. Da tied the busted basket up best he could with a length of string so it'd see out the day at least.

There was a bit of a fright besides when a van come rattling down the track toward us, and the youngest called out thinking it were a gavver come to move us on, but it were only the gorja off the farm come round early to see how we were getting on with it, and he went off again happy enough once Da had a word with him.

By the time the light started going gold and low the word went round quick to pick up the pace, everybody feeling it without needing telling twice, hands moving faster, less talk now, just the work of it, racing the dark same as every divvus like this ends.

Da: Come on now, quick as you can, we want this lot in before it's too dark to dick what we're picking.

That last section we all come together on, same as always, everybody drifting in off their own rows to finish the one bit together, chavvies and all, even the youngest give a proper basket to carry this time, tired as he was, wanting to be part of the finish same as the rest of us.

When it was all weighed and tallied at the end Da read it out and there was a bit of pride in his voice with it, more brought in than the divvus before, and my uncle nodded like he'd known it'd be that all along.

Da: That's a kushti tally, that is. Best we've had all week, and good wonga in it besides.

Uncle: Told you we'd get through it.

And then it was like the whole lot of us come apart at once, everybody sitting down wherever they stood, backs against the trailer, against each other, nobody saying much, just glad of the stopping, the light nearly gone now and the cold coming up off the ground.

But before anybody was let sit too long there was still the one last little job wanted doing, and it fell to my cousin, being nearest and not too wore through to manage it.

Mother: Go and see to the grai before you sit yourself down proper, love, get him watered and seen to while there's still light enough to see by.

My cousin: Go on then.

She went off grumbling a bit, not really minding it, and the rest of us sat there in the near dark, worn right through, waiting on the fire and the tea and whatever was left of the hobben, glad the divvus was done at last.
