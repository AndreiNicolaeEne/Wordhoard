We'd been six of us packed into that little house going on eight year, and it weren't never meant to hold more than four. Started out just me and the wife and two chavvies, and by the time the third one come along and then the fourth hard on his heels, there weren't room to swing a juk in it of a morning, everybody tripping over everybody else to get out the door. The plot at the back was ours an all, same bit of ground granddad used to pull the vardo onto of a winter, back when that whole stretch down by the lane was atchin tan and nobody minded who stopped there. Kept a grai on it still, at the far end, so there was land enough to build if we could just get on and do it. My mother used to say to me:

Mother: You can't keep four growing chavvies in two rooms, mush, they'll be climbing the walls before they're climbing trees.

She weren't wrong. So we sat down one evening, me and the wife and my brother-in-law who's handy with block work, and worked it out proper. Reckoned it'd take the two of us and maybe one more if we could get him, four months, five at the outside, working weekends and whatever evenings we could manage after work. Well. Eleven months later and six pairs of hands gone through that job, I can tell you now we never reckoned right on a single thing.

First fortnight in, we was up early, footings dug, feeling kushti about ourselves, thinking we'd got it sorted. Set the whole first run of block out on what we thought was the line off the old wall, only my brother-in-law come back from measuring the far end and his face had gone. We'd took the line off the wrong corner of the house, whole thing was out by near enough a foot along the length, which don't sound like much till you're stood there looking at a wall that won't square with nothing else you're going to build. Had to bring the whole lot back down, every block, and start the setting out again from scratch. That was near two weeks' work undone in an afternoon, and nobody spoke much that evening.

Mother-in-law: Well, better you found it now than when the roof's on it, mush.

Kushti sort of comfort, that, but she weren't wrong either.

Then there was the divvus the extra hands showed up and nobody had asked them. We was round the back mixing muck by hand, me, the wife's brother and my own brother, when up the path comes my cousin and his boy and a mush from two doors down we barely knew to rokker to, all of them just turned up with their own tools like it was nothing.

Cousin: Heard you was struggling on with just the three of you. Thought we'd come and give it a shove.

Never asked, never rung ahead, just come. That's how it goes though, among your own, somebody hears you're at it and the word gets round and next thing there's more mushes than you've got jobs for, and you're glad of every one, and the wife out with hobben for the lot of them at dinner time whether she'd planned for six extra or not.

Weather had other ideas an all. Must have been week six, seven, when it come down and didn't let up, near a week of it, rain that turned the whole plot to soup, couldn't get a barrow across it let alone mix a bag of sand and cement without it going to porridge. We stood under the tarp of a morning looking out at it and my brother said:

Brother: No point standing here getting wet for nothing, mush, might as well jal home and come back when it's fit.

So home we went, day after day, losing the best part of a week just watching it come down, chavvies going spare stuck in the house with us under their feet.

Ran short of block halfway through an all. The gorja at the merchant's hadn't got what we needed in and wouldn't have none till the following week, and we weren't waiting on that, not with the job half up and open to the weather, and it had already cost more wonga than we'd bargained on chasing what we could round three different yards. So we made do, used what timber and block my brother-in-law had left over from another job, lying in his yard going spare, mixed sizes and all, had to pack it out and level it up as we went. Looks a bit rough if you know where to look but it's held solid, never moved an inch since.

Then there was the fall. Up on the scaffold boards putting the wall plate on, must have been getting on eight, nine month in, and one of the boards give way under me, not proper fixed down, and down I went, near ten foot of it, landed on the muck heap thank God and not the concrete, knocked the wind clean out of me and give everybody the fright of their lives, but nothing broke, nothing worse than a few days walking like an old man and my brother-in-law wouldn't let me back up a ladder without he'd checked every board himself after that.

Wife: You want to be more careful, you dinlo, you've got chavvies down here need their father in one piece.

Fair play to her.

Biggest row of the whole job come over the roof, how the rafters was to sit, my brother wanting one way, my brother-in-law dead set on another, the two of them stood there of an evening rokkering at each other loud enough the neighbours must have heard every word.

Brother: I'm telling you, done it this way twenty times, it's kushti, it holds.

Brother-in-law: And I'm telling you it ain't right for this span, you'll have it sagging in five year.

Went on the best part of two hours that did, till the wife come out and told the pair of them to give over or jal home, and in the end we went with my brother-in-law's way, and he's never once let anybody forget he was right about it.

Worst of it though was the side wall. Had it up, pointed, looking finished, and then somebody noticed it weren't plumb, not by a little either, leaning out enough you could see it from the road if you knew to look. No way round it, had to bring the whole thing down, every block, back to the footing, and build it again proper this time with a mush checking the level every third course so it never happened twice. Sick to the stomach, that was, knocking down something you'd only just built, but there weren't no other way of it.

Day the frame finally stood up though, roof timbers on, you could stand inside it and see the shape of the rooms for the first time, that was a kushti divvus. Everybody stood round having a look, even them that hadn't lifted a finger on it come round to dick at it, and the wife brought tea out and we stood there in what was going to be the front room drinking it out of the wind for the first time. Eldest chavo was old enough by then to be up on the boards himself passing block, and proud as anything about it.

Last stretch of it, we was out there four nights running past dark, torches rigged up off the van battery, finishing the last of the plastering and the fitting, everybody dog tired, chavvies long since put to bed by their nan, but nobody wanted to stop that close to done. Gavver come round the second night, some gorja up the lane had rung about the noise, had a look, had a laugh about it more like, told us to keep it down after eleven and left us to it. Fourth night we come in past midnight and the wife just looked at us and put the kettle on and didn't say a word, which was its own kind of kushti.

And then it stood finished. Eleven month, near enough, from the first sod turned to the last nail in, six pairs of hands through it one time and another, my brother-in-law who was there every week of it bar none, my brother, the wife's brother, my cousin and his boy, and that mush from two doors down who never left once he'd started. We sat down after and totted it up, laughed at how far out our first guess had been, four months we'd said, five at the outside, and it took near three times that with a wall pulled down twice over and a week lost to the rain and another lost chasing block round three different yards.

Stand in it now of an evening, chavvies thundering up and down stairs we built with our own hands, and you don't think about the fortnight lost or the wall come down twice or the board that give way under me. You think, we built this, all of us, with our own two hands and whoever's turned up to lend theirs, and it's ours, and it'll stand long after the ones that put it up are gone to dust.
