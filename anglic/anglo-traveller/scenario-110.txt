Mother: Well go on then, sat there like the cat got your tongue, what's it like, this place of yours.

Me: Honest, Mum, it's a different world. You can't hardly picture it till you're in it.

Auntie: Bigger than round here I s'pose. Everything's bigger up there or so they say.

Me: Bigger ain't the word for it. You'd walk an hour some divvus and still be the same side of it you started. I mean that, an hour just to cross from one end to the other, and you've not even left the one bit of it.

The eldest: Get out. An hour to go nowhere.

Me: I'm telling you straight. And the noise of it never stops, day nor night. Round here of a night you get the dog barking, the juk two vardos over, or the wind in the trees, that's your lot. There it's engines and horns and folk shouting and I don't know what else going all hours. First few weeks I couldn't sleep for it, kept waiting for it to go quiet and it never does.

Grandmother: No wonder you look thin, mush, not sleeping.

Me: I'm alright, Nan, you get used to it. Sort of.

Uncle: So where you living then, in all this bigness.

Me: Little flat, up above a shop. One room does for the sitting and the eating both, and a box room off it barely fits the bed. You'd think a place that size in a place that big'd be a mansion and it's the opposite, it's tiny, smaller than the vardo near enough, only it don't move and there's no stopping tan out the back, just a bit of concrete and some bins.

Auntie: And what's it cost you, a little box like that.

Me: More than you'd want to hear. What I pay for that one room'd near enough buy the ground under this whole place. Everything's dear there, the hobben, the bus, all of it. You go to get your messages and it's gone before you've filled one bag.

The eldest: Robbing gorjas.

Me: It ain't just them, everyone's paying it, that's just what it costs to be there.

Cousin: Are they friendly though, the people there.

Me: That's not a straight yes or no, that one. Some of them, kushti as you like, hold a door, pass the time of day, ask how you're getting on. Others walk straight past you like you're a lamppost, won't dick at you twice in the street. It's not unfriendly exact, it's more like everyone's got somewhere to be and no time to be nowhere else. You can live by a mush a whole year and never learn his name, that's the truth of it.

Grandmother: Don't sound like much of a life to me.

Me: It's not what we've got, that's certain. But it ain't cruel either, just different, folk keep to their own business more.

Uncle: Where d'you get your hobben then, is there a market or the like.

Me: There's a market, aye, and shops besides, big ones, open all hours near enough. But the market's the best of it, all sorts there, stuff you'd never see round here. I go of a Saturday mostly.

Auntie: And the work, how's a mush find work in a place like that.

Me: Different to round here altogether. No knocking on doors, no asking about, half of it's done through a screen, you put your name down and wait to hear. I done a bit of this and a bit of that before I landed the one I've got now, on the sites still, same trade near enough, only bigger jobs, more mushes on it, and a gavver of a foreman watching the clock on you.

The eldest: Still graft though.

Me: Still graft. That never changes wherever you are.

Cousin: Did you get lost much, first off.

Me: Lost? I was lost three, four times a week starting out, dinlo that I was, walking round in circles looking for the same corner I'd just come from. Everything looks the same there till you learn it, same shopfronts, same sort of streets over and over. Took me the best part of a year before I could jal about the place without my heart going, before I knew which turn led where. Now I could nearly do it with my eyes shut, but it took some learning, I won't lie to you.

Grandmother: And the hobben, is it good there.

Me: Better in some ways, worse in others, I'd say. More of everything, food from every corner of the world if you want it, and some of it lovely, proper lovely. But plain and simple, the sort Nan does, a bit of bacon and cabbage and done right, that's harder to come by. Everything's dressed up fancy or it's rubbish out a packet, not much in between. I do miss a proper Sunday dinner, the kind we get here.

Uncle: So come on then, straight up, is it better or worse than home.

Me: Ah now, that's not a fair question, is it.

Uncle: Fair enough question I'd say. Better or worse.

Me: It's neither, it's just its own thing. Different sort of good, different sort of hard. You can't hold the two of them up side by side like that and say one wins, they ain't the same sort of life to start with.

The eldest: That's you dodging it, mush.

Me: Maybe it is. Wouldn't be the first time.

Mother: Well, last thing then, and I want a proper answer this time, none of your dodging. Will you come back, for good I mean, not just visiting.

Me: I don't know, Mum, honest to God I don't. Some divvus I miss it that bad I'd jal home tonight and never look back, this life, the fire, all of yous rokkering away half the night. Other times I think of what I've built up there and I'm not ready to walk from it yet. So I'll say what I can say true, which is I don't know. Might be. Might not. Ask me again next time I'm home and see what I say then.

Grandmother: You'll come back. They always come back.

Me: We'll see, Nan. We'll see.
