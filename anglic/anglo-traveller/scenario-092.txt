By the middle of the night it had thinned right down to just us, the five of us, everyone else had said what they come to say and jalled off home, and now it was just the fire going low and the pot gone stewed and nobody minding. The widow had her shawl round her shoulders even though it wasn't cold, just something to hold onto I reckon, and the rest of us sat round her not saying much for a bit, just letting the quiet sit there with us.

Then the widow started it, quiet like, not looking at anyone in particular.

The widow: He used to stand out by the gate of a morning, first thing, before anyone else was even up. Didn't matter what the weather was doing. I'd be shouting at him come in out of that, you'll catch your death, and he wouldn't even turn his head.

The eldest: That wasn't the gate though, Mam. That was down the bottom, by where the grai used to be tethered. You've got that muddled up again.

The widow: Have I. (she laughed, soft) I always do get that wrong, don't I. In my head it's always the gate, I don't know why. Maybe because that's where I'd be stood shouting after him.

The daughter let a laugh out then, quick and sudden, one she plainly hadn't meant to let go, and she put her hand up over her mouth after like she could take it back. Nobody minded it. It weren't the wrong kind of laugh, everyone there knew that.

The son-in-law: Go on then, tell the gate one properly, I've heard the bottom field one a hundred times.

There was a bit of quiet again after that, just the fire ticking, and then the eldest sat forward with his cup between his two hands.

The eldest: You remember what he used to say though. Every single divvus, near enough, first thing out his mouth. Put the kettle on, there's a mush at the door, even when there weren't no mush at any door. Just liked saying it.

The widow: Go on then.

The eldest, dropping his voice down lower, doing it exact the way the old man used to say it: Put the kettle on, there's a mush at the door.

Nobody said anything for a second after that. It landed the way them things do, hearing it said back proper, in the actual rhythm of it, like he was stood just outside the trailer door saying it himself.

Me: He said that to the postman once and the fella near jumped out his skin.

That got a small laugh round, the kind that eases a room rather than fills it.

Then the youngest, who'd been quiet up till then, sat forward like she'd been holding something back all night waiting for the right gap to put it in.

The youngest: I don't think any of you know this one. When I was small, proper small, he used to take me down the gorja shops of a Saturday and buy me sweets on the quiet, and tell me not to say nothing to Mam because she'd have his guts.

The widow: He never.

The youngest: He did. Every Saturday, near enough a whole year of Saturdays.

The widow: Well I never knew that in my life. (a short, startled sort of laugh, half shock in it) All them years and he never once let on.

The room went quiet again after that, proper quiet, the kind where everyone's just sat with it a minute, and then the son-in-law got up without being asked and started passing the hobben round again, the bread and the bit of meat left over, just something to do with his hands and give everyone else something to do with theirs too.

The son-in-law: Get that ate, no sense it going to waste.

And while the plate was going round the eldest and the son-in-law had a bit of a moment between them, the old business about the last grai the old man sold before he took bad, whether it went for what it was worth or whether the eldest had let it go too cheap to settle a debt that weren't even his to settle. Voices went up a notch for a second, sharp enough that the widow looked between the two of them, but neither one pushed it any further. Not tonight, one of them said, and that was the end of it, the plate kept going round same as before.

Then the daughter, maybe to shift it off that, started on the stubborn one.

The daughter: Do you remember when he wouldn't have the doctor out for that hand, and it swole up like a bladder, and he still wouldn't have him. Said there weren't nothing wrong that a bit of time wouldn't sort.

The eldest: Weeks he went on like that. Wouldn't dick at it properly himself even, just kept it wrapped up and carried on same as ever, doing everything one-handed sooner than admit it.

The widow: I told him, you dinlo, you'll lose that hand carrying on like that, and he just looked at me like I'd said something daft.

The son-in-law: And then when he finally did go he made out it was his own idea all along.

That got a proper laugh, warm, no edge left in it at all now, the kind of laugh that only comes once enough time's gone by that the fear's worn off it and just the daftness of the man is left standing.

Nobody said it straight out, but it sat there plain enough in the room after that, the thing they'd all remember him by, that stubbornness, not the hard kind, the soft daft kind, the kind that only ever hurt himself and never anybody else, and every story that got told after that circled back round to some version of the same thing, him digging his heels in over something that never mattered a bit.

Partway through the next one, the daughter started crying, quiet, not making any show of it, just sat there with the tears going while the talk carried on gentle around her, nobody stopping to make a fuss of it, just somebody's hand finding her shoulder without a word said about it, and the talk kept moving same as before, soft, unhurried.

Then it turned practical, the way it does eventually.

The eldest: Right. I'll see to the men about the site in the morning, squaring it with whoever needs squaring, so we're not moved on before we're ready.

The son-in-law: I'll deal with any of the wonga side of it, what's owed and what's due, so nobody else has to think on that.

The widow: Somebody'll need to tell the ones down the other atchin tan, they'll want to know before word gets round wrong.

The youngest: I'll go over first thing.

And that was that settled, plain as anything, no ceremony to it at all, just who was doing what, same as deciding who'd fetch water of a morning.

It was the eldest still up at the end, the rest gone quiet or near enough asleep sat up, and he lifted his cup that had gone cold long since and didn't make any speech of it, just said what come to him.

The eldest: Here's to the old mush. Kushti divvus, wherever you've got to now. Save us a place by the fire.

And that was it, the cup went round one more time for the last of it, and the fire burned down low, and nobody said another word that night that needed saying.
