Oh you heard about that then, did ya. Right, sit yourself down a minute, cos it's a proper tale that, and everyone round here's got their own bit to add to it, but I'll give you it straight from the start.

So it were, what, back end of the year sometime, and first anyone heard of it was the young mort's little chavo, must've been six or seven, come running in saying he'd seen a great big dog-fox thing down by the far bins, bigger than any juk he'd ever seen, standing dead still just watching him. Course nobody believed a word of it. His mother clipped him round the ear and told him he'd been at the telly too long and to stop making things up, and half the atchin tan was laughing about it for a divvus or two after. Kids see all sorts, don't they, monsters under the trailer and all that, so nobody give it a second thought.

Then not two, three weeks on, the old boy down the end, the one who's kept greyhounds his whole life and don't scare easy and don't tell tales neither, he seen it himself, broad daylight and all, going through the black bags what had come loose off the top of the bin store. And when he says he seen summat, you believe him, cos that mush don't rokker rubbish. So then it weren't the chavvies' imagination no more, it were real, and that's when the whole street started dicking out their windows of a night to see if they could spot it.

And it were about, doing what a fox does, weren't it. In the bins, tipping em, dragging bags out across the grass, and getting into people's little gardens an all, the veg patches, scratching about, and it frightened the chickens something rotten down one end so they wouldn't lay for a week. Nothing too terrible to look at it plain, but it put the wind up people all the same, a wild thing coming right up close to where the chavvies play.

That's when the street split proper in two, like it does. Half of em saying leave the poor thing be, it's only doing what it's got to do to eat, it ain't bothering nobody serious, and the other half saying no, it wants dealing with before it does summat worse, before it gets in with the little dogs or the chavvies get too close to it thinking it's tame. Proper falling out over it an all, cousins not speaking to cousins over a fox, if you can believe that.

Then the washing went missing off my auntie's line, whole set of it, blew off in the night she reckoned, but half the street said it were the fox, dragged it off for its den, and there weren't a scrap of proof either way, just people rowing about it, some saying a fox wouldn't touch washing, others saying it'd take anything that moved if it were hungry enough. Same week somebody's rabbit hutch got its door stood open and the rabbit gone, and that got blamed on it an all, though truth be told the latch on that hutch never did sit right and everybody knew it.

All this come to a head one evening when a good few of us was sat round the fire having a natter, and it turned into a right shouting match. One side wanting it trapped and done with, the other side saying you leave a wild thing be, it's unlucky to go killing summat that ain't done you no real harm, and voices going up and up till the old dear had to tell everyone to pack it in before they woke every chavo on the site.

One family, they wouldn't wait on it being sorted proper, they had a go themselves. Set some manner of trap by their own bins two nights running, stayed up watching an all, and got nothing for it but a sprung trap and no fox, and the husband near broke his ankle chasing a shadow across the grass thinking he'd got it. Made him a right dinlo in front of everyone, that did, and he weren't best pleased about it being brought up for weeks after.

Then come the night that really shook it up. The mother two trailers down, going out to the wash-house late on, near stood right on it in the dark, it screamed right up at her, that fox-scream what sounds like a woman crying out, and she come back in white as anything and shaking, wouldn't hear a word said against getting rid of it after that. That's what brought the whole row to a head proper, cos even them as wanted it left be had to admit it'd give anybody a fright coming on it sudden like that in the dark. But there was still them saying it weren't dangerous, only unlucky in where it'd chose to wander, that a fox screaming in the night's just a fox being a fox, and no different to giving somebody a jolt walking round a corner into a stranger.

So in the end somebody had the sense to jal and get a fella in who actually knew about such things, a proper pest man, gorja fella but decent with it, done this kind of work for years. He come round, had a good look at the bins, the gardens, where it'd been getting in under the fencing, and he told the street straight it weren't after nobody, it were only doing what any hungry animal does when the hobben's easy got at, and there weren't no call to go killing it over that, but he'd help secure it up proper so it lost the taste for coming so close.

And that's the terms the street settled on in the end. Bins all got proper lids put on, weighted and clipped down so nothing could tip em, and the gap under the fencing got blocked up where it come through. The old boy with the greyhounds, he sort of took it on himself, without nobody asking him mind, to keep half an eye out of a night, just a look round before he turns in, and if he dicks it he don't say much, just mentions it over a cup of tea in the morning like it's nothing.

And that's about it now, if you want the truth of it. Sightings come every now and again still, somebody'll see it crossing the grass of an early morning or hear it having a go at summat in the far corner, but nobody jumps up over it no more. All the alarm's gone out of it, and it's just one of them things gets talked about round the fire of an evening, more for a laugh than owt else, how that one mush near broke his ankle chasing his own shadow, how the mother screamed the place awake, how half the street fell out over a fox that never chor'd nothing but a washing line and a rabbit that had a bad latch to begin with. Nobody round here's frightened of it now. It's just part of the place, same as anything else that's always been here.
