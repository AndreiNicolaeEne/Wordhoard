This is the way it was give to me, sat on the steps of the vardo my nan still kept for best, not to live in no more but to keep, and I'm going to tell it the same way now, the way it come down to me, so it don't get lost between one telling and the next.

Before ever they come down this way the family was up north, proper up, working the fairs and the hiring days, dealing a bit of everything, grais mostly, and stopping where they could get a nod off a farmer for the night. It weren't a bad life up there by all accounts, but the ground got hard for our sort. The stopping places kept getting shut up one after another, gorja farmers that used to let you in the corner of a field turned nasty, said no more of that, said move on before the gavvers come and move you on theirselves. Work dried up an' all, the fairs going small, less call for a good grai once the engines started coming in everywhere. So it weren't one thing pushed them, it was a slow closing up all round, till there weren't hardly nowhere left to put a wheel down.

The one that come first was the old man's brother, my great-great-uncle, though he weren't spoke of much by that name, just "him as went first." He jalled off alone, walking most of it, leading one grai and what he could carry, and he'd told them he'd send word back once he found ground worth the family following him to. That was the plan of it. He'd heard there was work down south, farm work, hopping, and stopping places that weren't so quick to turf a body off.

My nan used to tell it like this, sat there with her hands going the whole time like she was still folding something:

The nan: He went off with nothing but the clothes he stood in and that grai, and he never once wrote a letter home, because what would he write it with and who would read it him anyway. Word come back by mouth, off other travellers on the road that had passed him and carried the news on. That's how it went in them days, one mush telling another mush, all down the roads, till it landed back where it started.

The journey he had of it weren't no easy thing, and I won't make it sound easy neither. Weeks of it, sleeping rough under the vardo when he even had one, going hungry more days than he'd have liked, the grai going lame on him at one point so he'd to walk it slower than he wanted, through weather that didn't care one way nor other whether he lived or dropped. There was a river crossing somewhere in it that near did for him, current higher than he'd reckoned, and he come out the other side of it with nothing dry to his name and half his gear gone downstream. He got down here in the end with the grai, a bit of harness, and precious little else. No wonga, no place fixed, nothing.

First while he had, there weren't no atchin tan of his own to speak of. A family already stopping down here, distant kin by marriage, took him in alongside their own fire, let him bide with them, borrowed ground if you like, not his to claim, just theirs he was let share of. He hobbened off what they hobbened, slept where they said, and worked when there was work, which for a long stretch weren't often. Day here, day there. A farmer wanting a hedge laid, another wanting help at the hopping when the season come round, odd jobs a gorja wouldn't bother lifting a hand for himself. No steady graft, nothing you could call a living, just enough some weeks to put a bit by and other weeks nothing at all.

It took him the best part of a year before he'd anything worth calling savings, and longer again before he'd a way of earning that held up regular. What turned it for him was the dealing, same as the family had always known, only down here it were dealing what he could pick up cheap off one gorja and sell dear to the next, bit of harness, bit of tack, then grais when he'd the eye for a good one again, and later on when the grais started going out of fashion he turned his hand to engines the same way, buying rough and selling on. That trade stuck. It's the trade the men of this family have kept at one way or other ever since, dealing, whatever the article of the day happens to be.

Once he'd enough put by and a stopping place that was his to call his, not borrowed off nobody, he sent the word back up north the same road it had come down, mush to mush, that there was ground here and hobben enough and would they come. It weren't quick. Two year and a bit it took, near enough, before the rest of them, his own mother, his brothers, the whole clat of them, made the same road down after him. Two year apart, him down here alone building what he could, them up there waiting on the word, not knowing from one season to the next was he even living still.

He weren't alone the whole while though, not truly, because there was a wife's family that had a chavo about the family's age, and they'd got close enough by then that when he sent for his own blood he sent for her an' all, and she come down in that same crowd. Kushti that was, my nan always said, kushti for him not to be the only new face when they landed.

Not everyone made the journey down whole. There was a young one, a cousin to the old man, that took bad on the road, some fever that come on quick and wouldn't shift, and there weren't no doctor near enough nor money for one if there had been. The nan never gave that one a name when she told it, not once in all the tellings I heard, just "the young one that didn't make it," or "the poor little chavo we lost on the road." That's how it's still spoke of now, no name to it, just the loss, kept that way out of respect more than forgetting, I think, though I never rightly asked why.

They didn't come down with much. A pot, some harness, a few bits of blanket, and off the mother that made it down there was a small brass box, no bigger than your two hands together, that had been her own mother's before her, up north, and it come the whole way down on that journey and it's kept in the family yet, passed along the women's side, never sold, never even offered for sale no matter how thin things got in bad years. It sits in my own place now, and I'll pass it to my own girl same as it was passed to me.

Once the family was whole again down here, they found a stopping place proper, ground a farmer was willing to let stand as a fixed atchin tan rather than a one-night favour, and within a few year of landing they'd settled into it good and proper, built it up bit by bit, and that's near enough the same ground some of the family sits on to this day. It weren't a house at first, just the vardos set right and a bit of fencing put up, but it were theirs to stop on and nobody moving them off it come Monday morning.

And it was the chavvies born right there on that ground, the old man's own children and then his brothers' after, that made it home proper, my nan always said. Not the arriving, not even the stopping, but the being born there, that's what turned ground into home for travelling people, she said, because once your own chavo first opens his eyes looking at a bit of land, that land's yours whatever paper says otherwise.

That's the whole of it, near enough the way she give it me, sat there of an evening with the kettle going, and I'd sit and dick at her hands folding away at nothing while she rokkered on, not understanding half the weight of it at the time, only understanding it proper once I'd chavvies of my own to tell it to. So that's how I'm passing it on now, same road it come down to me, mush to mush, so as it don't get lost, so as whoever hears it after me knows where their own feet are standing and what it cost to get them stood there.
