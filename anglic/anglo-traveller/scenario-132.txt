Eleven divvus that cold held, straight through, never once let go, and I don't reckon anyone in this trailer's ever known the like of it. First morning it come down we all said, well, it'll break by dinner, it always does. It never broke. It just sat on top of us like a lid and got worse.

We had the stove going round the clock, never let it drop, someone up every couple of hour chucking more on, and still you could see your breath sat inside same as out. Chavvies slept three to a bunk just to keep the warm between them, wrapped up proper, and even then the littlest one was crying with it some nights.

The tap on the standpipe went solid on the third divvus, never happened before in all the years we've stood on that ground. Froze right through the pipe and cracked it an' all, so it was carrying cans of water from down the lane morning and night, hands burning with the cold of the handle alone.

The eldest: I mind the winter your grandfather lost the grai to the ice, back when I was no bigger than you, and I thought nothing could touch that one for cold.

Me: And now, Nan?

The eldest: Now I'll tell you straight, chavo, that one was kushti weather next to this.

The worst night was the eighth, no argument on it, the wind come round from the north and it was like the metal itself was trying to get in at you. We lost three of the hens that night, found them stiff in the coop come morning, no saving them, and that's food and a bit of egg money gone for the winter both.

Eleven divvus it held and then the twelfth morning the wind turned soft, just like that, dripping off the roof by dinner time, and I swear I could've stood out in that thaw and rokkered to it like an old mush, just glad to feel my own face again.
