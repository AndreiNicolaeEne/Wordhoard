Well I'll tell you how the divvus went, start to finish, because you wasn't there and it ought to be told proper.

The house was my nan's, had been years and years, bought back when the council was still moving families on left right and centre and she wanted four walls that couldn't be shifted off a verge by no gavver. She never give up the road in her heart, mind, she just had somewhere to come back to between times. When she went, back in the spring, none of us fancied keeping the rent going on a place with nobody living in it, so it was settled quick enough, the house had to go, sold on to whoever the agent found, and everybody who'd ever called it home was to come and clear it before the new lot took it. Not just my mother and my uncles neither. Aunts, cousins, my nan's own sister who's near eighty herself, a couple of the older chavvies who was old enough to be some use, the lot of us, cars and vans up the lane from early doors.

We started in the front room, kettle on before anything else because you can't rokker sense into anybody without a cup in their hand first, and my aunt says, sound as anything, "Right, we're not stood about all divvus, let's get to it," and that was that, coats off, sleeves up.

First thing that stopped us dead was in that same front room. My mother pulled an old shawl out the bottom of the sideboard, proper worn thing, moth at the corner of it, and she went quiet holding it, and then she says, "This was hers for chapel and for funerals both, I used to watch her fold it just so," and she had to sit down a minute with it on her knee before she could carry on. Set the whole room off soft for a bit.

Second thing turned up not ten minutes after, and this one nobody could make head nor tail of. A little tin box, locked, no key anywhere, rattling with something inside, and none of the old ones could say where it come from or whose it ever was. My uncle give it a shake by his ear and says, "Never seen this in my life, and I've been in and out this house forty year," and it got put to one side, still shut, still a mystery, and stops there to this day if truth be told.

Then come the row over the dresser. Big old thing, dark wood, been in the kitchen since before any of us was born, and two of my cousins was at it proper, one saying it was owed her on account of she was the one drove nan to hospital every appointment for two years, the other saying that counted for nothing, family's family and it should go by who's got room for it. Went on that long my aunt had to step in.

Aunt: "Right, enough. Whoever's got a house big enough to stand it gets it, and that's the end of the rokkering."

That settled it, because only the one of them had a proper house at all, the rest of us in trailers or too tight for a thing that size, so it went to her and no more said.

Different story with the old chair by the stove. Half the family wanted shot of it outright, said it was falling apart and no use to man nor mush, chuck it on the fire and be done. The other half wouldn't hear of it, said it was the one my nan sat in every single divvus of her life and it wasn't going nowhere near a skip while they'd breath in them.

Cousin: "It's rotten through the arm, dick at it, it's not fit to sit a chavo in never mind a grown mort."

The eldest: "I don't care if it's fit or not, it stops in the family or I'll have summat to say about it."

Nobody wanted to be the one that crossed her, so the chair got wrapped in a blanket and put in the back of a van, and that's where that stood.

The find that got everyone was under the floorboard in the back bedroom, one of the chavvies pulled up a loose one messing about and there was an old biscuit tin stuffed with letters, my nan's marriage lines, and a fold of notes gone soft with age, proper old money, more wonga than anyone expected to see turn up like that. Dead silence in that room a good minute, then my uncle says, "She never told a living soul about this, the sly old article," and everyone had a laugh through it even though half of them was near crying two minutes before.

That back bedroom, hers, was the one that ate the whole afternoon. Every other room we was through in an hour, hour and a half, but that one nobody could hurry through. Every drawer had something in it somebody had to stop and hold and pass round. Her sister sat on the edge of the stripped bed going through a box of photos so slow you'd think she had all week, and nobody rushed her, because you don't rush a mort through the last of her own sister's things.

By the time that was near done everybody's stomachs was going, so we broke off, spread a cloth over what was left of the kitchen table and everyone tucked into whatever'd been brought, sandwiches, a flask of tea each, one of the aunts had done a proper pot of stew that morning knowing full well nobody would've thought to eat otherwise. Chavvies running about the garden while the grown ones sat on boxes and old chairs eating together, first proper laugh of the divvus, somebody telling a story on my nan that had the whole yard going.

After that it was the big wardrobe out the front bedroom needed shifting, great heavy thing, and not a soul among us wanted it, too big for any trailer, too battered for any of the houses either, so in the end it went out the door on its side and left by the gate for the scrap mush to take, and nobody argued a word over that one, everybody just glad to see the back of it once it was decided.

Her teapot though, the brown one she made every cup in for sixty year, that went without a single word of argument, my mother just picked it up, wrapped it in a tea towel and says, "This is mine," and not one person round that kitchen so much as looked sideways at her over it. Some things settle themselves.

Last room to stand empty was the little box room off the landing, and when the last bag come out of it and it was bare boards and bare walls, somebody stood in the doorway a minute just looking at the empty of it, because a house don't feel like a house no more once every room's like that.

What was left after, odds and ends, spare linen, bits of crockery, a few old tools out the shed, got spread on the front step and divided out there and then, this to one, that to another, no fuss to it by then, everybody worn out and easy with each other after the divvus they'd had.

Last thing, my uncle went round every room one more time, checking nothing left behind, and then he stood in the hall and pulled the door to behind him, proper shut, and turned the key, and that was it. House give up for good. We all stood on the path a minute not saying much, then jal on, cars pulling off one after the other down the lane, and that was the last any of us ever stood inside them four walls.
