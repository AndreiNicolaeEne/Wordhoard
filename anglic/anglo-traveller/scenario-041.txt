Right, I'll tell you the whole of it, start to finish, because you keep asking and I keep going on about it round the fire like it's still fresh, and I s'pose it is still fresh in a way. Two year I was there. Two year, near enough to the divvus, loading and stacking at that timber yard out past the ring road, up at six, home stinking of creosote and sawdust in me hair, and I stuck it, I did, longer than any of my family reckoned I would, longer than I reckoned meself if I'm honest.

Weren't one thing that done it in the end. That's what I've come to understand looking back. It was a long line of little things, wore down like a shoe on a stone track, and the last one weren't even the worst of them, not by a long chalk, just the one that happened to land on the day I'd nothing left to give it.

There was a new security fella took on at the gate, must be four month before I went, doing bag checks end of every shift on account of some tools going missing out the store back in the spring. Fair enough, I thought, first week or two. Only it weren't fair, not really, because he'd wave the gorja lads through with a nod and a "night then, mate," and every single divvus, rain or shine, he'd have me stood there opening mine up for him like it was customs. Never found nothing. Never was nothing to find. But it kept on, week after week, and nobody said a word to stop it, not the foreman, not the guvnor upstairs in his office who knew my name well enough when he wanted an extra shift covered.

The divvus it finally happened, it was nothing much. Bag check same as always, he's rooting through me flask and me gloves same as always, and he says, dead casual, like he's remarking on the weather, "can't be too careful round here, can we," and give this little laugh and waved me on. Didn't even look at me proper when he said it. And I walked out them gates and something in me just went quiet and hard at the same time, if that makes sense to you, and I thought, right, that's me done. That's it. Two year of biting me tongue and that was the drop that filled the cup over.

I didn't say nothing to nobody straight off. Went home to the atchin tan, sat with a brew, and it was the mother I rokkered to first, told her the whole of it, and she just nodded like she'd heard it a thousand times before, because she has, because we all have.

Mother: You want to sleep on it, chavo. Don't go in there hot.

Me: I'm not going in hot. I've been going in cold for two year and it's brought me here anyway.

So I did sleep on it, one night, and then I sat down proper the next evening and worked out what I was going to say. I wanted it kushti and calm, nothing thrown at nobody, nothing they could turn round after and say I'd made a scene, because that's always how it goes, isn't it, you say your piece dignified and it still gets called trouble after. So I practised it out loud to meself doing the washing up, practised it walking the dog round the field, had it word for word by the time I went in.

Asked the foreman could I have a word with the guvnor, proper, in the office, not just catching him on the yard. He looked at me a bit funny but he fetched him down.

Me: I wanted a word, if that's all right. Won't take long.

The boss: Course, come in, sit down. Everything all right?

And I sat down opposite him, hands in me lap steady as I could keep them, and I come out with the line I'd been turning over three days.

Me: I've been thinking a good while now, and I've decided it's time I moved on. It's not about any one thing in particular, I just feel it's the right time for a change.

Which was a lie, or half a lie, because it was about one thing in particular and about forty things besides it, but I wasn't going to stand there and list off every bag search and every sideways look I'd had off half the yard in two year. Truth is I held back because there's no arguing your way out of that sort of thing with a gorja in an office, not one who'll believe you over his own security fella regardless what you say, and I wasn't about to hand him the satisfaction of watching me get worked up over it. Kept it clean. Kept it mine.

He looked proper surprised, I'll give him that, sat back in his chair like I'd slapped the desk.

The boss: Blimey. I didn't see that coming, I'll be honest. You've been solid, one of the best we've had on that line. Is it money? Because I could look at getting you another fifty a month, and I could shift you off the early shifts if that's what's grinding you down.

Me: It's kind of you to offer, but no, I don't think that'd change how I'm feeling. I think I just need something fresh, that's all it is.

Which again weren't the whole truth, because no amount of wonga was going to make that fella stop rifling through me bag while the others strolled past, but you don't say that. You give the reason that lets everybody keep their dignity and walk away clean.

We settled on the two weeks, standard notice, shook on it civil enough, and he said he was sorry to lose me and meant it in his way, I think. But I'll tell you, walking back out onto that yard after, I had this cold little worm of doubt turn over in me stomach. Two year of a wage that come regular, and I'd just thrown it down over a bag search and a comment. Sat in the van that night and thought, have I gone and been a right dinlo here, chucking away a steady divvus's work over pride. Didn't sleep proper for a night or two after that, if I'm honest with you.

But the two weeks went, same as any two weeks go, and the last divvus come round quick. Fellas shook me hand, said take care, said keep in touch, all the usual, and it was mostly hollow, the way them things always are, everyone knowing full well they'll never see you again and being polite about it regardless. Foreman clapped me on the shoulder and said I'd be missed on the line, which might've even been true in a small way.

Only one bit of it felt honest, right at the end. The security fella himself, stood at the gate on my very last walk out, no bag to check that divvus, nothing left in me pockets belonged to the place, and he says, "all the best then," and there was a second, just a second, where I nearly said it. Nearly said, you know why I'm going, don't you, you know exactly what you done, plain as that, right to his face. Had it right up in me throat. But I only dicked at him a moment, held it, and said "yeah, you an' all," and jalled off down the road, and that not-saying-it has sat with me longer than near anything I did say the whole fortnight.

It's been a few month now since, and I'll tell you straight, it's relief sitting heaviest, not regret, though the regret does poke its head up on a quiet divvus now and then when the money's tight. But I sleep easier. Don't dread the gate of a morning. Got taken on doing groundwork with me cousin now, proper outdoor graft, and nobody's asking to look in me bag of an evening, I promise you that.

And if you asked me now, would I go back and do it different, I'd say only the one thing I'd change: I'd have said the true reason to his face that last divvus, instead of the kushti polite one I give him in the office. Everything else I'd do the very same. Two year was long enough to give any place that wasn't giving it back to me proper, and I don't lie awake over it no more, whatever that fella thought he was being careful about.
