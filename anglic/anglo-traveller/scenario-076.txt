Me: You can't keep on with this, mush. I mean it, this has got to stop now.

My friend: Stop what.

Me: The fighting, you know what. Them bare-knuckle dos out past the atchin tan of a Saturday night, that's what. Last one, when they carried you out that yard I thought I was watching you go, I did. I thought that was it.

My friend: I got up though, didn't I. Kushti as anything come the Sunday divvus.

Me: You never got up, not for the longest half minute of my life you never. Lay there blue round the mouth and none of them boys knew what to do with theirselves, and I'm stood there and my hands wouldn't stop shaking, couldn't hardly hold the phone to ring for help. Heart going like a juk chasing a hare down the road. That en't nothing. That en't sport no more, that's you nearly gone.

My friend: It's always been the same. Always has, since we was chavvies even.

Me: And what about your back, two winter gone? What about that, eh? Six weeks laid up in the vardo, couldn't lift a chavo, couldn't hardly dress yourself of a morning, and that was the lucky one. Next time it en't your back. Next time it's your neck don't hold, or your head don't come back right, or it don't come back at all.

My friend: Alright. Alright, leave it now.

Me: No, not alright. Who's left grieving then, tell me that. Your mort, your chavvies asking where their dad's got to, your mother stood crying over a hole in the ground while I never said one proper word to stop you. That's who.

My friend: ...I hear you, I do. I en't promising I'll never jal to one again, but I'll dick good and proper who I'm up against first, no more taking on any dinlo just because there's wonga in it. That's the truth I can give you.

Me: It'll do. For now, mush, it'll do.
