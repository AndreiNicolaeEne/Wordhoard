Right, I'll tell you the whole of it, because you weren't there and you missed a right performance and no mistake.

So it come up round the fire, whose turn it was to have the big divvus this year, everyone chucking it about, and before I could stop my own mouth I'd gone and said I'd have it. The mother looked at me like I'd lost my head altogether.

Mother: You sure, love? You know what it's like, doing for this lot.

Me: Yeah go on, I'll do it. How hard can it be.

Famous last words them, weren't they. Soon as I said it my stomach went over. Never done the whole dinner on my own before, always just helped, peeled a few spuds and stopped out the way. This time it was on me, start to finish, and I lay awake that first night with my head going nineteen to the dozen.

So I sat down and drew up the menu like I knew what I was doing. Turkey, course, but then I thought I'd do a goose an' all because the old boy always goes on about goose, and then I thought, well if I'm doing a goose I might as well do a proper trifle, and bread sauce, and a soup to start, and three kinds of veg, and pigs in blankets, and stuffing two ways because half of them like sage and half like chestnut, and by the time I'd finished writing it out it looked like I was catering for a wedding, not a dinner. The fella looked over my shoulder at the list and just laughed at me.

Fella: You're never doing all that, mush. Who's this for, the whole gavvers' station?

Me: Shut up, it'll be kushti, you watch.

And the numbers kept creeping an' all. First it was just us and the mother and father. Then the auntie says she's coming and bringing her lot. Then my cousin's mort rings up and says can they come, they've nowhere else to be. Then the uncle says he'll pop in "for an hour" which we all know means all day. By the finish I'd gone from feeding six to feeding sixteen, chavvies included, and not one extra thing bought in yet.

Shopping day was its own disaster. Round every shop for miles looking for a tin big enough to take that goose, because the one I had at home was nowhere near, and three different places didn't have one, and by the fourth shop I was ready to pack it in and boil the thing in a bucket. Found one in the end, dented on one side, didn't care, grabbed it. Everything else I could get, chestnuts, sausages, the lot, but that tin had me proper wound up.

Night before, I was up till gone one doing prep. Peeling, stuffing, chopping, the mother come over to give us a hand with the spuds and we sat there rokkering away half the night about nothing, keeping each other awake more than working half the time. Chavvies long gone to bed, juk stretched out under the table not moving for no one. I wrote myself out a whole schedule on paper before I finally went to bed, times for everything, bird in at this hour, veg on at that hour, like I was running a proper kitchen. Felt dead pleased with myself looking at it.

Didn't last five minutes past the actual morning, that schedule. Chavvies up at some ungodly hour wanting their breakfast same as any other day, not understanding why everyone's rushing about. Oven wouldn't come up to heat quick as I wanted. Someone wanted the bathroom the same minute I needed the sink for the veg. Whole paper plan went out the window before nine o'clock and I was just running on instinct and panic from then on.

Then the first proper disaster. I'd put they pigs in blankets in with the veg roasting and got dragged off dealing with the goose fat spitting everywhere, and by the time I remembered them they were black, proper black, ruined outright, stunk the whole trailer out an' all. I could've cried right there. Scraped the worst of them in the bin and had to send the fella's brother out quick to the shop for more bacon and sausages so I could do a fresh batch, praying they'd cook in time.

That's when the auntie turned up early, hour before she was meant to, walked straight in still with her coat on and took one look at my face.

The auntie: What's gone on here then?

Me: Don't ask, just don't ask.

The auntie: Right, give us an apron, I'm not stood here watching you have a breakdown.

And she was straight in, peeling and stirring and rolling the new sausages in bacon like she'd never taken her coat off, God love her, saved my life that woman did.

Oven was a proper battlefield by then, goose in, tin of roasties fighting for room, bread sauce needing a ring on the hob same time as the gravy, and the veg pans lined up three deep with nowhere to put them down hot. In the end I took the sprouts round to the mother-in-law's trailer to finish off in her oven because mine just wouldn't hold no more, running back and forth between the two like a mad woman.

Only good thing that whole morning, them sprouts. Done with bacon and chestnuts, and I only chucked the chestnuts in because I'd bought too many for the stuffing, no real plan behind it, and they come out better than anything else on that table, if I'm honest. Weren't expecting that at all.

Dinner that was meant to be on the table at two didn't get served till gone half three, everyone sat about getting hungrier and crosser, chavvies moaning, but soon as the food started coming out the mood turned right round.

The uncle: Now that's what I call a divvus dinner, that goose is kushti.

The eldest: Get in there, our kid done us proud.

First bites went down lovely, all the praise coming at once, made the whole morning worth it just about. Then the bread sauce got passed round and my cousin, never one to hold his tongue, just come straight out with it.

The cousin: This bread sauce is rank, love, tastes burnt, what's gone on there.

Me: Yeah, I know, I know, don't rub it in.

He weren't wrong either, it had caught on the bottom of the pan in all the chaos and I hadn't noticed till it was too late. Fair play to him for saying it plain rather than pretending, that's just how we are with each other.

But them sprouts, the ones I nearly didn't bother with, gone in about four minutes flat, empty bowl passed back for more before half the table had even had the goose. Loudest praise of the whole day went to that dish and it was the one I put the least thought into, funny how that goes.

By the end everyone sat round proper full, chavvies running off outside, the fire going, and I was wrecked through, hardly sat down all divvus. The fella put his arm round us and said:

Fella: Well, you got there in the end, mush.

Me: Never again. I mean it. Never again.

Mother: You said that last year an' all, when it was my turn.

And she was right, weren't she, because deep down I already knew, sat there dead tired with a kitchen like a bombsite behind us, that come next year I'd probably go and open my big mouth and say I'd have it again.
