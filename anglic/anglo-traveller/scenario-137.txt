The gate at the atchin tan was stood wide open when the grai come out onto the road, right as a gorja lorry come round the bend, and only the driver's quick hands kept it off being bones and blood on the tarmac. Everybody come piling out the vardos to see what the noise was, and the blame went straight onto my cousin's mort, who'd been sat inside feeding the chavvies their hobben the whole time and never gone near that gate.

The uncle: You left that gate open, didn't you. You're the one was meant to mind it.

Cousin's mort: I never touched that gate.

The uncle: Don't tell me that. Somebody let that grai out and it never opened it itself, did it.

Cousin's mort: I never touched it.

She said it once, flat, no raising her voice up to meet his, no going on about where she'd been sat nor who else had come through by there that divvus. The uncle's own boy and the young fella who'd had the horses in hand all afternoon never said one word through any of it, stood dickin at the ground like it had gone sudden interesting. My mother caught my eye and I caught hers and we said nothing neither, and that's a shame sits on a person after.

The uncle stood there waiting on her face to crack, waiting for tears or temper, something to feed on, but she give him nothing, stood there kushti and steady, looking back at him level till he run dry of words to say.

He went off rokkering to himself, something about a dinlo loose about the place and nothing done right no more round here, and she jalled off back to the vardo without another word to nobody. The gate hung open same as before, no sorry said, no different said, and the grai grazed the verge quiet as you like, like it weren't nothing to do with him neither, and half the atchin tan knowing full well whose fault it really was, and not one saying so, not wanting the gavvers called nor the day made any worse than it already was.
