So the house is sold, right, papers signed and all, and the new crowd wants in by the first of the month, so there was nar a way round it, we had to get down there and clear it out proper. Nan's been gone since the winter and none of us could face it before now, but Aunt said if we left it any longer we'd be doing it in the snow again, and she's not wrong, so she rung round everyone, not just us, mind, she got the cousins out from Torbay and the in-laws and even the fella next door who used to cut Nan's grass, cause he was near enough family the last twenty years anyway. Some crowd we had by nine in the morning, cars up both sides of the road.

We started in the kitchen cause that's the heart of the place and you got to get your legs under you before you tackle the rest. Aunt went into the pantry and come out holding this old chipped mug, blue rim near wore off it, and she went quiet right there with it in her two hands.

Aunt: Luh at this. This is the one she'd have her tea in every morning of her life, this exact one, cracked handle and all. I'm after not thinking about that mug in twenty years and now I can't think of nar thing else.

The Eldest: Keep it so, don't be leaving it in the box with the rest of the crockery.

Aunt: I will, b'y. I will.

Right after that, down behind the flour bin, someone's workmate who come to help hauling turned up a little tin box, rusted to nothing, and inside it a brass button off some kind of uniform and a folded bit of paper too far gone to read.

Her Workmate: Anyone know whose this was to? There's no name nor nothing on it.

The Mother: I never seen that in me life. Could be Pop's father's, could be off a coat he never even owned. Nan never said a word about it to me.

The Eldest: Well it's a mystery so, put it up on the counter with the other mysteries.

We had a right go in the dining room over the table itself, the big one, cause half the crowd wanted it kept in the family and the other half said sure nobody's got room for a table that size in a townhouse.

The Uncle: That table's been in this house longer than any of us has, I'm not seeing it go to the dump or to some stranger neither.

The Cousin: Nobody said dump, we're saying who's got a kitchen big enough for it, that's all I'm saying.

The Eldest: I got the room for it. I'll take it and that's settled, and anyone wants a Sunday dinner round it, my door's open.

That quieted it down some, cause nobody argues much with the eldest when she puts it like that.

Then there was the ructions over the stuffed seabird up on the mantel, some kind of gull Pop had done up years back, moth-eaten now and one glass eye gone yellow.

The Cousin: That thing is shockin ugly and it's been giving me the willies since I was small, I say it's for the bin.

The Mother: You will not put Pop's bird in the bin in front of me, he shot that thing himself out the tickle and had it done proper, that's history that is.

The Cousin: It's a moth-eaten old crow with one eye, is what it is.

The Mother: It's a gull, and it's staying in the family, I don't care whose wall it goes on.

Nobody won that one outright, they just wrapped it in a towel and put it with Aunt's pile, and Aunt only rolled her eyes and said she supposed she was stuck with the ugly gull now, some laugh went round over that.

Down in the cellar was where the real find come, behind a loose board near the coal chute, a tin biscuit box full of letters tied up with string and a bit of money besides, old bills, not a fortune but not nothing either.

Uncle: Well I never. She had a stash down here the whole time and never told a soul.

The Mother: That's Nan for ya, sleeveen right to the end, God love her. She wouldn't tell ya the time of day if she thought you'd waste it.

We had a laugh over that one too, cause it was so like her, squirrelling things away and letting on she'd nothing.

The attic took the longest of the whole house by a mile, and that's saying something cause the cellar wasn't quick neither. Boxes of Christmas things, the mummers' old sheets and the bit of lace curtain Nan used for a veil, decorations going back to when the youngsters were small, photo albums three deep in dust, someone's old boil-up pot, a bag of buttons, another bag of nothing but string. Every box you opened had another story in it and someone stopping to look through it proper, so what should've took an hour took near the whole afternoon.

Round one o'clock everyone downed tools and Aunt had toutons going on the stove, the last of the kitchen still working before the stove itself went out the door, and someone else brought up a scoff of fish and brewis with the scrunchions done just right, and we all sat round on boxes and the porch step eating together, the young ones tearing round outside and the old fellas talking about the weather turning mauzy later on.

The Neighbour: Some good scoff, this. Haven't had brewis like this since I don't know when.

Her Workmate: Whadda y'at over there, don't be hogging all the scrunchions.

That was the best part of the day if I'm honest, everyone sat round together, half laughing half not, cause it was a hard old day underneath it all.

After the break we got the piano out of the front room, and that was the one thing nobody, in the end, wanted. Everyone had a reason. One had no room, one had no youngsters left to learn it, one said she never could stand the sound of it since her own lessons as a girl.

The Cousin: I'd take it only I haven't the room, honest to God.

The Uncle: Nor I. It's a grand old piano but sure who's playing it.

In the end a fella came with a truck and took it off for the church hall, and that felt right somehow, better than a stranger's front room anyway.

The one thing nobody argued over at all was Nan's clock off the mantel, the one that chimed on the half hour and drove half the house mad growing up. The minute someone said the eldest should have it, on account of she was the one minding Nan those last years, everyone just nodded, not a word of argument in it, which was some strange after the day we'd had.

By duckish the last room, the little back bedroom, stood bare, just the marks on the wall where the bed frame had sat for forty years and a patch of brighter paint where a picture hung. We stood in the doorway of it a minute, nobody saying much.

The Mother: Well. That's that, then.

What was left over, the odds and ends nobody had claimed proper, we spread out on the kitchen floor and just went round taking what we wanted, no fighting left in anyone by then, everyone dead tired.

The last thing was locking the door. Aunt held the key a good while before she turned it, and there was a silver thaw starting on the wires outside, everything gone quiet and shining. Someone said go way b'y when the key finally turned easy, like even the lock was letting go of it too. We stood out front a minute, the whole crowd of us, looking up at it one last time, and then we all went home our separate ways.
