Well now, b'y, you wants to hear the whole of it, do ya. Sit down then, I'll put the kettle on and we'll have a mug-up while I tells it, cause it's some long story and I'm not one for the short version anyway.

It started on account of we were bursting out of the old place. Four of us and then five, and the young fella and the young one sharing a room the size of a porch, and me and the wife still in the bed we had since we got married, no closet to speak of, and the whole crowd tripping over each other of a Sunday morning trying to get ready for anything. I'm after saying to the wife for two winters running, we can't keep on like this, and she'd say I know it, and neither of us did a blessed thing about it till the roof started letting in over the stairs and that was that. Something had to give.

So we sat down at the table one night, me and the wife and the brother who's handy with a saw, and we reckoned it out. Figured three of us steady and maybe four months if the weather held and the Lord was good to us. Some optimistic, that was. Should've knowed better. Eleven months it took in the end, start to finish, and six different sets of hands passed through that yard before we were done, but we didn't know that the night we sat reckoning it up over tea, thinking we had it sized up grand.

First wrong turn came awful early, mind you, before we were hardly out of the ground. The brother laid out the footings by eye more than by string, said he had it, no bother, and I trusted him cause he's built sheds and stages his whole life. Turned out he was off near four inches over the run of it, and we didn't catch it till the forms were poured and set hard as sin. Had to break the whole lot out with the sledge and start over, and that cost us near two weeks of work we'd already done once. I remember standing there looking at the rubble of it thinking, oh me nerves, here we go, and the brother not saying much, just looking shockin sorry for himself and swinging that sledge harder than he needed to.

Then there was the day the extra hands showed up and nobody called them. I'm out there of a Saturday morning with just the brother, the two of us trying to wrestle a wall panel up on our own and losing the battle of it, and up the road comes buddy from two doors down, and behind him his son-in-law, and behind them a fella I only half knowed from down the tickle side who'd heard from someone that we were at it. Never asked a one of them. They just come. Where ya to with that end, buddy says, grab on there, and inside twenty minutes we had that wall standing plumb and the five of us grinning like fools over it. That's just the way of it round here, you don't ask, you just goes.

The weather got us good in the middle of it all. A stretch come in off the water, rain sideways for days, then it turned to that thick mauzy fog that sits on ya and won't lift, and there was no working in that, nothing dries, nothing sets right, and the whole crowd of us sat around for the better part of a week doing nothing but drinking tea and looking out the window at it and cursing the sky. Right miserable stretch, that was. You loses your rhythm in a week like that, and getting everyone back and keen again after took some doing.

Round about month five we run right out of two-by-six, order was late coming and the truck couldn't get up the lane with the mud from all that rain anyway, so we made do with what we had lying round, some of it salvage off an old stage that was coming down, some of it shorter stock we had to double up and brace different than we planned. Wasn't pretty in a couple spots but it held, and the brother says, sure b'y, nobody's gonna see that once she's closed in, and he was right, nobody has.

I had me a fall off the scaffold up round the gable end, second storey height, and I'm not shy to say it put the heart crossways in me. Went down with a board that wasn't nailed proper and landed half in the wheelbarrow full of shavings, if you can believe that, and the wife come running out screaming blue murder thinking she was after losing me for real. Didn't break nothing, didn't even sprain nothing, just laid there a minute getting me breath while the whole crowd stood over me white as ghosts. Some scare, that was. I got up laughing after, cause what else can ya do, but I tell ya, I looked at that scaffold different after that.

Had a right go with the brother-in-law over the porch roof, how the pitch of it should run. He wanted it steep for the snow load, said he'd seen a place up the shore where the flat pitch caved in one bad winter, and I wanted it shallower cause I liked the look of it low. We were at each other over that for the better part of an evening, raised voices and all, the wife finally coming out and telling the pair of us to give it a rest before the neighbours thought we were killing one another. We split the difference in the end, went with something between the two, and it's held fine through every winter since, though he still tells me he was right and I still tell him he wasn't.

The worst of it, though, was the back wall. Found out near month seven that the whole thing was out of square, something in how it got framed early on, before the second lot of hands come, and there was no fixing it partial, no shimming it straight. Had to be took right down to the studs and built again from nothing. That was a hard day, tearing down something we'd already stood and closed in and thought was done. Felt like starting the whole job over. But there was no way round it, you can't hang a door proper on a wall that's crooked, so down she come and up she went again, right this time, checked six ways before a nail went into it.

The small win, the one that kept us all going after that, was the day the frame finally stood whole, every wall up, every rafter set, and you could walk through the rooms and see the shape of the place for the first time, see where the kitchen window was gonna look out over the water and where the young ones' rooms would be. We stood in the yard that evening, the whole crowd of us who'd worked on it by then, and just looked at it a long while. Nobody said much. Didn't need to.

Last stretch, the final four nights, we were out there past duckish and well past dark, working under a rigged-up light off the truck battery, trying to get the trim done and the last of the shingling before the crowd who'd been helping had to get back to their own work. Cold them nights, hands half numb, but there's something about finishing in the dark with tired men that pulls ya together. We got her done the fourth night, near midnight, and stood there not saying nothing, just looking.

Day it stood finished proper, the whole family walked through it room by room like we'd never seen it, though we'd built every inch. The wife cried a small bit in the kitchen. I didn't tell nobody I near did too.

After, we sat down and tallied it up. Eleven months from the day we broke ground to the day the last nail went in. Six different people helped along the way, not counting me and the wife and the young ones handing up tools and holding boards. Three of them we asked, three just come cause that's what ya do round here when someone's building.

I stands in it now most evenings, just in the front room looking out, and I still can't hardly believe we done it ourselves, the whole of it, wrong turns and falls and rows and all. It's ours though, every crooked bit that got made straight again, and that's some good feeling, I don't mind telling ya.
