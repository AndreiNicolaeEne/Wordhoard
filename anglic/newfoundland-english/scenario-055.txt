Since ye missed the whole of it, my friend, I s'pose I better tell ye proper, start to finish, cause it was some day, I tell ye.

We was meant to be out of the old place by nine, van booked and all, but sure I never got to bed till after two, still taping up boxes and swearing at meself for leaving it so late, so nine came and went and I was still standing in me housecoat with a roll of tape in me hand and the kettle not even on. Didn't get moving proper till gone eleven. That's the first thing, right there, before we was even out the door good.

And then before we was out the door at all, the handle come clean off the good box, the one with all the glasses wrapped in the good towels, and down the stairs it went, bang bang bang, and I'm standing there at the top going oh me nerves, oh me nerves, thinking every glass in it was smashed to bits. Half of it was, too. Broken glass right across the bottom step and I hadn't even had a cup of tea yet.

So we're only after starting and already I'm thinking, this is going to be some kind of day.

Didn't ask a soul to come, but wasn't the neighbour from downstairs up on the step by half eleven with his own two hands free and a face on him like he wasn't going nowhere else.

The neighbour: Where ya to with all this, then? Ye didn't think ye was doing this on yer own, did ye.

Me: Sure I never even rung ye, b'y.

The neighbour: Didn't have to. Saw the van out front and the door open and I says to meself, that crowd's moving today and not a one of them's slept.

And that was the truth of it, and glad of him we was, cause he had the truck backed right up and was hauling before I even had me boots laced.

Now the thing that broke the worst of all, and I still can't hardly say it without me stomach turning, was the mother's good china cabinet mirror. Not the cabinet itself, that come through grand, but the mirror off the top of it, been in the family since before I was born, and my brother-in-law had it wrapped in a blanket going down the front steps and his foot went out from under him on the ice that was only just starting to form, and down it went, and the shout that went up out of him and out of me both you'd have heard it three streets over.

Brother-in-law: Oh no, no no no, b'y, no.

The mother, arriving in the door behind us: What was that. What in the name of God was that.

Nobody had to answer her. She seen it in his face.

And on top of that, there was a box gone missing entire, never did turn up, not that day, not the next week, not ever after. Small brown box, had all me grandmother's letters in it and a few bits of jewellery wasn't worth much to anyone but me. Loaded onto the truck same as everything else, I'd swear to it, and by the time we got to the new place it just wasn't there. Might be sitting in the back of somebody's van still, for all I knows. Might be at the dump. I still thinks on it now and then, wondering.

The worst of the argy-bargy come over the chesterfield. Big heavy thing, the mother's old one, solid as a boat, and there was four of us on it going up the front steps of the new place and not a one of us agreeing on how she should go.

The neighbour: Turn her on the end, b'y, on the end, or she's not going up at all.

Brother-in-law: If we turns her on the end she's coming down on somebody's foot, I'm telling ye now.

Me: Just lift, will ye, the both of ye, and stop yer arguing over it.

The mother, from the doorway, arms folded: Ye're going to put a hole clean through me wall before ye're done, the lot of ye.

We tried her every way there was. Straight up, on the end, sideways, tipped back. That stairwell was built for a person and nothing wider, and no matter how we turned that chesterfield she would not go through the door at the top, kept catching on the frame no matter what angle we had her at. In the end we had the door itself off its hinges and even then it was a near thing, four grown adults sweating and swearing and the mother standing there the whole time with her hand over her mouth.

And wasn't that about the time the weather turned on us and all. Been dry and grey all morning, and then round two it come in mauzy, that fine wet fog rolling up off the water, and by three it had turned to a proper drizzle that soaked clean through everything left out on the step, and the ice on the steps that had already had my brother-in-law over once got worse again with it, silver thaw starting to form right there on the rail.

By then not a one of us had ate a thing since the night before, and I don't know what we'd have done only the neighbour's wife come by with a tray, toutons still hot and a pot of tea, and we stood right there in among the boxes eating off paper towel, no table set up yet, nowhere to sit, just standing in the hallway with the fog coming in the open door behind us and them toutons the best thing I ever put in me mouth I do believe. Saved the whole afternoon, that did. Don't know what state we'd have finished in without it.

The last few loads near killed us. Every box after that felt like it weighed twice what it did that morning, and I was after losing all feeling in me arms be the end of it, just going through the motions, up and down, up and down, not even talking anymore, just grunting where's this one going and putting it wherever looked closest.

Before we locked up the old place for the last time I walked through it on me own, every room empty, just marks on the walls where the pictures used to hang and dust in the corners nobody bothers to see till the furniture's gone. Stood in the kitchen a minute. Some strange, that, walking through a place ye lived in for years with not a stick left in it. Quiet as anything. I turned the key in the door and that was that, done.

We got into the new place well after dark, duckish long gone, the streetlights all on, and half the boxes still sitting in the back of somebody's truck somewhere between the two houses for all I knew. No mirror, no small brown box, glasses smashed, and every one of us wet through and wrecked entire. But we was in. Sat down right on the floor, the whole crowd of us, too tired to even put the kettle on, and I says to the mother, well, we're here anyway, and she says back to me, some day that was, and I says, ye're not wrong there, b'y, ye're not wrong there at all.
