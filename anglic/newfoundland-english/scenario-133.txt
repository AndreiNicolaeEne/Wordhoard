Well b'y, I got to tell ye about the garden this year, start to finish, cause it were some year for it, up and down like everything else in this place.

I signs up for the plot back in the winter when the community list goes round, and soon as March turns over I'm down there most evenings after work just standing in it, foot in the mud, trying to figure what I'm at. I loves a feed of vegetables same as anyone but ye can't just fling seed at the ground and hope, so I settles on the usual crowd in the end: potatoes cause what's a Newfoundlander without a potato, carrots, turnip, some cabbage for the Jiggs dinner come fall, and I puts in a row of peas along the fence for the young ones to pick at when they're by. Buddy two plots over does nothing but flowers and herbs, fair enough, each to their own, but I'm after telling him more than once, ye can't eat a marigold.

I waits on the ground same as everyone does, cause ye put things in too early round here and a late frost'll take the lot of it, no odds how warm one Tuesday felt. So it's the third week of May before I'm out with the fork, a few weeks after the last cold snap broke, and once it's in, glory be, we gets a run of weather that's right decent for once, sun most days, warm nights, and everything comes up some fast. The peas were up out of the ground inside the week. I says to meself, now this is the year, this is the one where I gets it all right.

Course it never lasts. Not two weeks into June and I'm down looking at the cabbage and there's these little green fellas all over the leaves, chewed to lace, and I knows right off it's the cabbage worm, everybody round the plots has had a go with them one time or another. I tries the soap spray first, mix it up in a bottle and go at it of an evening, and it does knock some of them back, I won't say it done nothing, but it never cleared them out proper, I were still picking the odd one off by hand right through the summer. Some crowd swears by the row covers but I never got round to it and paid for that a little.

Then July comes and it goes dry, dry like I never seen it, week after week with no rain worth speaking of, and I'm down there every evening with the watering can and the hose reel, hauling water by hand for what felt like a month straight, cause the tap's only the one for the whole lot of plots and ye can't leave a hose running unattended or ye'll hear about it. My arms were sore from it, honest, up and down the rows with two cans at a time, duckish light most evenings before I'd get the last of it done.

And that's where the trouble starts with the water, cause there's a sign-up sheet for the tap, half-hour slots, and buddy next to me, he's some particular about his time, and one evening I'm running a bit over cause the turnip bed took longer than I figured, and don't he come round the corner.

The neighbour: Where ya to with that hose, b'y, that's my time gone by now.

Me: I'm just finishing up the one bed, give me two shakes.

The neighbour: Two shakes were twenty minutes ago. I got me own stuff dying over here waiting on ye.

Me: Go way b'y, it's not dying, it rained Tuesday.

The neighbour: Tuesday were nothing, a spit of rain, sure the ground's like a biscuit.

It went on a bit longer than that, the both of us right cross by the end of it, and I'll admit I were some sleeveen about the sign-up sheet meself, always finding an excuse to run over. But we settles it in the end sensible enough, sat down at the picnic table by the shed with the fella that runs the association and we splits the evening slots proper, twenty-five minutes each with five minutes between to switch over, and puts a little timer out there so nobody's arguing over whose watch is right. Once that were sorted we were grand, even had a mug-up together after, tea and a bit of toutons someone brought down, and no hard feelings kept.

Now the potatoes, b'y, the potatoes were the story of the year. I don't know what I done different but they come in shockin heavy, dug the first hill in August just to see and there were more spuds under that one plant than I knew what to do with. Right good year for it all round I heard, but mine done exceptional, I'm not ashamed to say it. Meanwhile the carrots were a different tale entirely, rot got into a good third of them down in the ground, some kind of soft rot from all that watering I imagine, pulled them up in September and half were gone to mush right at the root end. Some disappointing after all that hauling water to keep them going.

Then the storm comes through, must've been early September, one of them ones that blows in off the water and just levels everything that's standing tall. Went down the next morning and the pea vines were flat on the ground, the whole fence row of them, and a couple rows of the turnip tops all bent over and half buried in mud where the water run through. Spent the better part of a Saturday out there with buddy from the plot next door, the both of us staking the peas back up and propping what we could, hands black with dirt the whole day, but we saved most of it in the end.

By the time October come round and I'm pulling the last of it in, I sat down and had a proper tally. Potatoes were the big win, more than the family could get through, so I traded a good sack of them to a woman down the row for a run of her rhubarb, cause I hadn't put in any this year and the young ones do love a rhubarb crisp. Cabbage come through alright despite the worms, turnip and carrots were middling on account of the rot, and the peas that got flattened still give a decent last picking once they were staked back up.

Put the whole plot to bed the last weekend of October, turned it all over, laid a bit of seaweed and old leaves across the top of it for the winter, cleaned the tools off and locked the shed. Standing there looking at it, all bare and waiting, I'd call it a right good year on the whole, more wins than losses by a fair margin, and any year ye come out of it still on speaking terms with the fella next door, I'd call that some kind of a win an all.
