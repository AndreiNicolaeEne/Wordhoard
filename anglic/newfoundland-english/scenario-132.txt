Eleven days, b'y, straight through, no let-up at all, and it started worse than anyone was after callin for. First morning I goes out to the woodpile and the air just takes the breath clean out of ye, and I says to meself, this en't right for this time of year at all. We had the stove going round the clock the whole stretch, up every few hours stokin her, and still the front room never held past cool. Me hands was cracked to bits from splittin so much.

The water line to the shed froze solid on the third day, first time ever, and that line's been there longer than I'm alive. Buddy from next door come over with a torch to help thaw the pipe and even he said he never seen it go that hard.

The eldest sat by the stove most days and wouldn't hear a word about it bein the worst.

The eldest: I mind the winter forty-some year back, when the tickle froze right across and ye could walk it. Cold enough that year, some cold.

Then round day nine she says different.

The eldest: No, b'y. This one's after beatin that one. Never seen the like.

Worst night was the ninth, the wind come up off the water and the windows had ice thick as your thumb inside the glass, not out. Lost the hens that night, every one of them, found them stiff in the mornin, and that's a loss ye feel.

Then the eleventh day the wind swung round soft and mauzy, the whole yard drippin, silver thaw sliddin off the wires, and I stood out in it like a fool just lettin it run down me neck.
