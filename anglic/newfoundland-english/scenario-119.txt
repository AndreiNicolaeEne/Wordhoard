Luh, sit down here a minute, I got to tell you this one, you never saw a second of it and it's some story.

So this was over to the house, a Friday night, a bit of a scoff after and the cards come out after that, same as always, forty-fives round the kitchen table, tea gone cold in the cups and somebody's rum going round besides. And I'm playing against my friend, the one that's always so sure of himself, and it starts as nothing, just a joke really. He goes, "I'm after winning the last three hands off ya, you're some sook tonight," and I says back, "Go way b'y, you wouldn't win off me if I dealt with me eyes shut." And that's all it takes, that's the whole spark of it.

The eldest at the table, she's the one that gets it going proper. She goes, "Well now, why don't ye put something on it so." And my friend, cocky as anything, says sure, fine, a fiver on the next hand. And I'm some confident, I says done, easy money. But the rest of them, the whole crowd standing round with their tea and their mugs, they weren't havin' it be a fiver.

His workmate goes, "A fiver, is that all ye got in ya? Make it worth watchin'." And somebody else, right at the counter with the kettle still in her hand, she goes, "Twenty. Twenty or don't bother." And the crowd's roaring laughing and egging it on, and my friend, he's some cocky still, he goes, "Twenty, fine, sure, easy," but I could see it, he wasn't half as sure as his mouth was. Twenty dollars, mind, not enough to sting nobody, just enough to make a hand of cards mean something. Twenty it was, agreed before a card even got turned over.

Now the game itself, b'y, back and forth it went for the longest time. I'm reneging on him, he's reneging on me, the crib board getting knocked half off the table twice from someone leaning in too hard to watch. Neither one of us clear ahead, hand after hand, and the room's gone dead quiet every time someone turns a card, then some roar of noise after. And then it turns. It turned hard. He needed the five of hearts bad, dead needed it, and I had it sat right in me hand the whole time and never let on. And I could see the exact second he knew he was beat, before the hand was even played out, his face just went. And when it was over, well and truly over, no counting left to do, he just put his cards down flat and groaned, this long groan, and says, "Yes b'y, that's it, I'm beat, I'm after losing me own money on me own big mouth."

Now here's the turn in it. Twenty dollars was owed, plain as anything, everybody heard it agreed. But the crowd wasn't havin' that either.

The host goes, "No no, we're not takin' his twenty dollars, sure that's no good to nobody. He's doing something for it."

And that set the whole table off, everyone talking over everyone else about what he'd have to do, and it didn't take them long to land on it exact, nothing left vague about it at all. He had to put on the host's mother's ol' fur hat and her rubber boots, stand up on the kitchen table itself, and sing "I'se the B'y" straight through, every verse, doing the actions for every line of it, cuttin' the wood and haulin' the boat and all, no skipping a verse, and the youngster of the house was to stand up alongside him conducting with a wooden spoon like he was the front of a big band. And it wasn't to be done quiet in the corner either, it had to happen proper, in front of the whole crowd, everyone that was there that night and whoever else wanted to come see it.

He tried to get out of it, mind you. He goes, "Sure look, I'll just give ye the twenty, that's grand, that settles it fair," and the whole room went up laughing at him for even trying it.

The workmate goes, "Not a chance, b'y. You're doin' it. You agreed to the terms, you're doin' the terms."

The eldest goes, "The twenty's off the table now, my love. This is better."

So they set the day and the place right there, the following Saturday, back at the same house, everyone told to come round for it, no excuses. And come Saturday it happened, and it happened proper, the whole crowd packed into that little kitchen, standing on chairs, some of them out in the porch trying to see over each other's shoulders.

Up he gets on the table in the fur hat, looking like he's after robbing some old woman's closet, and the boots up to his knees, and the youngster stood there with the spoon like a little conductor, dead serious about it. And he starts in singing, "I'se the b'y that builds the boat," and doing the actions along with it, sawing away at nothing, hauling an invisible rope, the whole crowd roaring with him from the first line.

But the moment, the one that still gets brought up every single time, he gets to the line about digging the pit and every mother's son wanting more, and he goes to give a big spin on it, real dramatic, and his boot catches the edge of the tablecloth, and down comes the whole spread, the plate of toutons and the tin of scrunchions and all, right into the dog's lap under the table. And that dog didn't hesitate one second, just went straight into it like he'd been waiting his whole life for that exact moment. And here's my friend still up on the table in the fur hat and the boots, mid-verse, mid-spin, and he just carries on singing right through it like nothing happened, not missing a word, while the whole room's gone to pieces laughing and the dog's down there working through scrunchions like it's Christmas.

That was it, that was the moment. Half the crowd couldn't breathe right for a full minute after.

And that's the thing, that story's outlived the whole bet by miles now. Whenever the crowd's together since, someone always brings it up, always, and every time it's told it's got a bit more to it than the last time, the hat's gotten bigger, the spin's gotten wilder, the dog's near got a speaking part in it now. Twenty dollars nobody even remembers who owed who anymore. But that dog and them scrunchions, b'y, that's forever.
