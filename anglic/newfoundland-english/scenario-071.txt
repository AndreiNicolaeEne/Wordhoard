Where was I to, is that what you're askin? Sit down by me a minute, love, I'm wrecked. No, don't be gettin up again, just sit, I wants to tell ya about the street the day, because you wasn't home for it and I don't think ye'd believe me otherwise.

It started with the roof. You knows there was that silver thaw the night before, the wires all glazed up like glass, and the mornin come in some mauzy, thick fog you could hardly see the pole at the end of the street through, and then the snow come down right on top of it all, thick and wet, the worst kind for weight. By eight o'clock the roof over the widow's back porch was groanin under it something shockin, you could hear it out on the road, and everyone knew if that let go it was takin the whole back step and half her fence along with it, and her livin there on her own with nobody young enough in the house to get up and do a thing about it.

It was the fella who ploughs for the top of the street noticed it first, out clearin his own drive at some ungodly hour, and he come bangin on doors up and down.

The one behind the door: "Whadda y'at, what's all the racket for?"

The one ploughing: "Get yer boots on, b'y, we needs hands out here, the widow's roof is after gettin too heavy to hold."

That's all it took. Word went up the street faster than the snow itself, one door to the next, youngsters sent runnin in their boots not even laced right, and inside of twenty minutes there was people comin out of houses I didn't even know had people livin in them. We figures maybe eight or ten'd turn out, that's what I says to meself standin there in me boots, but b'y, by the time the shovels was really goin there was near thirty of us on the go, more than twenty past what anybody counted on. Cars pullin in from the next street over even, and buddy from round the corner showin up with chummy, that big blower of his, roarin away like he'd been waitin all week for the excuse.

The work sorted itself out quick enough, nobody stood round askin what to do. The men took the roof and the big drifts, the women had the walks and the mailboxes goin, and buddy from the corner house, the big fella built like the side of a shed, he took on the heaviest of it, up the ladder with the roof rake pullin that ice and snow off in great slabs, hour after hour, never once come down for a mug-up till it was near done. Some job that was, right shockin heavy, and him not stoppin the whole while.

Course it never all goes smooth. Partway through, a whole slab of it was after comin loose before anybody give the word. "Luh!" somebody hollered, and down it come off the eaves in one go, took the handrail clean off her back steps, near had the young fella below it too, he jumped clear just in time. Oh me nerves, I thought we was gonna have an ambulance out on top of everything else. No harm done in the end, just the rail busted and everyone's heart in their mouth for a minute.

That's when the argument started, mind ya. Buddy on the ladder wanted to keep rakin from the top down same as before, and the lad holdin the bottom of it was after sayin that was madness with slabs comin off like that, that they should be clearin from underneath instead and lettin it come down easy, not pullin great chunks loose all at once.

The lad holding the ladder: "You'll have the whole works down on somebody's head, b'y, is that what you wants?"

Buddy on the roof: "And what, we leaves it and it comes down on its own in the middle of the night and takes the fence with it? Go way b'y, that's worse."

Went back and forth some good while, the pair of them, till the widow herself come out on what was left of her steps and told the both of them to quit their arguin and get on with it whichever way, she didn't care so long as it come off, and that settled it quick enough. They found a middle way after, slower, safer, and no more slabs come down the rest of the day.

All through it there was food comin out of houses, one after the other. The woman two doors up had a griddle goin in her porch, toutons fryin, and sent her youngster round with a plate for whoever's hands was closest. The young fella below, mind, he's a right sleeveen when there's toutons on the go, first in line every time before the plate's even set down proper. Somebody else had tea and buns out on a tray, and later on the missus up top brought out a pot of fish and brewis with the scrunchions done proper, real fish, cod she'd put by back in the fall, not none of that other stuff off the shelf. There was a flask goin round among the men too, quiet like, for the cold, and nobody sayin no to a drop of it.

The youngsters had their own little job, mind, clearin the path down to the mailboxes and keepin the steps salted so nobody'd go over, and they took it dead serious, marchin up and down with their little shovels like it was the most important job on the street, which I s'pose to them it was.

And the old fella at the end, must be near ninety now, he wasn't havin none of it, sittin inside while the street worked. Come out on his cane and stood right in the middle of it, givin orders nobody asked for.

The old fella at the end of the street: "I'm not sittin in there like some angishore watchin out the window while ye does all the work. Somebody give me a job."

So they made him foreman, more or less, had him standin by the gate tellin fellas where to pile the snow so it didn't block nobody's step, and he loved it, stood out there the whole day in his big coat, wouldn't come in for nothin.

The one moment everybody went at once was when the dog got loose, the black one from the house with the blue door, went tearin straight into the biggest pile we'd made and disappeared right into it, then come bustin back out white all over, lookin like he'd been rolled in flour, shakin himself off and prancin round like he'd won somethin. Every last one of us, shovels down, laughin so hard we couldn't hardly stand up, even the old fella at the gate, even the widow on her steps with her hand over her mouth. Went on for a good five minutes, that dog struttin round like the hero of the day.

We figured the whole thing'd be done by dinner time, but b'y, it went on and on, one thing after the other, and we was after finishin a good three hours past what anybody figured before the last shovel come down, near duckish by then, the light goin grey and the street lamps just startin to come on. But when we counted it up, every drive on the street cleared, every walk done both sides, that roof stripped right back to safe, the rail already half fixed back on, and near two dozen cars dug out that would've sat buried till the weekend otherwise. Somebody let a cheer go up when the last of the roof snow hit the ground and didn't take nothin with it, and it went right down the street, one house after the other joinin in.

And after, love, the street just felt different. People sittin out on their steps in the cold still, not wantin to go in yet, everybody wavin at everybody, porch lights on all down the road. I don't know how long that'll last, a week maybe, but the day, for a few hours anyway, it was like the whole street was one house.
