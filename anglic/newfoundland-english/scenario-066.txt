Whadda y'at, come on in out of it, sit down, I'll put the kettle on. You wants to know about chummy, do ya. Ha, well, luh, get comfortable, this is a bit of a tale and I'm not one to hurry through it.

It started back around Tibb's Eve, before the mummers were even out yet. The young one from up the street, she come in from playing and says to her mother she seen a fox, big as a dog, going in behind the Andersons' shed. Well, 'twas a mauzy old evening, duckish already, and nobody thought a thing of it. Sure, youngsters are always seeing something in that kind of light, and the mother only laughed and told her she was after imagining it, go on and wash up for supper. That was the end of it, or so we thought.

Weren't but a week or two after when the woman up over seen it, clear as day, broad daylight, crossing right over the road by the mailboxes. Now she's not one for makings things up, that one, she don't tell a lie in her life, so once she come out with it, the whole street sat up and took notice. Right there, she says, plain as anything, trotting along like he owned the place, not a bother on him.

After that 'twas one thing after another. He'd be into the bins of a night, tipping them right over, garbage strewed halfway down the road by morning. Into the gardens too, digging round the carrots and after the strawberries, and somebody's flower bed got dug clean up looking for grubs I s'pose. So the street split itself right down the middle over him. Some crowd sayin leave the poor divil be, he was here long before we put up half these houses, he's got to eat same as anybody. Other crowd sayin no sir, he's got no business round here at all, he'll have somebody's cat, or worse, some youngster's fingers, and something's got to be done.

Then didn't the tomcat from two doors down go missing. Gone three days, and right away 'twas chummy got the blame, though not a soul saw the like of it happen. No proof a-tall, just talk, but talk was enough. That done it. There was a bit of a gathering called, right out on the corner one evening, half the street there with their tea gone cold in the mugs.

The one wanted him dealt with: Sure b'y, we can't be having that, next thing 'twill be somebody's dog, or a child, and then where'll we be.

The one wanted him left alone: Go way b'y, he never touched that cat, that cat was old and half blind, prob'ly wandered off and died somewhere quiet. You're blaming the poor animal for nothing but bad luck.

The one wanted him dealt with: Bad luck is it. Oh me nerves, you'd say that till it's your own bin tore open of a morning.

Voices went up something shockin that night, real heated, the two camps hollering back and forth till somebody's husband had to step between them. Nothing got settled, only more mad at each other than before.

Round that same time, one household took it on themselves to sort him out. Borrowed a live trap off someone, baited it up with a bit of fish, set it out by the shed thinking they'd catch him and bring him off somewhere far. Well. He'd have the bait gone and the trap still set, three nights running, sleeveen that he is, too smart for the lot of it. Then didn't they leave the shed door open by accident chasing after him one night, and he got in and made a right mess of the tools and whatnot in there. Angishore state that shed was in after. That was the end of anybody trying that on their own.

Then come the night that really put the fear in people. The fella from the end house, he'd gone out late to put something in his bin, no light on, and chummy was right there behind it, not two feet off him. Let out this screech, this awful high scream, right in the dark, and the poor fella nearly went down the steps backwards he was that startled. He come in shakin, said he never heard the like of it in his life, sounded like something being murdered. That's the fox scream, mind, they do that, but nobody told him that beforehand, so he was some rattled.

After that, even the ones on the leave-him-be side went a bit quiet, and the argument came right back to a boil, worse than before.

The one wanted him gone: There, now. That's what I'm on about. That's not natural, screaming like that in the dark. That thing is dangerous.

The one wanted him left alone: He's not dangerous, he's only unlucky where he wandered to, is all. Foxes been round these parts longer than the road was paved. He's only doing what he does, and doing it too close to people, that's the whole trouble.

The one wanted him gone: Unlucky or not, I don't want him doing it two feet from my back step.

Nobody could agree, so in the end somebody had the sense to ring up a fella who used to trap up in the country, knew animals proper, and asked him down to have a look. He come round of an afternoon, walked the whole street, looked at the tore-up shed, looked at where the bins got into, even found the den he was using under an old woodpile at the end of the road.

He told us straight, no rabies on him, no sign of sickness a-tall, coat was good, eyes were clear, he was only a healthy fox living close to easy food because we made it easy for him. Stop leaving bins loose, stop leaving scraps out, don't go feeding him for the sake of it, and he'll keep his distance same as any wild thing does when there's nothing in it for him. Leave him be otherwise, he said, he's not after anybody, he's after garbage.

That settled it, more or less. The terms the street come to: bins get strapped down or brought in at night, no more leaving scraps out for him even by the ones who liked having him around, and everybody keeps their eyes open, especially where the young ones play after duckish.

And that's about the shape of it since. Bins is all secured now, bungee cords and the like, you'll see them on every step round here. The old fellow across the road keeps half an eye out of his window most evenings, more out of habit now than worry, tells you if he's seen him about. Sightings still happen now and again, somebody'll see him trotting the tickle path down by the water of an early morning, but nobody thinks much of it these days. The alarm's long gone out of it.

Now it's more a story we tell than anything else, comes up over a mug-up or a scoff same as any other bit of foolishness that's happened on this street. Somebody'll bring him up at Jiggs dinner of a Sunday, and there's always somebody adds a bit more to the tale than was really there, the way these things go. But that's chummy for ya. Right character round here now, whether he knows it or not.

Anyway, that's the kettle boiled. Sit up, love, I'll get ya a cup.
