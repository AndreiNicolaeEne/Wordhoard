Where ya to? Sit in by the stove there and I'll give ye the whole of it, start to finish, the way me own mother give it to me, and her mother before that.

We come off a scrap of land in the south of Ireland, the way it was told, where there wasn't nothing but rock and rain and too many mouths for what the ground would give ye. The landlord back home was a right sleeveen, would skin ye for the rent whether the crop come in or not, and when the potatoes failed again there was no more staying for it, none at all. Wasn't no angishore in the family, mind, just poor the ordinary way, but ordinary poor with the potatoes gone is still starving.

The great-grandfather was the one went first. All on his own, no wife nor youngsters with him yet, just himself and a bag he could carry and whatever bit of money the family scraped between them. He used to say after, and it come down mother to daughter to me:

The great-grandfather: I never had e'er a soul aboard that boat I knowed before I stepped on, and I never had e'er a soul when I stepped off neither.

That's the whole of it, right there, some lonesome thing to carry. The crossing was a shockin hard go, weeks of it, packed in below with people retching sick the whole way, no proper food, and storms that had her pitchin so bad ye thought she'd go under ye for sure. There was a cousin of his aboard too, a young one, and that cousin never come off alive, took sick partway over and was buried off the side, and that's the only way it's ever spoke of in the family since, no name to it at all, just "the cousin that was lost on the crossing," like the name itself got left behind with him in the water.

He landed with next to nothing, not enough in his pockets for a scoff let alone a roof. No work waiting, no house, nothing arranged, just another fella off the boat same as fifty more that season. For the first while he boarded in with another family down by the harbour, a crowd that took him in on account of knowing some buddy who knowed some buddy back home, and he slept in with their sons and ate at their table and give what little he could toward it when he had it. Wasn't his own, mind, not a stick of it, only borrowed shelter till he could stand on his own two feet.

Work was hard got at first. A day here loading whatever chummy come off the boats down the wharf, a day there splitting wood for some crowd needed a hand, whatever come along and nothing steady in it at all. He'd be down before the light was hardly up hoping for a call, and some days there was nothing and he'd go back with empty hands and go again the next morning the same. It was near two year before he had it steady enough, and enough put by besides, to think on sending for the rest.

When the letter finally went back it was to the grandmother, his wife by then, and the children they already had between them, and it took every cent he'd saved and borrowed besides to bring them over. Two year and a bit, near enough, from the time he stepped off alone to the time she stepped off with the youngsters hanging off her skirts. She used to tell it that she never thought she'd see land again neither, and that when she seen the harbour coming up out of the mauzy she thought she was dreaming it entire.

The grandmother: Go way b'y, I'm after telling ye a hundred times, when I seen him standing there on the wharf I hardly knowed him, he'd gone that thin and grey off the worry of it. Oh me nerves, the state of the both of us that day.

There's one thing come over with her that's kept in the family the day still, handed down mother to daughter, a little cross, nothing grand, just a plain bit of metal on a bit of chain that come off the old place, and she had it round her neck the whole crossing and never took it off, not once. It's in a drawer this very minute, wrapped in a bit of cloth, and every so often it comes out and gets shown round and put away again careful as anything.

Once they were all here and settled the great-grandfather took up fishing for good, same as half the harbour, and stuck with it the rest of his days, went out after cod, for that's the only fish worth the name round here whatever else swims in the water. He knowed the tickle like the back of his hand, knowed when the weather was coming on bad off the way the sky went, knowed a silver thaw was coming on the wires before it ever showed itself. They found a little place to live within the first few years, nothing much, but their own door to shut, up off the harbour a ways, and that's where the children come, the ones what were born right here, not carried over on any boat, that never knowed anywhere else as home. Luh, that's the whole of it right there: it wasn't the getting here that made it home at all, it was them young ones being born right in it, so there was nowhere else left for anyone to be from anymore.

Come Christmas the whole crowd of them kept up the old ways some, mummers going round house to house, and Tibb's Eve the night before it to get the excuse for a drop early, and a scoff after, fish and brewis with the scrunchions on it, toutons going too if there was time, and a Jiggs dinner of a Sunday regular as church. A boil-up down the shore in the summer, a mug-up whenever anyone come by the door, and none of that ever changed from that day to this, not a bit of it.

Me own mother told me all this the same as I'm telling it to ye now, sitting by the stove of a duckish evening with the wind going round the house something shockin, and I used to sit there some quiet, hardly breathing, waiting on the part about the cousin and the part about the cross, and she'd always finish the same:

Mother: We never had much when we come, b'y, but we had each other in the end, and that's the whole of what mattered. Yes b'y, that's the whole of it.

And now I got youngsters of me own asking after it, and I tells it to them the same way, near enough word for word, sitting them down of an evening and going through it slow, the old place, and the one who went first alone, and the hard road over, and the cousin with no name, and the little cross still in the drawer the day. Some day they'll be telling it to their own, I s'pose, and it'll go on some way or other, long after all of us that lived it first is gone off the wharf for good.
