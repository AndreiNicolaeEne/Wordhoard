Up before the light, that's how it always goes with the garden day, and I'm after telling meself the night before I'd sleep in, and sure I never. The father was already below in the kitchen banging the kettle around when I come down, and he says, go on and get the youngest up, we're losing the morning standing here talking about it. No mauzy this morning, thank God, just that grey before-light with the frost still sitting white on the fence, and you could see your breath going up like smoke off a chimney.

Father: Right, we got the whole top bed and the two turnip rows and if the weather holds we'll get the cabbage in too. Everyone knows their job or do I got to be telling ye twice.

The youngest one got sent down first to gather the buckets and the burlap sacks out of the shed, on account of she's small enough for the shed but too small yet for the fork work, and she never mided a bit, tearing off down the garden path with the buckets banging her legs the whole way, delighted with herself to be first out. That's the easy job, always is, but nobody says it to her, you lets her have it.

The heavy end of it fell to my uncle, like it does every year, on account of he's built like the side of a shed himself. He took the fork to the first potato row before the rest of us even had our boots laced, turning the ground over in these long steady heaves, and the mother stood there a second just watching him go.

Mother: He's some strong, that one. I don't know where he puts it all.

Uncle: Sure somebody's got to do it, b'y. Ye all stand round admiring while I does me back in.

But he wasn't cross, he never is, that's just the way of him, giving out while he works twice as hard as anyone else. We come along behind him picking the spuds up out of the turned dirt into the buckets, and the youngest one hauling the full ones back up to the sacks by the fence, proud as anything every time.

Once that top bed was cleared we all shifted down to the turnip rows, and that's heavier digging again, the ground packed harder down that end, so my uncle stayed on the fork and the rest of us swapped round, some of us pulling and some of us knocking the dirt off before they went in the sack. The middle one was working steady enough at the start but by mid-morning I noticed her falling behind the row, not saying nothing, just going slower and slower with her hands going red and stiff from the cold dirt.

Me: Ye alright there?

Middle one: Yeah, I'm alright, just me hands is froze.

Nobody made a thing of it. The father just quiet come round the far side and started pulling on her row without a word about it, working it in alongside his own, so by the time we all met in the middle you'd never know she'd been behind at all. That's just how it's done, you don't be blaming a person for going slow, you just come in and even it out.

Round midday the mother come down the path with the big basket covered over with a tea towel, and we all straightened up glad enough for it, backs cracking. Toutons, still warm, and a thermos of tea strong enough to stand a spoon in, and a bit of bread and jam besides. We sat up on the fence rail and ate, and my uncle ate about four toutons to everyone else's one.

Uncle: A man works like I works, a man eats like I eats.

Mother: Go way b'y, you'll eat us out of house before the cabbage is even up.

There was a bit of an argument going then over how to pull the cabbage, whether you twists it or cuts it clean with the knife, the father saying twisting saves the knife edge and my uncle saying twisting near takes your arm off if the head's a big one, and it went back and forth a good while, nobody giving an inch, until the mother told the both of them to shut up and eat their tea before it went stone cold, and that was the end of that.

We were about to get back at it when the father looked off west and didn't like the look of it one bit.

Father: Luh at that coming in over the hill there. That's not mauzy, that's rain, and it's not far off neither.

So the plan changed right quick, we left the cabbage for after and all piled onto the turnip to get what we could of it in before anything come down, working faster now, less talking, everyone just putting the head down and going. And sure enough it spat a bit of rain around one o'clock, just enough to make everyone shockin nervous, but it held off from anything worse and by half two the sky had cleared right off to blue, and the sun come out hot on our backs so we were peeling off jackets left and right.

That's when the fork went. My uncle brought it down hard into what he thought was clear ground and there was this crack, and the handle split clean in two, and he stood there holding half of it looking like he'd lost his best friend.

Uncle: Well now. That's her finished for the day.

Father: We got the spare below in the shed, go on and get it, b'y, we're not stopping for a broke fork.

So the youngest was sent running again, delighted to have a job, and back she come with the spare and we never lost but ten minutes over the whole thing, my uncle grumbling about the price of a good fork the whole rest of the afternoon like it was somebody's fault.

By the time we got into the cabbage the light was starting to go, that duckish grey creeping in early like it does once the year turns, and the father come down the row calling out to pick it up now, we want this last bit in before dark or we'll be out here with the lamps.

Word went round and everyone moved quicker, no more standing about, and for the last stretch we all ended up working the same row together, the whole crowd of us bent down side by side getting the last of the cabbage heads cut and stacked, laughing now because we were nearly done and you could feel it.

We tallied it all up back at the shed by the last of the light, near on twelve full sacks of potato, six of turnip, and two crates of cabbage besides, and the father stood there looking at it all with his hands on his hips, right pleased, saying that'd see us well through the winter and then some.

And then it hit everyone at once how done in we were, the whole crowd of us just sitting down wherever we stood, backs against the sacks, not a word said for a good while, just everyone's legs gone to jelly and glad of it.

Father: Right, before anyone gets too comfortable, somebody's got to run the milk jug back up before the shop closes.

Youngest: Not me, I'm after doing everything the day already.

Father: Go on, love, you're the only one still got legs under ye.

And off she went again, grumbling the whole way up the path, while the rest of us sat there in the last of the light not moving an inch.
