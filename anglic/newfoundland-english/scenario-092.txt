The house had thinned right out by then, down to the five of them round the kitchen table, the front room gone dark and the door shut on the last of the crowd gone home near an hour before. The teapot sat cold in the middle of the table and nobody had the heart yet to get up and put the kettle on again. The fish and brewis from earlier was still out on the counter, the scrunchions gone cold and greasy in the pan, and nobody was touching it either.

The eldest was picking at a bit of fruitcake off her plate, not eating it, only turning it over with the fork.

The eldest: Do ye remember he'd never go to bed without checking that stove, even in July, not a fire lit in it for months and still down on his knees checking the damper. I used to say to him, sure b'y, what odds is it, there's nothing in there to burn, and he'd only look at me like I was some foolish for even asking.

The aunt: 'Twasn't July he started that, maid, that was after the chimney fire below at the old place, the winter you were only small. You've always got that one wrong.

The eldest: (laughing) Go way, is that right. I tells it wrong every time then, don't I, every single time, and I never once catches meself.

The husband let out a laugh he clearly never meant to, short and quick, and put his hand up over his mouth after like he could take it back. Nobody minded it a bit. The mother only reached over and patted his arm without looking up.

The mother: Sure let it out, don't mind us, love.

A little quiet after that, just the clock going in the hall. Then the mother spoke again, quieter.

The mother: He'd say the same thing every time one of ye came in with some big plan or other. Now hold on now, hold on, he'd say, hands up like that, hold on now till I hears the whole of it. I can hear him saying it this minute.

The youngest: (saying it right along with her, the exact same way) Now hold on now, hold on.

The mother: That's him exactly. God, that's him to the life.

They sat with that a minute, smiling at it, and then the aunt looked round at the rest of them like she was deciding whether to say a thing at all.

The aunt: I don't suppose any of ye ever heard he went out mummering one Tibb's Eve, years before he married your mother, dressed up in an old dress and a pillowcase over his head, all to work up the nerve to go inside a house he'd never have gone into on his own two feet in daylight. He told me that years after, swore me to say nothing.

The youngest: Oh me nerves, I never knew that in me life.

The room went quiet again for a second, everybody looking at the mother, who only smiled down at the table and said nothing to it, and the quiet held a moment longer before the mother stood up sudden.

The mother: I'm after letting that tea go stone cold on all of us. Pass your cups over, and somebody get into that fish and brewis, no sense letting it sit there going to waste.

There was a bit of shuffling then, cups going round, a plate of the toutons coming out from the warming oven where they'd been keeping, and for a minute the talk broke into smaller pieces, who wanted sugar, who was after having enough.

The eldest: You never did pay me back for minding the youngsters that whole week, you knows, back when Mother was in hospital.

The aunt: Lord, are we back on that again the night of all nights.

The eldest: I'm only saying it, not pressing it.

The aunt: Well don't, not tonight.

And it was let go, the two of them not looking at each other for a second and then looking anyway, and it passed like it never happened, the way it always did with them.

The husband, quiet till then, spoke up with something of his own.

The husband: The stubbornest I ever seen a man be over nothing at all was that time buddy came round selling the outboard motor, some sleeveen off the shore road, and he wanted forty for it and your father offered thirty-eight, and buddy wouldn't budge and neither would he. Stood out there in the mauzy for the better part of an hour, right down to duckish, the pair of them, over two dollars. Two dollars, and he'd have paid four hundred easy for the boat itself and never blinked.

The youngest: And did he get it in the end.

The husband: He did not. Walked off and left it, and went and bought a worse one off someone else the next week for forty-five, just so as not to give buddy the satisfaction.

That got a real laugh out of all of them, even the mother, a proper laugh with no edge left in it at all, only fondness, and nobody said the word for what it was, but they all knew it the same, that was him, that was the whole of him in one story, the man who'd cut his own nose off before he'd let a sleeveen win two dollars, and every one of them loved him some fierce for it.

It was in the middle of that laughing the mother went quiet, and when the eldest looked over her eyes were wet, spilling quiet down without a sound to it, no shaking, no fuss made of it. The eldest reached and put a hand over hers on the table, and the aunt kept right on talking, easy, not stopping for it, not making the mother into the centre of the room for crying, just letting her sit there in it while the talk moved gentle around her like water round a rock.

The aunt: He was some proud of that boat after, mind, sleeveen or no sleeveen.

The husband: Prouder of the story than the boat, I'd say.

After a while it turned to practical things, the way it does, someone always the one to say it plain.

The eldest: I'll ring the funeral home the day about the flowers, and somebody's after needing to tell the priest what he wants read.

The husband: I got the days off already, I'll drive whoever needs driving.

The aunt: I'll do the sandwiches for after, same as I always does.

The youngest: I'll see to the chairs coming back to the hall.

Plain as that, no ceremony to it at all, said and done and moved past.

It got on late after, the aunt gone up to lie down, the eldest and her husband gone home, till it was only the mother and the youngest left, the youngest the last one properly awake, the mother nodding some in the chair. The youngest looked at the near-empty cups and found a drop of something stronger left in the bottle on the shelf, poured it out small into what glasses there was, and held one up, nothing prepared, nothing thought out beforehand.

The youngest: Here's to him, then. Stubborn as they come and we wouldn't have had him no other way. Now hold on now, hold on, b'y.

And they drank to that, the two of them, in the quiet kitchen, the cold tea and the cold fish and brewis still sitting there on the table same as it would sit there the next morning too.
