Well now, I'm some ashamed it's took me this long to get a proper letter down for you. Near on a year you're gone, and all you've had out of me is them short bits over the phone and the odd photo fired off in a hurry between jobs. I kept telling meself, the day, I'm going to sit down and do this proper, and then the day would slip off same as it always does. So here I am at last, kettle on, house quiet, and I'm not getting up again till it's done.

First off, the porch. You'll mind how it was leaning off to the one side since the winter before you left, and Dad kept saying he'd get to it and never did. Well, he's after getting to it finally. He had some sleeveen from town in to look at it first, took one look at the state of things and quoted a price would make your eyes water, took the deposit and we never seen hide nor hair of him since. So Dad did it himself in the end, the better part of two weekends and more swearing than I ever heard out of the man, but it's square now, new boards and all, don't creak when you go out for the mail no more. Oh me nerves, mind, no sooner was that finished than the back step went and rotted clean through, so that's queued up next. This house is never done confessing what's wrong with it. We put up a little lean-to off the shed too, for the wood, on account of it kept getting soaked through every fall, and Dad's some proud of it, shows it off to anyone that comes by like it's a cathedral.

Garden did some good this year, better than most. Mom put in more turnip and cabbage than we'll ever eat between us, and there's a patch of peas coming up out back where you always used to put yours in. I kept it going, didn't have the heart to plant nothing else there.

Now for the big news, and don't go falling off your chair. The young one's after moving out. Got taken on permanent with the home care crowd back in the spring, after two winters of just being on call and never knowing week to week if there'd be hours, and when the old woman she used to relieve for finally retired out of it altogether, the job went full time and that was that. "Go way b'y," is about what I said myself when she told us. She's got a little flat over top the shop on the main road now, right nice for what it is, one room and a kitchen you can barely turn around in, but she's some delighted with it, sends pictures of the curtains like she invented curtains. House feels different with her gone off too, quieter than I like some evenings.

You knows what I'm not going to say to your face, so I'll say it here instead. Tell buddy in the truck I still haven't forgot, and neither has he.

That's for you and nobody else, don't go asking me to explain it to Mom.

Neighbors, now. The crowd across the road, the ones with the three youngsters, they're gone off to Alberta, work dried up here and buddy got on with a rig crew out there, whole family packed up in the spring and the house has sat empty since, grass gone to seed something shockin. New crowd moved into the blue house down past the tickle, a young couple, quiet, keep to themselves mostly, though chummy's been up asking Dad about the boat twice already, so I imagine that'll turn into something before long. And the neighbor next door, her daughter had the baby back in the winter, a girl, and now there's photos of the youngster on every surface in that house, can't set your tea down without setting it on the child's face. We had the mummers around at Christmas same as ever, knocking on Tibb's Eve first the way they always do, and the young one near had a heart attack not knowing the neighbor from next door under the getup, right in her own porch.

Two people asks after you near every time I sees them, without fail. The neighbor next door is one, always wants to know are you eating, are you warm enough, like you gone off to the moon and not just the mainland. And the one from the shop, you knows the one, went to school with us, she still asks how you're getting on every time I'm in for the milk.

The one from the shop: Whadda y'at. How's your sister making out?

Me: She's making out grand, got herself a job and everything, sends her love.

The one from the shop: Tell her the shop's not the same without her stealing the good chocolate bars.

I never did tell her you still owes her for them, by the way.

I'll confess something to you here I'd never say with my mouth. The night you left, I sat out in the truck in the driveway a good hour after everyone else went in, didn't even have the radio on. I'm not one for making a fuss and I never let on to a soul, but I missed you something shockin that night and every night for a good while after, and I'm only telling you now on account of a letter don't have to look you in the eye.

The winter, b'y, I won't lie to you, it was some hard. We had a silver thaw in January took the power out three days, wires down the whole road, everybody cooking on camp stoves and laughing about it after because what else is there to do. Then it just hung on, mauzy and grey clean through til near May, duckish coming down by three in the afternoon it felt like, shockin cold with it, and I thought the sun forgot us altogether. Spring came in decent once it finally came, though, and the capelin rolled in good, and the men had a mug-up down the wharf before they hauled the boat up, and Dad got out for a few fish himself before they closed it up again. Mom did up a boil-up down the shore first fine Saturday we got, tea out of the tin kettle and all, and the place felt like it remembered itself again.

Mom had us all over for a Jiggs dinner Sunday last, whole production, and Dad tried his hand at toutons the next morning and burnt the first batch shockin awful, smoke right out the door, standing there like a proper angishore staring into the pan while it went to charcoal on him.

Mother: I'm after telling you to watch the pan, not stand there yarning!

Father: I was watching it, woman. It watched itself right into charcoal.

We had a scoff after church all the same, fish and brewis with the scrunchions done up crispy the way you like them, and I thought of you the whole meal, wished you was at the table stealing more than your share same as always.

Now I got questions for you, and I wants real answers, not the two lines you usually gives me. What's the weather like out there this time of year, is it anything like ours or does it not even know what a proper winter is? Have ye got yourselves settled in proper, you and whoever you're minding house with, anyone out there that feels like home yet, someone you'd ring up if the power went and you needed to laugh about it after? And are you actually eating right, or is it takeout every night same as when you first landed there, because I knows you, and I knows what your fridge looks like when nobody's checking on you. You still answers the phone with a where ya to like nothing's changed, and that's some comfort, I'll say.

I'm going to close it off here before this turns into a book, though it's near one already. I'm sending you a parcel time I get down to the post office, same as I always say I will, and this time I means it, I've got half of it packed already sitting by the door so I can't forget it, the molasses, the partridgeberry jam, and whatever else fits in around it. Don't be waiting up for it, knowing me it'll take a while getting there, but it's coming, yes b'y.

Write me back when you can, and don't leave it as long as I did.

Love,
Your sister
