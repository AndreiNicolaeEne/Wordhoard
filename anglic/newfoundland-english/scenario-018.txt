Now b'y, since the whole crowd of ye is after askin for it start to finish, and me love here's not sayin one word all night, only sittin there grinnin into her glass like she don't already know every bit of it, I s'pose I got no choice only to tell it proper.

'Twasn't no grand thing, how we met, whatever ye're expectin. Down to the chip shop on Water Street of a Friday night it was, the one does the good scrunchions in with the dressing, and I'm standin there some hungry after a double shift, fumblin in me pockets like an angishore for change I already knowed wasn't there. She's behind the counter doin the fryer, looks up at me straight-faced, not a smile on her, and says:

My love: "Luh, b'y, you gonna pay for that the day or you waitin on a better offer?"

And that was the whole of it, right there. One line, and I never forgot a word of it, not to this day. If I'm ever slow gettin me wallet out even now, that's exactly what comes back to me.

I was the one draggin me feet after that, and I'll say why plain, no shame in it. I'd been with someone before that turned out a right sleeveen, sweet as anything to yer face and somethin else altogether behind yer back, and I wasn't fit to trust nobody's talk again, not for love nor money. So when this one kept comin round the chip shop askin was I workin the next Friday, I held meself back a good while. Wasn't nothin she done, only I was gun-shy as a dog that's been kicked. But she never pushed me, not once. Just kept turnin up steady, same night, same order, same bit of talk over the counter, till one duckish evenin in the fall she walked me home in the mauzy without me even askin, carried me bag the whole way and never tried nothin only that. And I minds thinkin, well, this one's not after anything only company, and that's when the wall come down in me.

First time we went out proper, not just chance meetins over a counter, was a walk out by the tickle of a Sunday evenin, some cold already, duckish comin on fast. Halfway round a squall took us and turned her umbrella right inside out, snapped two of the ribs clean off, no good to man nor beast after that. I went to sling it in the first bin we passed and she wouldn't hear of it, said it seen us through our first proper time together and it was stayin. We took to callin it chummy, don't ask me why, and chummy's hangin off the nail inside the porch door this very night, and there's not a one of ye come through that door lately hasn't gone luh, what's that old broke thing doin there still.

No sooner had we got goin good than I had to go out west, out to the oil, on account of there was nothin steady goin here them years. Eighteen months of it, six weeks on and two off, and that's a hard way to court a body, whatever anyone tells ye. We got through it only by talkin every chance we got, she'd answer the phone out there with a whadda y'at instead of hello, cost me a small fortune in calling cards just to hear her say it, and she'd send a parcel now and then with a tin of biscuits and a note stuck down in among them, and I'd write her back on whatever paper was to hand out in camp.

The night I knew for certain, I was out in camp, some homesick, and told a buddy from Corner Brook I was bunkin with that I reckoned I was goin to marry the girl in the chip shop back home. Didn't think no more of it. Only that buddy's wife was some thick with her sister, and word got back to her before I ever laid eyes on her again. She rung me up laughin and says:

My love: "I hear tell you're plannin to marry me, and here's me still waitin on you to ask proper."

So when I come home off rotation for good, I asked her right there at the sink with me hands full of suds, no ring even bought yet, only:

Me: "Well, I s'pose we better make it official then."

My love: "B'y, took you long enough."

And that was that. Married the followin autumn, close on the twelve month after, which round here they'll tell ye is quick, only we'd already put in our years of waitin before that.

The families took to it easy enough on me side, me mother thought the world of her from the first Sunday she come for Jiggs dinner and ate two plates and asked after the makin of the pea soup besides. Her father took a bit longer warmin to me, never said much of anything, only shook me hand some hard the day we told them and said take care of her or you'll answer to me, and that was the whole of his blessin, and it was plenty. The mother, mind, said go way b'y when we told her the date was set, and then went off in the kitchen and cried anyway, happy tears, she said after. That first Christmas we were courtin I dragged her out mummerin on Tibb's Eve with the whole street, and she never let on who she was under the getup, not even at her own mother's door, laughed so hard after she nearly give herself away.

The thing that comes back to me from the weddin day itself, and no photograph ever got it, was the silver thaw that mornin, ice thick on every wire and rail in town, and the whole crowd of us comin up the church steps holdin onto one another for dear life, slidin and grabbin and laughin so hard half of us couldn't hold a straight face for the pictures after. Ye'll see it if ye looks close at any shot from that mornin, everyone's got a hand out grabbin someone else's arm.

We've had our hard stretch since, same as anyone. The year the work dried up on me things got shockin tight, tighter than either of us likes to let on even now. What carried us through it was nothin fancy, only fish and brewis of a Saturday with plenty of scrunchions on top, toutons and molasses of a Sunday mornin no matter how lean things were, and her not once throwin it up to me. Me mother's pea soup turned up on the step some weeks when she knew we were struggling, without a word said about it either way. We just held onto one another and got on with it, the same as ye do.

And I'll tell ye what hasn't changed one bit in all this time. I loves her the exact same as I did that first duckish evenin walkin her home in the mauzy, and she still stands there and gives me that same straight-faced look, waitin, like she's got all the time in the world and none of the patience for it. Some things never change, and thank god for it, oh me nerves, I don't know what I'd do if they did.

So luh, all of ye, get yer glasses up now. Here's to the both of us, and here's to every one of ye that's put up with the two of us this long and never once let on ye were sick of the story. Yes b'y. Cheers, me loves.
