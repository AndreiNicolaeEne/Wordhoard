The new one: I heard tell there's some falling out between yourselves and next door. What happened there, if you don't mind me asking?

Me: I don't mind, I'll tell you, but I'm only telling you my end of it, cause that's the only end I knows proper. You want the other side you can go knock on their door, see how far that gets you.

Now. You wouldn't believe it to look at it now, the state of it, but there was a time me and them next door were closer than family. Years and years of it, b'y, more years than you been alive I'd say. Her mother and my mother grew up two streets over from each other, went to the same school, and when we all ended up on this street it was like it was meant. We was in and out of each other's houses like they was our own. Boil-up down by the water of a Sunday, the whole crowd of us. Mug-up in her kitchen most evenings, the kettle never off the go. Come Christmas we'd have Jiggs dinner back and forth, one year at our place one year at theirs, and fish and brewis with the scrunchions on top after Mass, and toutons in the morning for the youngsters. Tibb's Eve we'd all be into it together and mummers coming to the door and nobody locking up. I minded her young ones after school for years, never asked for a cent, and she done the same for mine. That's the kind of close I mean. Not neighbourly. Family.

So you can see why it stings the way it does now.

It started small, the way these things does. Foolish small. He put up a shed, out the back, for his gear and whatnot, and he put it too close to the line. Not right on it, but close enough that when the snow come and slid off his roof it was landing half on my side, and his eave was dripping onto my flowerbed all spring. I let it go the first year. Didn't say nothing, thought maybe it'd settle, maybe I was reading it wrong. Second year I mentioned it to him nice as you please, right out in the yard, said luh, that shed of yours is a bit close to the line, would you mind shifting the eave trough or putting a bit of flashing on it so it's not running onto my side. He said he would. Never done it.

I let that go too, figured he was busy, sure everyone's busy. Come the third spring I asked again, and this time I asked his wife, thinking maybe it'd get through better that way, and she said oh yes, she'd remind him, she was some sorry about it. Nothing happened. Fourth time I asked I'll admit I had an edge on me by then, I'm only human, and I said it wasn't a favour I was asking no more, it needed doing.

That's when the neighbour started carrying word back and forth, the one two doors down that was friendly with both of us. She'd come to me and say, oh, they're saying you were some rude to him about the shed, going on like he'd done something criminal. And I'd say I never raised my voice once. Then she'd go over there and by the time it came back to me a second time it was that I'd been over banging on their door shouting about it, which never happened, not once, I'm after telling you exactly what I said and it wasn't that.

And when I finally said it to his face, that I'd asked him four times and been ignored every time, he stood right there and told me flat out that request was never ignored, not once, that I never asked him nothing about it till that very day. Called me a liar to my own face, more or less, over something his own wife had told me she'd pass on to him.

So I'll admit it, I did a small thing back. Quiet, mind you, nothing anyone could point to. There's a bit of my hedge that always leaned his way and give him a bit of shade for his tomatoes, and I let it grow out that summer instead of trimming it back like I always done. Petty, I know it. I'm not proud of it. But I was some vexed.

Word got back to me not long after, through the same one that carries everything, that he'd said to someone at the shop that I was always one to make a production out of nothing, always was, even as a young one. That's the one that stung. Not the shed, not the ignoring, not even being called a liar. That, because it wasn't true and it come from someone who'd known me since I was in diapers, and it was said to a stranger like it was common knowledge.

After all that I still tried. I brought over a plate at Christmas, same as always, toutons and all, thinking we could let it lie, water under the bridge, for the sake of the years we had before any of it. It come back to me the next day, plate and all, with a thanks-but-no-thanks that wasn't said kind at all.

The street took sides after that, more or less down the middle. The ones up top of the hill mostly stuck with me, the ones down towards the tickle mostly went with them, and there was a fair bit of muttering at the mailboxes for a good while. It all come to a head one evening out on the road, right where anyone walking by or looking out their window could hear the whole of it, the two of us going at each other proper, years of it coming out at once, and neither one backing down an inch. That's when he brought up his weed-whacker gone missing off his step and near enough said I had it or knew who did, and I told him straight I never touched his weed-whacker and never would, and that was near the last civil word passed between us.

That's, what, going on eleven years now. Eleven years of not a word. We'll nod if we pass on the road, that's the height of it. The young ones that used to run between the two houses are near grown now and don't hardly remember why it's like this, only that it is. Every silver thaw that comes through and takes the wires down I still think of how he used to come check on my mother when the power went, and I have to push that thought away same as I push the driveway.

And I'll say this much to you, and I wouldn't say it to many. Some days, this long on, I'm not entirely sure no more who started it. Whether it was the shed, or the asking, or the not-listening, or something said that never even reached my ears at all. I only knows my own side of it, like I told you at the start. That's the only side I got.
