I got them all sat down in the mother's front room the day, the whole crowd of them, and I never had to say a word yet for them to know something was coming. Ye can always tell. Nobody was reaching for the toutons the mother had made, nobody was on about the weather nor who's after buying what new truck. They just sat there, some quiet, mugs going cold in their hands, waiting on me.

Me: I got something I'm after keeping back a long time now, years, and it's time I said it plain, before I gets too old to say anything at all.

Ye all remembers the stage burning. That winter, when I was no more than knee high to a puncheon, and the whole crowd of us lost near everything we had put by for the season. The salt fish, gone, every last quintal of it. The nets gone, the twine, the gaff, the punt near went up too only for the wind turning at the last. That was some hard winter after, b'y. Ye all knows how hard. Bread and molasses some nights and be glad of it. There was talk for years of what it done to this family, the debt of it, the going without, and it never did stop being talked about, not really, not under the surface.

And ye all knows too who got blamed for it. Uncle. You were down to the stage that evening with the lantern, everybody said, and you'd had a drop taken, and it went round the whole shore before Christmas morning that you left something burning and walked off home not minding. And nobody in this family forgave you proper for it. Not once, not ever, not really. I heard it said of you at scoffs and mug-ups both, sleeveen this and sleeveen that, careless old angishore, never trusted with so much as the key to the new stage after. I seen the looks you got at your own brother's table.

Well I'm here to tell the whole crowd of ye the day, plain, it wasn't you.

It was the eldest.

I seen it with me own two eyes. I was hid up behind the flakes that evening, duckish it was, getting on for dark and a bit of mauze rolling in off the tickle, and I seen the eldest and a couple of the crowd she used to run with sneaking a smoke before they went off mumming, it being Tibb's Eve and all. I seen her flick the butt in amongst the oakum and the old twine and never so much as look back. I was no more than eight year old and I never rightly understood what I was after seeing till the whole thing went up not ten minutes on.

She caught me looking. Grabbed me right by the arm in the dark and told me if I ever breathed a word of it she'd see to it I never lived it down neither, and I believed her, young and frightened as I was.

I never told, if I'm honest with the whole of ye, for fear of what it'd stir up again. The anger had only just gone quiet by then, years on, and I couldn't bear the thought of setting it all off fresh in this house. And there was some part of me wanted to protect her too, being she was me own sister and I loved her whatever she done. And there was some part of me plain scared of being the one who caused more grief, on top of what was already caused.

I nearly told it once, years back now, at the aunt's own mother's wake. I had it right up in me throat that day, I did, seeing uncle stood off by himself in the corner the way he always was at them things, not fully welcome even there. I opened me mouth to say it and I seen the mother's face across the room, wore right through with grief already that day, and I couldn't put another thing on her. So I swallowed it back down again and said nothing, and I've regretted that swallowing ever since.

But I've been carrying it since, b'y, and it's some heavy load I'm telling ye, some shockin heavy. Watching uncle go round all them years wearing a name that was never his to wear. Watching him not get asked to skipper, not trusted, hearing sleeveen said of him at his own table and him saying nothing back because what was there to say. That's took a toll on me nerves something wicked, keeping that in me all this time.

The room went that quiet after I said it ye could hear the clock going in the hall.

The eldest: You don't know what you're on about.

Me: I do know, b'y. I'm certain of it. I seen it with me own eyes and I've carried it near forty year now, and I'm not saying it to hurt no one. I'm saying it because it's the truth of it and it's overdue.

The aunt: Forty year, is it. Forty year this family let my husband go round with that name on him and nobody once thought to say different.

Me: I know it. I know what it done to him and I know what it done to you an all, watching it happen to him and not able to change one thing about it. I'm not asking to be forgiven for that part. I'm only telling ye now so it stops.

Uncle sat there a long while and never said nothing at all, just his hands going on the arms of the chair, and when he did speak his voice wasn't steady.

The uncle: I always knew it wasn't fair. I said so till I was blue in the face and not one soul believed me over it, and now I'm supposed to just take that in, am I, all them years gone that I can't get back. Some relief in it, I won't lie, and some anger too, right shockin anger, that it's forty year late.

The eldest sat there white as a sheet, and then it come out of her all at once.

The eldest: I was seventeen year old and scared out of me mind and I never meant for none of it to happen, and don't any of ye think I haven't lived with it too, every day of it, watching him carry what should've been mine to carry.

The mother turned on her then, and the old anger that had gone quiet all them years come up fresh in her face, only this time it was pointed the right way at last.

The mother: Forty year I watched me own brother take that off ye and ye let him. Ye let him, and ye let this whole family think less of him for it, and ye said not one word.

Me: That part's on me as much as her. I'm the one kept it all this time, and I'm sorry for it, every year of it. Not making excuses, just saying it's owed, that sorry, and I'm saying it now in front of the whole of ye.

Nobody in that room was reaching for the tea after that neither. The uncle sat looking older than I ever seen him look, and the eldest had her face in her two hands, and the mother's own anger was still going, low and steady, finding somewhere new to land after forty year of having nowhere to go.

It's not fixed, what happened the day. It's not tidy and it's not over, and there's them in this family will be a long time coming round to where the anger ought to sit now instead of where it's sat all these years. But it's standing on the truth of it now instead of around it, and that's more than we had this morning, and I s'pose that'll have to do us for a start.
