Awright, siddown, leh me tell yinna how we people come to be here een Nassau, the way it pass down to me and the way I tellin it to yinna now, same way I hear it off the old people when I was pickney sittin on the step of a evenin.

Cat Island da where we come from, way back, generations befo any a we born. The old people dem did have a lil ground down dey, ground provision, a lil fishin off de rocks, nutten grand but it did mek do. Den one storm come through and wash 'way de crop two year runnin, and after dat de ground jus dead fu true. Nutten fi nyam, nutten fi sell, and de young men dem nuh have no work to turn to atall. So it come to be dat somebody had to go.

My great-great-granddaddy, 'e was de one went first. Alone, bey, no wife, no chilren wit 'em, jus 'e one self and whatsoever 'e coulda carry. 'E tek de mailboat outta Cat Island, and dem say dat crossin was someting terrible. De sea a knock de lil boat bout fu two, three day straight, everybody sick, water comin over de side, and 'e nuh have nutten fi eat but a lil hard bread 'e own mother mek 'em carry. 'E use to tell it seh 'e never see water vex so bad een 'e whole life, and 'e never figure 'e mekkin it to Nassau atall.

But 'e mek it. 'E land here wit nutten but de clothes on 'e back and a lil straw bag 'e mother did plait fu 'em befo 'e leff, so 'e woulda have someting from home wit 'em. Dat same bag still deh, right now, in a drawer at we house, all wore an patch up, but still deh. Nobody nuh use it fi carry nutten no more, it jus deh fu remember.

Nassau nuh easy fu 'em, not atall, not first. 'E ain had no work, no place fi lay 'e head neither. 'E gone stay wit some people from Cat Island who did come befo 'em, over de hill, and dem tek 'em in. 'E sleep on a lil cot een de front room, borrowin space, nuh 'e own. Months pass so, 'e a walk de dock every mornin lookin fu someting, anyting, and mos day 'e come back wit nutten atall. De people 'e stayin wit did have a potcake by de step, and dem say dat dog use to walk down to de gate wit 'em every mornin like 'e know 'e need de comp'ny.

Den one day 'e get tek on down de dock, luggin cargo off de boats, a day here, a day dey, whenever a ship come in and dem need extra hand. Somebody from de wharf woulda come go call 'em from de corner soon as a boat dock, and 'e woulda run go tek de work quick befo anybody else get it. Nuh steady work, mind yinna, but 'e tek wha 'e could get, and 'e save wha lil 'e could out a it. 'E did sen word back seh 'e wahn sen fu we soon as 'e have enough put by, and slow slow, dat's wha 'e do.

'Bout two year and a lil bit more after 'e first leff, 'e finally have enough fi sen fu 'e wife and de chilren dem fi come. Dat's when de sip-sip start, 'cause de ole people say when de wife and chilren dem finally reach, wasn't all a dem mek it. Dey had a cousin travelin wit dem, and dat cousin never see Nassau. Whatever happen on dat crossin, de fambly nuh talk 'bout it straight, only de old aunt use to speak on it, and even she nuh call no name when she tell it.

The old aunt: 'E was poorly befo we even leff, but wasn't nutten we coulda do bout it out dey pon de water. By de time we reach, 'e was gone from we.

Me: But who was 'e, though? What was 'e name?

The old aunt: Nuh good come from callin it up again. 'E was fambly, dat's all yinna need fi know.

So dat's how it stay, right down to today. Nobody nuh call de name, jus "de cousin," and everybody know who mean.

Once de fambly reach whole, 'e and de wife find a lil place over de hill, Bain Town side, rentin at first, borrowin from nobody but payin week to week. But befo three year pass, 'e did buy a lil plot and put up a house wit 'e own hand, wood frame, tin top, same as most house een dat time. 'E did tek up de carpentry proper 'bout dat time too, buildin fu 'eself and buildin fu whosoever did have de need and de money, and dat trade 'e keep straight through, right to de end a 'e days.

De wife, she keep on wit de straw plaitin she did learn from 'e own mother befo dey ever leff Cat Island. She start plait hat and basket and mat fi sell down by de market, and dat trade nuh stop wit she neither. It come down de fambly, straight to me gran, and even now I could still plait a lil basket if yinna give me de straw and set me down long enough.

De chilren dem born right dey, een dat same lil house over de hill, one after de other, and dat, dem say, was wha mek de place home fu true. Befo dat, Nassau was jus a place dey did land, a place 'e did have fi mek do. 'E did wuk and 'e did save and 'e did wait, but 'e wasn't home yet, not really. It was only once de chilren dem born right dey, deh in dat house, dat de place stop bein someplace 'e end up and start bein someplace 'e belong.

Dat's how de old people tell it to me, sittin on de step of a evenin, potcake sleepin unda de step, and de whole yard smellin like whatever was pon de fire. I remember hearin it dat same way, half listenin, half nyamin whatever was in me hand, while de big people dem sip-sip 'bout who doin wha down de road, who 'e seein, who 'e vex wit. I ain understand den why dey did keep tellin it so much, over and over, every lil while somebody bring it up again. I understand now, dough. Yinna tell it so de chilren dem don't forget where dey come from, so when dem own chilren ask, dem have someting fi pass on too.

Dat's how I tellin it now, same way it come to me, sittin right here wit yinna, so it nuh dead wit me neither.
