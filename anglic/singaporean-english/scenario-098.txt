Every Chinese New Year when we clean the house, my mother will take out this one small brass box from the altar drawer, very old already, the hinge also rusty, and she will say, this one cannot throw ah, this is your great-grandfather's, all the way from the village one. Then she will tell the story again, same story, but I never sian of it, because each time she tell, she remember one more small thing.

Back in the old place, she always say, was nothing to eat one. Not like here where you complain the economy rice too pricey lah, over there real no food, the land also not enough to go round, too many mouths, one small plot, everybody fighting for it. Got flood some years, got drought some years, whichever also cannot win. So the men in that village, one by one, they all leave. My great-grandfather also, he was the one to go, alone, leave his own wife and the small children behind, because cannot bring everybody at once, no money what.

The mother, telling it: he never even tell his own mother he was going first one, he say he don't want her to cry and stop him. He just walk out early morning, still dark, carry one bag only, and go down to the port. That time already very old man when he tell his own children this part, but his eyes still red one, my mother say.

The journey itself, wah, don't play play. Weeks on the boat, packed like that, below deck, hot, no air, everybody seasick, water rationed. My great-grandfather always say the smell alone can kill you already, never mind the storm. And there was a storm, a bad one, the boat rocking so hard people praying to whatever god they believe, some Buddha, some just crying for their mother. Not everybody who went on that boat reached here.

The mother: your great-grandfather had a cousin travelling with him also. That one, he never make it. Something happen during the crossing, some sickness going round, spread fast in that hold, no doctor, nothing you can do. My great-grandfather never liked to say the name, till the day he die he don't say the name, he only ever call him "that poor cousin of mine," like that. So even now we don't know what to call him what, we just know there was one, and he never reach.

So my great-grandfather arrived here basically with nothing. No relative waiting, no proper place, just the bag he carry from the village, inside got a few clothes, some dried stuff his mother pack for him, and this brass box, which was actually for holding a bit of ash from the home village temple, so he not totally cut off from the ancestors, he say.

First stretch here, jialat one. No steady work at all. He just hang around the docks and the godowns, do whatever come, carry this, carry that, one day got job, three days nothing. Sleep also no proper place at first, until a clan family, same dialect group, distant distant relation somehow connected back in the village, took him in, let him roll out a mat in the corner of their room. He never own that place, just borrowed, and he always remember that, my mother say, he tell his children never forget that family, whatever happen, you help them if they ever need.

Slowly slowly he pick up steady work, that time as a carpenter's helper, because turns out back home he used to help his own father with furniture a bit, so he already got some hand for it. The towkay saw he was not lazy, gave him more work, and from a helper he become properly a carpenter himself, and that trade he kept for good, all his life, then pass some of it down too.

Once he saved enough, bit by bit, coin by coin, he sent word back, that time also not simple, must find someone trustworthy going back, must send money through the right channel, cannot just send like that, and he called for his wife and the children to come. From the day he left until the day they finally step off the boat here, was a little more than two years. Two years he was here alone, working, saving, and waiting, not knowing really if they will even survive the crossing themselves, only praying.

The mother: when the wife and children finally arrive, your great-grandfather almost never recognise the smallest one, because that one was still a baby when he left, and now already walking around. He just stood there at the place they were told to wait, and when he finally see them come through, he never say anything first, just stood there looking, everybody around also quiet, then he walk over slow slow and touch the child's head, that's all, he don't even talk, my mother say, that generation not the type to make a big scene.

After the family reunited, they found a proper room to rent, not big, just one room in a shophouse, shared toilet and kitchen with two other families, but at least it was theirs to hold onto, not borrowed like before. Within the first few years there they settled properly, and it was there my grandmother was born, then a few more after her.

The mother always say this part with some feeling: it was only once the children start being born right there, in that room, that the place really become home for your great-grandfather. Before that he say his heart still half in the old village, always thinking one day go back. But once his own children only know this place, know no other place, then what to go back for, he say. This is home now lah, he tell people, my children born here.

That brass box, the one still sitting in our altar drawer now, that's the same one he carry all the way, still got a bit of the old ash inside, my mother top up a little more incense ash over the years so it never finish, never truly empty. Everything else from that crossing long gone already, the clothes rot, the bag fall apart, only this one small thing survive till now.

Me: and this is how I hear it too, sitting on the floor of my grandmother's flat as a kid, half falling asleep, listening to my mother and my grandmother go back and forth filling in bits, one remembering the storm part, one remembering the carpenter part, arguing a bit over small details, whether it was two years or two years plus a few months, whether the cousin died on the way there or just after landing. Nobody sure of everything one hundred percent anymore, that's how these things go what, passed down mouth to mouth, some bits get soft round the edges, some bits stay sharp forever, like the part about the cousin with no name, that part never change, every time told the same way, no name, just "that poor cousin of mine."

Now when my own children ask about the brass box, I tell it the same way it was told to me, in my own words, my own rhythm, but same bones underneath, the hunger back there, the one man going first alone, the boat, the cousin lost, the borrowed room, the carpenter's trade, the two years apart, the children born here that finally made this place home instead of just a stopping point. One day it will be their turn to tell it to somebody smaller than them, sitting on some floor somewhere, maybe already forgotten which flat, which void deck, which year, but the box will still be there, still not to be thrown, still holding a little bit of somewhere very far from here.
