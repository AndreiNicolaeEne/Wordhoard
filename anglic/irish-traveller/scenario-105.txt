The mother: Right. Sit down there a minute and give us your ear, I'm after doing the sums half the night and I want them wrote proper before your father's up. We're going, the pair of us, over to Appleby for the Fair this year, please God, and I'm not moving off the one thing, a thousand, and not a shilling over it. That's the gred we have between us and that's the gred that has to do us, start to finish.

The eldest: A thousand for the whole of it, Mammy, going and coming and all?

The mother: The whole of it. Every bit that's spent comes out of that thousand or we're not going at all, do you hear me.

The eldest: I hear you well enough. What's the dear item first, get that out of the way.

The mother: Sure isn't it the getting there itself. The boat below and the diesel up through after, that's the big bite taken out of it before we've even wet a drop of tea over there. Call it four hundred, and I'm not codding you, that's being fair to it and all, not gammy money, the boat's gone up and the diesel with it.

The eldest: Four hundred only to be standing on the far side of the water.

The mother: Four hundred only to be standing on the far side, God help us. Write it so. Four hundred.

The eldest: That's four hundred wrote down. How long are we stopping for, with that much ate out of it already?

The mother: I was thinking the fortnight, if I'm honest with you.

The eldest: Ah Mammy, you'll not get a fortnight out of six hundred, not with everything else still to come out of it.

The mother: I know well I won't, amn't I only saying what I'd love, not what's going. Give us a week so.

The eldest: A week's an awful short stop after all that getting there. You'd only be after settling in and you'd be turning the wagon for home again.

The mother: Ten days.

The eldest: Ten days I'd say, aye. That's a fair stop and it leaves us something still to work with.

The mother: Ten days it is. Write that under the four hundred.

The eldest: And we have to be above at the Fair itself the whole of the big day, Mammy, I'm not missing that for gold nor gred, no matter what it costs us.

The mother: You needn't be telling me that, amn't I the one wanted it these fifteen years. We'll be above there if it's the last thing we do, and we'll go without something else to have it. We're not paying for any grand site with the leccy and the showers and the whole of it for ten nights, we'll rough it the nights either side of the Fair and put the gred where it counts, up on the hill for the two big days.

The eldest: What's the sham above looking for the pitch, so, that owns the ground?

The mother: Two hundred and eighty for the whole stay, he said, and that's counting the two Fair days dear and the rest of it cheap enough, rough as it'll be.

The eldest: Two eighty. That's, four hundred and two eighty, that's six hundred and eighty between the two of them.

The mother: Six eighty. Write it under.

The eldest: We're over half of it gone already and we've not ate a bite nor slept a night yet.

The mother: We haven't, God bless us. Now, if we can solk that crossing any cheaper, the early one does go dearer the longer you leave it, but if we're quick booking it we might bring the four hundred down, if we can knock even the fifty off that, we could stretch the ten days to eleven. I'd love the eleventh day above on the hill, so I would, one more evening at it before we turn for the tober home.

The eldest: We'll see nearer the time so, no harm in hoping on it.

The mother: No harm at all. Please God it comes in cheap.

The eldest: What about the one night out, Mammy? A proper feed, the two of us, in off the site, maybe a bit of music after if there's any going. Call it fifty.

The mother: Fifty for one night's eating, are you gone soft in the head. We'd be better off solking that fifty and putting it by, you don't know what'll turn up over there, gammy weather or the van playing up or anything.

The eldest: We're only over the once in our lives, Mammy. What's the whole trip for if we're not having the one good night out of it, sat above in the kena every other night eating out of the one pot?

The mother: We'll leave that sit for the minute, we'll come back to it. Don't be pushing me on it now.

The eldest: Fair enough, we'll come back to it.

The mother: Right, the food. What'll we say, twenty a day between the pair of us, the eating and the tea and the bread and whatever?

The eldest: Twenty's fair enough if we're not going mad on it. Ten days of that is two hundred.

The mother: Two hundred. Six eighty and two hundred is eight hundred and eighty.

The eldest: Eight eighty. Write it under the site.

The mother: And a hundred aside, whatever happens, God between us and all harm, the van breaks down, the cob takes gammy, one of us takes sick, whatever it is, that hundred's not to be touched only if it has to be touched.

The eldest: A hundred's only sense, Mammy, you'd be mad to go without it. Eight eighty and the hundred is nine hundred and eighty.

The mother: Nine eighty. Twenty left in it as it stands.

The eldest: Now what about the fifty, so.

The mother: Ah, go on. Put the fifty in. Sure amn't I after saying no to myself the whole time we've been sat here, we might as well have the one good night, we'll be tired enough scraping the gred together for the rest of it.

The eldest: Nine eighty and the fifty is a thousand and thirty, Mammy.

The mother: A thousand and thirty. We're thirty over.

The eldest: Thirty short of where we need to be.

The mother: Gloke at that now. Thirty over and we've the whole thing wrote out proper and everything.

The eldest: We could come the morning of instead of the evening before, save the one night off the pitch above. The sham won't miss the one night's rent off us.

The mother: How much would that bug off it?

The eldest: He said fifty less if we're not there the extra night, sure we'd only be sleeping in the van at the port anyway if we go that road.

The mother: Do that so. Take the fifty off the site. Two eighty down to two thirty.

The eldest: Nine eighty and the thirty over goes, and we're, four hundred, two thirty, two hundred, the hundred, and the fifty. Add the whole lot again.

The mother: Nine hundred and eighty.

The eldest: Nine eighty out of the thousand. Twenty left over.

The mother: Twenty left to spare and the whole of it paid for, the getting there and the ten days and the Fair itself and the one good night out of it and the hundred sitting by for whatever God sends us that we're not expecting. That'll do us grand.

The eldest: Twenty saved is twenty saved, Mammy.

The mother: It is, please God. Write the whole of it out fair on the good page now, not the back of the child's copy, and we'll show your father when he's up. Fair play to the two of us, we've it done.

The eldest: We have it done. God bless the work.
