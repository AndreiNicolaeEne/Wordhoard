The youngest: Go on so. Tell us the one, the one about the thing that calls out after dark.

Me: Sure it's always of an evening this one comes up, isn't it, when the light's near gone and someone's after asking why we don't be letting the children walk the bend on their own past a certain hour, and there's always one young one sitting there thinking it's only an old one's talk for keeping fellas in early. And it is that, God knows. But it's more than that as well. So if ye're going to sit there the whole way through without a word out of ye, I'll tell it the way it was told to me.

Now nobody ever seen the thing proper, not once, not in all the years this has been going round, and that's the first thing to know about it. It's felt before it's ever seen, if it's seen at all, and what comes first, always, is the voice. Ye'll be walking along, minding yer own business, and a voice will reach ye out of the dark before ye've laid eyes on a single thing, and that's the shape of it as far as anyone's ever managed to put a word to, a voice looking for a body to put itself into.

And here's the divilment of it. It doesn't call in any strange voice at all. It calls in the voice of someone ye love. A mother, a sister, a husband, whoever it is ye'd turn for without thinking twice, that's the voice it borrows, and it borrows it dead on, so there's no telling the difference by the sound alone. And the whole point of it, the only point of it as far as anyone's ever made out, is to draw ye off the path and away from anyone that might could help ye if things went wrong on ye. It's not roaring nor threatening. It's only calling, soft as anything, the way someone who loved ye would call if they needed ye bad.

It only comes after midnight, mind. Never a minute before it. And it only comes on a night with no moon in it at all, black dark, the kind where ye can't see yer own hand past yer face. Give it a moon and it won't show itself, or won't call itself, whichever way ye want to put it. That's held in every telling I ever heard, without fail.

And it's tied to the one spot, always the one spot, not the whole road and not the whole place, only the bend where the tober dips down by the old flooded ground past the site, where the water sits black even in the middle of the day and the reeds stand up through it thick. That's the spot. I never once heard tell of it following a body home, nor turning up anywhere else. It stays where it stays, and that's a mercy if it's anything at all.

I heard it first meself as a child, off me own grandmother, God rest her, sitting the very same as you're sitting now, and I'll tell ye, she told it two different ways depending on her mood that particular night. Some evenings she told it dead straight, no smile on her at all, handing it to ye the same as she'd hand ye a fact about the weather coming in. Other evenings she told it with a laugh riding along in her voice, but even then that laugh never went the whole way up into her eyes, and I noticed that even as a small one, long before I'd the words for what I was noticing.

The first proof anyone ever gives ye, and it's always the first thing offered, is the ground itself at that bend. Nothing grows there. Not a blade of grass, not a weed, not a briar, nothing at all, and men have tried, mind, planted things there over the years just to see would it take, and it comes to nothing every single time. Bare clay the whole patch of it, same as it's always been said to be.

The second proof is the sort ye can never run down to the root of, the way these things go. Someone always knows someone who heard it off someone else again, a sham walking home alone from the far site who heard his own name called clear as day off the dark of the bend and legged it the whole road back without a look behind him. Ye never meet that fella himself, not once. It's always a friend of a friend, a cousin of a cousin, but it's told the very same every time it gets told, near enough word for word, and that alone is its own kind of proof to plenty.

But the one that's told in full, every single time, without a word left out of it, is what happened a relation of mine, years back now. Walking the tober alone, late, no moon in the sky that night at all, and clear as anything they heard their own moniker called out from off the path, in a voice they'd have sworn on their life belonged to somebody at home waiting on them. And they went. Three steps, four steps off the path toward it before something in them stopped dead and turned them round again, and they walked the rest of that road home faster than they'd ever walked it before or since, and never once looked back the whole way.

And to this day, if ye ask them straight out, they'll give ye the very same words every time, near enough that the whole family could say it along with them: that it was the exact voice, not a voice near it, not a voice like it, the exact voice, and that whatever it was that was calling them wasn't calling out of any kindness toward them.

Now the detail that stops even the doubters going quiet for a second, even the ones that swear they don't believe a word of it, is that bare patch of ground. A body can laugh off a voice they never heard themselves. It's harder to laugh off ground ye can walk out and look at with yer own two eyes, ground that won't take a seed no matter what's put into it, year after year.

And the detail that gives the doubters their opening, the very same detail every single time, is that it only ever happens to the one who's alone, and only ever in the dark, and there's never a witness standing there beside them to back it up after. Convenient, they'll say, and I won't stand here and tell ye they're wrong to say it.

The story changes a bit depending on whose mouth it's coming out of, and I won't pretend otherwise to ye. Some tell it softer, more of a warning than a fright, gentle with the children that are listening. Others tell it hard, meaning to put the fear well into ye and no apology going with it. I've heard it told both ways off the one teller inside the one year, and I've never once minded the difference. A story's let breathe like that, the same as anything living is.

But I'll tell ye the one thing that's never changed, not once, not in any telling, off any mouth, in all the years of it going round. If ye hear yer own name called from somewhere dark, ye don't answer it. Not a word out of ye, not a step toward it, till ye've laid yer own two eyes on who's doing the calling. That's held in this family longer than I've been alive to hold it meself.

And I'll say this much for meself, since ye've sat there so patient the whole way through. I don't fully believe it, not in the way I'd believe the kettle's hot or the rain's coming in off the water. But I've never once broken that rule in the whole of my life, and please God I never will.
