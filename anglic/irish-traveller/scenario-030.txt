Me: Right, are you going to give us that brack recipe or am I to be asking you till I'm grey. You're the only one left has it in your head.

The aunt: I have it in me head, God rest her, sure she never let a page near it in her life and neither will I. Sit down there now and don't be rushing me.

Me: I'm sitting.

The aunt: Right. First thing, the tea. Black tea, strong, near a pint of it, maybe a small bit over, and you pour it over the fruit the night before, not the morning of, the night before, mind.

Me: How much fruit in it.

The aunt: A pound of it, sultanas and the peel mixed. And, hold on now, I nearly went and left out the spice on you, God she'd have my ear, a good spoon of the mixed spice goes in along with the fruit steeping, I near forgot that every time meself.

Me: Okay. And then in the morning.

The aunt: In the morning you're after having it all dark and swole up nice, you don't be straining much off it, and you fold in your flour, your sugar, your egg, the bit of butter, easy now, don't be bating it like you'd bate a man that vexed you, just fold it round.

Me: What was the one thing she always warned ye never to rush.

The aunt: The baking of it. She'd say to me, don't be at that door checking it every five minutes, leave it in the low heat and let it come to itself in its own time, that's the one she'd nearly redden your ear over.

Me: How would ye know it was done so, without a clock on it.

The aunt: Ah sure there was never a clock working in that kena anyway. You'd know it by the smell filling the whole place, and the skewer in the middle, comes out clean, that's your answer.

Me: And the sugar shook over the top after.

The aunt: That part now I never once measured, only judge it by eye, a good shake till it looks right to you and no more.

Me: Could I use margarine itself instead of the butter.

The aunt: Ah you could, God love you, it'd do at a pinch. It's not the same only it'd do.
