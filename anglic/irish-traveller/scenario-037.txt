I was woke before the light was anywhere near itself, the mother's hand on my shoulder and she saying get up out of that now, we've a long tober ahead of us and the farmer wants us above in the field for when the sun's barely over the ditch. The kettle was already sung on the gas ring, she was after having tea wet for the whole kena before any of us had our eyes open proper. Cold as the grave outside, the cob stamping in the dark below at the rail and the dog going mad round everyone's legs like he knew rightly there was a day's gruber ahead.

The father come out pulling his boots on and he give the first job straight off.

The father: Right. Get the bags loaded, get the graips up, and whoever's making the tea best make it quick, we're not sitting here till noon.

We went in the trailer down, four of us and the youngest ones half asleep still wrapped in the one blanket between them. The little sublik was moaning he was starving and the little lackeen only wanted to sit on someone's lap, and the mother told them stop the whinging, there'd be jobs for the both of them soon enough that wouldn't break their backs.

Sure enough, once we hit the field and the farmer walked us down the first drills, it was the youngest ones set to the easy end of it, gathering what was already turned up loose along the top of the ridge into the small buckets, no digging in it at all, just bending and picking and not letting any go to waste in the muck. The lackeen took to it like a duck to water, singing away to herself, and the sublik went at it for about ten minutes flat out before he was asking could he stop for a rest. The mother only laughed and told him no such thing, that's a young feen's work now and he'd want to get used to it.

The heavy end of it went, as it always does, to whoever had the back and the shoulders for it. My brother, the biggest of us and only two years off being a man grown, was put on the digging itself, driving the graip in and turning the drill so the rest could go along after him gathering into the bags. That's brutal work, that is, all day bent into the one motion, and nobody envied him it though nobody said as much either.

The father: Go on so, put your back into it, we haven't the whole week.

My brother: I'm putting more into it than yourself is this minute, standing there giving out orders.

The father only laughed at that and went and took a graip himself, so it wasn't all talk out of him.

Once the first section of the field was cleared, a good strip of it, the ridges bare and the bags stacked at the headland waiting on the trailer, the whole shape of the work changed. Them that were bagging moved down to where my brother had already turned, and the ones who'd been gathering loose spuds off the top were set instead to carrying the full bags back to the headland, which is its own class of heavy going with the mud sucking at your boots the whole way.

It was round about then the old aunt, God bless her, started falling behind. She's not able the way she used to be, and she was still working away at the drill everyone else had long finished, not saying a word about it, just going slower and slower and her breath going on her. Nobody made any remark. My sister only drifted over quiet as anything and started working the same row alongside her, taking the far half of it without a word said either way, so the aunt wouldn't be left standing there on her own in a cleared field with everyone looking. That's just the way it's done. You don't call a body out for slowing, you go and help and say nothing about it after.

Round about eleven the mother and the farmer's wife brought the food out, brown bread and rashers wrapped up, a big flask of tea, and biscuits for the children. We sat in on the headland on whatever we could find, sacks and the trailer bed, and ate like we hadn't seen food in a week.

The mother: Get that into ye now, there's a long day left in it yet.

The sham working the far field with us, a fellow the father knew from years back, come over for his tea and started slagging my brother over how he was holding the graip.

The sham: Would you look at the state of that grip. No wonder you're wrecked already, you've it backwards in your two hands.

My brother: There's nothing backwards about it, I've turned more of this field than you have all morning.

The sham: Ah you have in your eye. Gloke at the drill behind you, half of it's still standing.

It went on like that a good while, more slagging than sense in it, till the father told the pair of them to eat up and get back to it before the farmer thought we were paid to be sitting round telling stories.

The sky had been iffy since we started, but round midday it properly turned, a black bank coming in low off over the hills and the wind lifting sudden, cold and wet-smelling.

The father: That's rain in that, and no small drop of it either. Get them bags under cover now, quick as ye can, and we'll shift down to the far end where there's some shelter off the hedge till it passes.

We hauled everything we could under the trees along the ditch and stood in tight while it came down proper for a good twenty minutes, the youngest ones delighted with the excuse for a rest and the rest of us just glad of the breather if truth be told, though nobody would say that out loud with the father standing there.

Once it cleared off, and it did clear, the sun coming back stronger than before if anything, we were straight back at it, and the pace after the rain was fiercer than the whole morning put together, everyone trying to make back the time that was lost standing under the hedge.

That's when the trouble come. One of the graips went and the head come clean off the handle, my brother nearly going over on his backside with the shock of it swinging loose in his hands.

My brother: That's the handle gone on this one, it's no good to me now at all.

The father went and had a look and pronounced it gammy beyond fixing on the spot, but the farmer had a spare below at the shed, so it was only a matter of one of the lads misli-ing over on the bike to solk it and bring it back, and we lost maybe twenty minutes over the whole business, which in a day like that isn't the end of the world though it felt like it at the time.

By mid-afternoon the light was starting its long slow going, the way it does, and the father come down the field calling out to everyone at once.

The father: Right, pick it up now, we've maybe two hours of light left and a fair whack of ground still to clear. No standing about.

And pick it up we did. Every one of us, the old aunt included, working flat to it, the bags filling faster than they'd filled all day, till there was only the one last strip left standing at the bottom of the field, and by then it wasn't a matter of who was doing what job any more, every hand that was on that field, young and old, the youngest ones included with their little buckets, converged on that last bit of ground together and had it stripped bare inside twenty minutes, laughing and shouting at each other the whole way through it, the last of the light going gold and low over the hedges.

The farmer come down himself to see the finish of it and counted the bags stacked along the headland with the father, the two of them going up and down the row with a bit of chalk.

The farmer: That's a fine tally out of the one field, fair play to the lot of ye.

There was gred handed over then, counted out into the father's hand, and a nod that we'd done a good honest day's gruber for him.

We were destroyed getting back into the trailer, every bone in the body aching, my brother nearly asleep standing up he was that flaked, and the old aunt sat down on the nearest thing and didn't move for a full ten minutes, only sighing away to herself, God bless her.

But before anyone was let sit properly down at the kena for their tea, the mother turned round at the youngest.

The mother: You. Go and get that cob in off the rail before it's pitch dark, and mind you tie him proper this time.

And off the sublik went, dragging his feet the whole way, while the rest of us finally let ourselves collapse.
