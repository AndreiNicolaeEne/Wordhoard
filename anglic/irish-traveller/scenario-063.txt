I'll tell you the whole of it now and you can make what you want of it after.

'Twas a shocking bad night, the wind that was in it, roaring round the site till you'd think the roof itself was lifting off the kena. I never closed an eye the whole night, God's truth, listening to it tearing at the awning and the dog going mental below in the shelter. And sure enough when the light come up and I went out in me bare feet to see was everything standing, there was the fence - our fence, mind, the one between us and the sham next door - flat as a table the whole width of it, posts snapped like matchsticks and the wire wound up in a heap like something after being chewed.

I stood there gloking at it a good while before I even seen what done it. There was a big old tree stretched clean across the two gardens, roots pulled half out of the ground, and I could see then it was after growing right along the boundary the whole time, one side of it hanging over our bit, the other over theirs. So whose tree was it at all? That wasn't an easy question, for the trunk of it was sat square on the line, near enough down the middle, God's honest truth.

I went over to the sham's kena, banged on the door, and out comes the neighbour himself, still in his vest, looking at me like I'd two heads on me at that hour.

Me: That tree of yours is after taking the whole fence down between us. You'll have seen the state of it.
The neighbour: That's no tree of mine, that's the wind done that, and the wind's not a thing you can be blaming on me now, is it.
Me: The tree's been leaning that way three year if it's been a day. Sure everyone on this site seen it leaning.
The neighbour: Leaning's not falling. I never touched the thing.

And that was the start of it, the two of us stood there in the cold with our arms folded, neither of us giving an inch. It was the old fella from the far end of the row that put his oar in first, walking up slow with the tea still in his hand.

The elder: Didn't I say to the pair of ye, God knows how long back, that tree'd be down on top of someone's kena one of these nights? I said it to your own mother, God rest her, and I said it to yourself after.
The neighbour: You said a lot of things over the years.
The elder: I said it plain enough. Ye all heard it and none of ye done a thing about it.

That took the wind out of him a small bit, but he wasn't for giving up the fight yet. We went back and forth the best part of that morning, standing out in the muck with the tea going cold in our hands, him saying it was an act of God and nothing owing, me saying a tree that's leaning that long and warned about isn't an act of anything only neglect. Neither of us was for being the one to back down first. You know the way it is - once you've said your bit in front of the whole site, you can't be seen to fold, not for the sake of the thing itself but for what people'd say after. I wasn't going to solk the whole cost of the fence on meself when the tree was half his, and he wasn't going to bug me a penny while he could still say it was only the weather.

It went on near a gammy old week like that, the two of us barely passing a word only what had to be passed, the fence still down and the both of us stepping round the gap in it, not misli-ing past it only when we had to. Then it was the same elder stepped in again, and no one had asked him to.

The elder: Now I'll tell the two of ye how this is going. The tree was on the line, so the tree was the two of ye's between ye, whatever way ye want to look at it. So the timber's split between ye and the cost of new posts is split between ye, and not another word said about whose fault the wind was, for the wind's not a feen you can bring before anyone. But the labour - the digging and the cutting and the carrying - that's split by what hands ye have, not by what money's owed. That way neither of ye's the one that gave in, and neither of ye's the one that's out of pocket more than the other.

And God bless him, it was the only thing that could have worked, for it let the both of us step back without either of us having to say we were wrong. The neighbour grumbled a bit about it being more than his half, and I grumbled a bit about it being more than mine, but sure that's only what you say, isn't it, so as not to look too pleased with the way out ye've been handed.

So the cost was settled, gred split down the line for the new posts and the wire, and it come to the clearing of it after. That first morning working alongside him was stiff as a board, the two of us out there with the saws going, not a word between us only "mind yourself there" and "hand us that end", the whole thing done in a class of a silence you could have cut with the same saw. His own young fella was out helping, and my own crowd, and the sublik and the lackeen from both sides kept well clear of the pair of us, sensing the way of it same as anyone would.

It was near midday when the neighbour's wife come out with a plate of bread and rashers for the lot of us, not asked for, not a word said only "there's tea in the pot after ye finish that log", and set it down on the wall same as if we'd never had a cross word in our lives. And a small bit after that the neighbour himself handed me the bow saw without me asking for it, when he seen mine was after going blunt on the big root, and that was it, really. That was the whole of it mended right there, over a plate of bread and a saw handed across a gap in a fence. Nothing was said about it, no apologies going round, only the work going easier after and the talk starting up between us again, ordinary talk, about the site and the trotting and one thing and another.

We had the new fence up inside the two days, and it's a finer job than the old one ever was, straighter posts, better wire, dug in deeper on account of we weren't taking any chances a second time. The trotting cobs from both sides graze the one bit of grass along it now same as always, and there's not a soul that'd misli down that tober and know to look at the two of us that there was ever a cross word said, only the odd time himself'll say, going by, "that fence'll outlast the pair of us," and I'll say back "please God it will," and that's as near as either of us comes to talking about it at all. Better than it was before if anything, and that's the God's truth of it.
