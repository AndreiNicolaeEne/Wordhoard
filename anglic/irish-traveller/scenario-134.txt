I never seen a year like it for the spuds, not in my whole time going for the digging. Herself and myself were up beyond Galway on the same ground we always go to, and it was clear from the first fortnight that this one was leaving every other year standing. Near double what we'd be pulling of a normal year, and that's no aul talk.

It was the main crop done it, the one field above the house. Every other year you'd be glad of a fair yield off it, but this time it come in far past anything, past even the best that was ever put down in the farmer's own book before, and that's the best part of twenty year back now.

We'd to bring in more hands than ever was needed before, my brother and the sham beside him and two of the cousins off the far site, all misli'd up the tober to get stuck into it before it'd turn on us in the ground. My brother let the cob stand a good while, no evenings for the sulky with all that wanted doing that autumn. Thank God we got the whole lot in before the weather broke on us.

Farmer: Ye'll not shift that lot on yer own, get whoever ye can.

Me: We'll bug in the whole crowd so, there's plenty about glad of the work.

Once what was needed for ourselves and for the farmer's own use was seen to, the rest was clamped down for the winter, and a good share traded on besides, good gred in it too, to shops and to families on other sites glad of it.

The eldest among the growers, after that, set it against his whole life at the work and called it, hand on heart, the best he ever seen off that ground. He's after crediting the rain for coming at the right time in the spring of it, and a soft stretch right through the summer after, no hard frost ever came near it to knock it back.

They're still measuring every harvest since against that one. Whatever year it is, someone'll say, aye, but was it as good as that year, and there's never an answer but no.
