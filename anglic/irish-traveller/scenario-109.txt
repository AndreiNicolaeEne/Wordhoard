The latch went on the door before ever a knock come, and there he was stood in it with the bag on his shoulder, and for the length of a breath nobody knew him at all, and then everybody knew him at the one time.

The mother: Mother of God and all the saints, is that yourself standing there?

The father: Would ye look. Would ye look what's after landing on us.

The mother: It's him! It's him, I'm telling ye, it's himself that's in it!

She had him near knocked back into the door frame with the dint of the hug she put on him, and she wasn't for letting go of it either, holding on a good while past what an embrace wants to be, her face pressed into his coat like she was making sure of him with more than the two eyes.

The mother: Look at the size of ye. Would ye look at the size of the man that's after coming back to me. Sure I sent out a gasúr and there's a feen after walking in the door on me.

The aunt: I'm not able, I'm not able at all -

The father: Ah here now, don't be starting -

The aunt: I said I wasn't going to and look at me -

She had the back of her hand up to her eye and was trying to laugh through it at the same time, and the laugh kept losing.

The father: Eight years. Eight years and he strolls in the door of a Tuesday like he was above at the shop for the messages.

The brother: I was going to ring ahead of me but sure I said I'd surprise ye.

The mother: Surprise us! Musha, I near went through the floor.

The father: Well sham. Come here to me. God bless you and keep you, we near had ye given up for one of the shades had ye below in England, the length of time that was in it.

The brother: No fear of that. I was only working, that's all it was, one job running into the next one and never the fare spare to come home on till now.

The little one hung back by the mother's leg the whole time, half behind her apron, giving him the eye the way you'd give the eye to a stranger that everyone else was making an unmerciful fuss over.

The mother: Go on and say hello to him. That's your own uncle there.

The little one: I don't know him.

The mother: Ah you do, you were only a baba the last time he was in it, sure how would you know him.

The brother: She's grand, don't be pushing her. Sure I'm a stranger to her and that's the truth of it.

The eldest: You're after changing something shocking anyway. The shoulders on you. You went out of here a young fella and you're after coming back a grown man on us.

The brother: Ye haven't changed a bit, any of ye. This place hasn't changed a stitch either, only -

The father: Only what?

The brother: Only the wall's after getting a new coat of paint on it, and there's a caravan up the top end I don't know at all, and where's the two cobs got to?

The father: One of them's after being sold on, we've only the one now, out the back field there, and he's not fit for the sulky the way he used to be, he's getting on same as the rest of us.

The brother: And the tea in this house still smells the very same. That's the one thing hasn't shifted an inch. Soon as I came in the door I got the smell of it and I near went back to being a young fella again.

The mother: Sit down there so and I'll wet a drop for you this minute, don't be standing on ceremony.

Nobody moved the bag off the floor by the door the whole time, it just sat there where he'd let it drop, forgotten about entirely, while the talk went ten ways at once around it.

The cousin: Did they tell you the young one got married? Below in Galway, back in the springtime.

The brother: Married! Which young one is this now?

The cousin: Sure the young one, you know the one, she'd only be a lackeen when you went, and now she's after having a child of her own on top of it.

The eldest: And the family below the road, they're after moving off that site entirely, gone up to the new one this two year.

The father: And the young lad next door to us, do you remember him no higher than my knee, he's after growing into a great strap of a sublik, taller than his own father now.

The brother: Stop. Ye're firing it at me faster than I can hold it.

The mother: Sure there's eight years of it to catch you up on, what do you expect.

The father: And you, look at the state of you, we'll have to feed you up proper, you're after coming home to us like a greyhound.

The mother: He is not, leave the man alone, he's only tired off the road.

The brother: The tober was long enough getting here, I won't say otherwise.

The father: Well sit down and rest yourself so, there's nowhere you have to be tonight bar here.

The aunt, still dabbing at her eye but getting the better of it now: How long have we you for this time. Tell us straight, don't be giving us the runaround on it.

The brother: I don't rightly know yet. A good while, please God. Longer than I've managed before, anyway.

The mother: Please God is right. We'll take whatever length of it we're given and be glad of it.

The father: We'll want a bit of a gathering got up for you so, will we. Get the word round, get a few bodies in.

The mother: I'm already at it in my head. I'll have a dinner on for tomorrow, the whole crowd of them in, and we'll not be short of it either.

The father: There's gred enough for a feed at least, don't be worrying yourself over that end of it.

The brother: Ye don't have to be going to any bother over me.

The mother: Bother! Will you listen to him. Sure isn't it eight years of bother we're owed, and we'll be taking every bit of it back off you now you're in it.

The father: Do you remember what your own grandfather used call you, before he went, God rest him?

The brother: Don't.

The father: The long streak of nothing, that's what he had you as, and look at the long streak of nothing now, home to us at last.

The brother: I do remember it well. I haven't heard that this long while and it near put a lump in my own throat hearing it said again.

Nobody that hadn't been sitting at that table years back would have known what the father meant by it at all, and nobody stopped to explain it either, the words just passed round the room and everyone that knew them smiled the same smile at the one time.

And then for one minute the whole room went quiet, no one saying a word, only looking at him the way you'd look at something you were half afraid would go again if you took your eyes off it, the mother with her hand still resting on his arm, the aunt with the tears drying on her, the little one peeping out now from behind the apron with a bit more daring in her, and himself sat there in the middle of it all letting himself be looked at.

The mother: Well. Sit there now and drink your tea while it's hot, and don't be minding the rest of us, we'll all settle down to ourselves in a minute.

And they did settle, the whole crowd of them, the talk dropping down easy after that first great rush of it, into the kind of quiet, ordinary talk that goes on between people that are only delighted to have the time in front of them to say it in.
