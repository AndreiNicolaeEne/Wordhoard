I called the whole of them into the good trailer of a Sunday evening, sent the eldest's own sublik out to the green to mind the small ones, and yous could tell before I opened me mouth at all that it wasn't for the aul craic. Nobody touched the tea. The father had his eyes down on the floor already, the way a man does be when he's felt the weather turning before the first drop of it lands on him.

Me: There's something I've kept back off yous for years now, and it's owed, and I'm saying it tonight if it's the last thing I do.

Nobody answered me a word. I stood, for I couldn't have sat easy through the whole of it.

Me: Ye all mind the year the good cob went missing on us. The one we reared ourselves up from a foal, that we'd near every spare bit of gred we had put by on for the trotting that summer. Ye mind how she went off the tether one morning and how it come out after that she was sold, papers signed and all, and not one of us asked leave of.

Nobody spoke back. The brother had gone very still in the chair, the stillness of a man that's heard a story land on him a hundred times before and knows the shape of it before it's said.

Me: And ye all put it on him. The whole of yous, so ye did. Because he was seen boxing her up the evening before and said he was only walking her out, and because he had the name already for mixing with lads that'd solk the coat off yer back if ye turned it to them, and it was easier believe it of him than go looking any further for it. And he was never forgave for it since, not rightly, not once. I've heard it said of him at wakes, at weddings, low enough that they thought I wasn't listening, the one that sold the cob, and him stood there in the room to hear it said.

The brother: I'm well used to it by now, sure.

Me: Well ye needn't be used to it no more. Because it wasn't you sold her. It was never you. And I'm after knowing that the whole time.

The mother went the colour of the cloth on the table.

The mother: What are ye saying to me now.

Me: I'm saying it wasn't him. It was the father sold her.

There come a quiet down over that trailer the like of which I never heard in it before, not even the night the aul fella above was waked in it.

The mother: You don't know what you're saying to me.

Me: I do know what I'm saying, and there's no need to gloke at me like that either, for I'm certain of it. I'm not coming out with this off a guess, or a bit of talk I picked up below at the mission. I'm telling ye I know it, because I was told it meself, straight out, by the one that done it, and I've carried it since I was no more than a lackeen, and it's near broke me carrying it this long.

I could see the eldest's face going through it then too, the way ye'd watch someone add a sum up in their own head, and I knew well what she was at, going back over every hard word she ever give the brother, every do she never asked him to, and I got in ahead of her before she could say a syllable of it herself.

Me: And don't be tormenting yerself over it now either. Ye weren't to know. None of yous were. Ye only had what ye were give to go on, and ye went on it, the same as I'd have gone on it meself if I hadn't known better.

The eldest said nothing back, only nodded, but I seen the water come up in her eyes all the same.

Me: The father come to me the week it happened. Sat me down below by the tober where nobody'd hear the two of us, and he told me straight he'd sold her to settle a debt he had with a feen that wasn't the sort of article ye left owing him gred, a feen the shades had their eye on already for worse than that. And he made me swear on Our Lady I'd say nothing, that it'd blow over, that she was only an animal after all and we'd rear on another. And I swore it to him. I was a lackeen and he was me father, and when yer father tells ye swear a thing, ye swear it.

The father hadn't lifted his head the whole time. Not the once.

Me: And I nearly told it years back and all, at the brother's own wedding no less, when he stood up to make his speech and thanked nobody in this room for standing by him, because not one of yous did, and I had it sitting on the tip of me tongue that very night, I swear before God I did, and I looked over at the father across the table and I lost me nerve and I let it go by me. I'm sorry for that. I'm sorry for every year that come after it and all.

The brother had gone very quiet now, his jaw working the way it does when he's holding something back.

Me: I kept it because I was afeared of what it'd do, opening the whole of it up again after it had finally gone quiet between yous, and because I didn't want to be the one brought more trouble down on this family, and if I'm honest with ye, because I was trying to protect him. (I couldn't say the father's word for himself just that minute, only nodded at him.) But I've watched the brother carry a moniker that was never his the whole of his life. Watched him not let near a match on account of it. Watched him get skimished harder than he ever needed to, because sure what odds, the name was gone on him anyway. And I've had enough of it. I've had enough of carrying it meself and all.

The brother put his two hands over his face, and when he took them down there was a class of relief on him, God's honest truth there was, but there was a fresh raw anger sitting up under it too, the two of them fighting for the one bit of room on his face.

The brother: All them years. All them years I took it off every one of yous and said nothing, for what was I going to say against it, and the whole time you knew.

Me: I did.

The brother: The whole time.

Me: I did, and I'm sorry for it, and sorry's not near enough, I know that well, but it's what I have to bug ye.

The brother got up out of the chair then and stood a minute like he might misli straight out the door of it, and maybe he'd have been right to, but he only stood there, breathing hard, looking at nobody in particular.

Then every eye in that trailer went over to the father, and he lifted his head at last, and I'll say the rest of it plain the way I said all the rest.

The father: I done what I done because I was fixed gammy that time and no other way out of it that I could see, and I'm not proud of it. And I let it sit on you (a nod at the brother, and he couldn't hardly get the word kam out of himself, but it come) because it was easier than facing what I'd done meself, and that's the shame of it, and I've carried that shame longer than any of yous knew, only mine was a shame I chose and his was one I never earned.

The mother's hands started up shaking then, and the anger that come into her face wasn't the old anger, worn thin off years of being spent on the brother for nothing. It was a fresh one entirely, and it went at last where it belonged.

The mother: All them years I was cold to him in this family. All them years I let it be said in front of me at gatherings and never once put a stop to it. And it was you the whole time, sat there letting me do it to him.

The father said nothing back to that. There was nothing for him to say to it.

Me: I'm sorry too, Mammy. I should have said it long before now, I know that well. I'm not saying it to make it easier on meself, for I know rightly it can't be undone, the years are gone whatever way ye look at it. But ye all needed to hear it, and it's said now, it's out of me, and it can't be put back in.

Nobody cried out loud in it, but there was a fair few eyes wet round that trailer by the end of the telling, and nobody moved to put the kettle on for the longest while, which if ye knew this family at all ye'd know was the strangest part of the whole night. It's not sorted, none of it. Not the years the brother lost carrying a name that was never his to carry. Not the years the mother spent hard on him for nothing. Not the years I spent minding a secret that was never mine to mind either way. It's not tidy and it won't be made tidy, not this side of the grave. But we're standing on the truth of it now, the whole family of us, instead of standing round it the way we done before, and God knows that has to count for something, even this late in the day of it.
