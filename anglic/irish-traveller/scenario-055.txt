Wisha, my friend, you missed a right aul day of it, I'll tell ye that much for nothing. We were meant to be out of the old place and above in the new by the dinner hour, that was the plan the father had in his head anyway, but sure plans is only for laughing at after.

We were meant up at six, and it was near half eight before a sinner stirred, because the young fella was after being sick in the night with his chest and none of us got a wink till gone three. So there's the whole morning gone before it started, and the van fella beeping below at the gate at half seven like we'd forgot him entirely.

And then before I was even out the door, would ye credit it, the key of the old lock went and snapped in my hand, sitting there in two halves in my palm like it was codding me. Stood there in me nightdress still near enough, gloking at it, saying to myself, that's a great start now, that is.

The mother: What in the name of God is wrong with ye standing there gawking, get the pliers off your father and stop the drama.

Didn't have to ask a soul for a hand, that's the God's honest truth, because my sister's man was above at the gap before the tay was even wet, hadn't a word said to him, just seen the van and the boxes out on the grass and came straight down off the site to us. A good sham like that, ye don't forget it.

Her brother-in-law: Sure I seen the van and says I, they'll want another pair of hands so they will, no bother to me at all.

Now the good statue of Our Lady, the one that was on the dresser since the eldest was made her Communion, that was wrapped in nothing only an old towel because I hadn't the bubble wrap bought, and didn't the young lad go and set the box down that hard on the step that we heard the crack of it from inside the house.

The eldest: JAYSIS WILL YE MIND WHAT YOU'RE CARRYIN', THAT'S ME NANNY'S STATUE!

And the shout that went up out of that girl, ye'd swear the roof was after falling in on top of us. Half the site must have heard her below in it. And sure enough, when we opened the box, the head was clean off Our Lady and rolling round in among the tay towels like it hadn't a care in the world. The eldest crying into the towel over it and the mother trying to stick it back with superglue and it never sitting right after, ye could always see the crack in her neck, God love her.

And there was a box gone missing that whole day and it never turned up since, not to this hour. The good delph, the set we got the year of the wedding, packed careful as anything into a box marked KITCHEN in the black marker, and somewhere between the old kitchen and the new one that box walked. Might be it fell off the tailboard on the tober and some other crowd has it in their own press this minute, God bless them and their good delph now.

The father: I'm telling ye I put it on meself, I seen it with me own two eyes going up on that van.

The mother: Well it never came off it then, because it's not here and it's not there, and I'm not codding ye, I've the whole place turned upside down looking.

The big row of the day though was over the wardrobe, the good one, the heavy aul solid timber one that was my mother's before me, God rest her. The father wanted it carried flat on its back and the sham wanted it up on its end, and the two of them roaring at each other over it in the front garden like it was a matter for the shades to be called out over.

The father: Ye'll have the legs off it carrying it that way, put it flat, will ye listen to me for once.

Her brother-in-law: Flat it'll never go round that corner, sure look at the size of the thing, up on its end is the only way it's going anywhere.

And wasn't the sham dead right, only then didn't the whole lot of them meet their match at the stairs door below. That wardrobe went at that doorway every way God made a wardrobe to go, sideways, on the flat, up on the end, tipped this way and that, and it wouldn't budge through, not an inch. Twenty minutes we were at it, the four of them sweating and cursing and the mother above on the step giving out directions no one was listening to, and in the end the father had to take the door itself clean off its hinges before that yoke would come through at all. Gammy aul doorway, built for nothing bigger than a matchbox by the looks of it.

And of course, wasn't that the very minute the heavens opened on us. Black clouds after building up over the site all morning and not a soul paying them any heed, and the second that wardrobe was out on the path the rain came down like it was sent special for us, soaking every stick of furniture we had left out on the grass, mattresses and all. The young lad legging it round trying to throw tarpaulin over the half of it, roaring at everyone to hurry up.

Middle of all that, with the rain lashing and everyone soaked through and cross as two sticks with each other, the mother comes out with a great pile of ham sandwiches and a flask of tay, and God, my friend, I'm telling ye, nothing before or since ever tasted the way that tay tasted, standing there among the boxes in the rain eating a sandwich off a paper plate balanced on a chest of drawers. Saved the whole afternoon, that did. The sham ate about six sandwiches on him, the hunger of him.

Her brother-in-law: Best feed I had in a fortnight, so it was, God bless the hand that made it.

It was the last few loads that near finished the lot of us though. Ye'd swear the boxes got heavier the fewer of them there was left, and every one of us dragging ourselves in and out of that van like we'd aged ten year in the one day. The father sat down on the step at one stage and says nothing at all, only put his head in his two hands, and that man is never one to sit down for anything.

Before we locked her up for the last time I walked through the old rooms on me own, every one of them bare and echoing, the marks still on the walls where the pictures hung, the press in the kitchen with the door swung open and nothing in it, and I don't know what came over me, my friend, but I stood in the middle of the front room a good while, just looking, thinking of every Christmas and every wake and every row that room ever held. Quare aul feeling, that. Then I pulled the door after me and that was that, gone.

We didn't reach the new place till long after dark, the whole lot of us wringing wet and wrecked walking, and when we come to count what we had off that van against what went out the old door that morning, sure we were short the good delph and short the head off Our Lady and short whatever else fell out along the tober that we haven't even missed yet. Less than we started with, God knows, but we were in, and that was the main of it. Put the kettle on again the minute we were inside, for there's nothing else to be done after a day the like of that only sit down and be glad it's over.
