I'm after minding a house a full week, beyond in the town, the two that own it gone off above to Galway for a wedding and a removal both, and they after asking me special because they know our own crowd and trust us more than they'd trust half the settled ones round them, God bless them for it, though by the end of the seven days I was fit to go beyond in myself.

First night I hadn't the bags down proper when I knew there was something not right about the place. Not a bad thing, only a strange one, the back door wouldn't sit flush in the frame, kept easing itself open a crack no matter how I turned the key, gammy from the day I walked in, and the whole house had that quiet a house gets when it's not used to you being in it, every creak of the floor sounding like someone else's foot below in it. I lay there half the night with one ear cocked, telling meself sure it's only the house settling, and getting up twice all the same to check was the door shut.

Second day and I'm not five minutes with the tea in me hand when the dog has the gate open, someone, and I won't say who, hadn't the latch fastened proper the evening before, and he's off down the road like he was solked out of a cannon. I was after him in me slippers, roaring, the whole tober out looking at the poor Traveller woman chasing a mad dog down through the estate, and didn't he only go and corner himself in behind a wall with a bitch three doors down, delighted with himself entirely. Had him back on the lead inside the hour, panting, the two of us, checked was his moniker still on the tag, God between us and all harm, and I told him straight, if he ever tried to misli off on me again I'd leave him go to Galway on his own four legs.

Now the garden I didn't think much of at the start of the week, only that it wanted a drop of water of an evening the way she'd shown me. But it crept up on me gradual, by the Wednesday the tomato plants beyond in the little tunnel yoke were gone that limp and yellow, sure ye'd never credit it, I near cried looking at them, and I hadn't a clue was it too much water or too little, so I gave them a drop more just in case, which I found out after was the very wrong thing to do. By the Friday there was slugs on them the size of me thumb and I was out there in the dark with a torch picking them off with a fork, cursing the whole lot of them, plants and slugs and myself for ever saying I'd mind the place.

The washing machine went on me the same Wednesday, mid-cycle, a great bang out of it and then nothing, the drum stopped dead with a wash of her good sheets sitting sodden inside in it. I hadn't the first notion what was wrong with it. Rang me own mother.

Me: There's a bang after coming out of it and it's dead now, not a stir out of it.

My own mother: Is the plug hanging out of the socket, love, did ye check?

Me: I did, it is not.

My own mother: Well then leave it so, don't be footering with the settled feen and beoir's machine, ye hear me, get the sheets out by hand and hang them and say nothing till she's home.

Which is exactly what I did, wrung the head off meself getting those sheets dry, and never breathed a word about it after.

The visitor came on the Thursday, no warning given, a knock on the door about four in the evening and I nearly jumped out of me skin, thinking it was the owners home early to catch me at something. It was only a neighbour woman from two doors down, come to leave in a parcel that was delivered to her by mistake.

The neighbour: I seen the light on and I said I'd leave this in rather than have it sitting on my hall table another week.

Me: God bless you, come in for a drop of tea so.

The neighbour: I will not, I've the dinner on, but tell them I called when they're back.

I told her I would, and shut the door after her thinking, small towns, everyone minding everyone's business, God love them.

It was that same evening I got the desperate jolt. I went looking in the good room for the remote control and I couldn't find it high up or low down, and then I seen a little china figure, a dog it was, gone from the mantelpiece, and me heart went into me mouth thinking I'd knocked it off meself without noticing and it broke into pieces somewhere and swept up by accident along with something else. I was on me hands and knees under the couch, behind the curtains, in a desperate state altogether, sure I was after ruining something dear to her that could never be got again in this life. Found it in the end wrapped up in newspaper in a drawer, she'd taken it down herself before she left to keep it safe from the cat knocking it, and never thought to tell me. I sat down on the good chair after that and said a prayer of thanks, I was that relieved.

The locking out happened on the Saturday. I went out the back with a basket of washing, pulled the door to behind me the way ye would without thinking, and didn't it lock itself, and me spare key inside on the counter where I'd left it not an hour before, sure I'd be straight back in. Three hours I was standing about that garden, no phone on me either, it inside on the counter along with the key, thinking would I flag one of the shades passing only there wasn't one to be seen, of course, when ye'd want one. In the end a young sham passing on his bike went off and got his own mother who had a spare cut years back for emergencies. I hadn't a bit of gred on me to give him for his trouble, only thanked him from the heart, and I never was as glad to see a stranger's face in me life.

The rain got in on the Friday night through a window I'd left cracked in the small back room, only an inch, for the air, not thinking there'd be a downpour before morning. Woke to the carpet in there sodden and a smell off it already starting, and spent half the Saturday morning with towels down and the window wide open the other way trying to dry it out before it left a mark or a mould on the wall.

There was a noise in the night as well, the Sunday going into the Monday, a great scraping and knocking below in the kitchen that had me up out of the bed with me heart going ninety, sure there was someone below in the house that shouldn't be. Crept down with only the landing light to go by, ready to let a roar out of me, and it was only the wheelie bin blown over against the back step by the wind, rolling itself against the wall over and over. I stood there in the cold in me nightdress laughing at meself, half cross and half relieved, and went back up.

The Monday I finally sorted the back door that had been at me the whole week, easing open, letting a draught and half me sleep with it, found the latch was only loose in the frame, gammy the way I'd thought from the first night, and tightened the screws on it meself with a small screwdriver I found in a drawer, and it sat shut after that the way a door should, no bother at all.

The last day I was up early tidying like a woman possessed, hoovering carpets that were dry as a bone by then thank God, straightening cushions, checking every room twice over, gave the whole place one last gloke before I'd let meself sit, the good china dog back up on the mantelpiece exactly where it wasn't when I found it, the garden watered proper, the dog washed and combed and looking like butter wouldn't melt in him.

When the two of them came home that evening I had the kettle on and the whole place shining, and the man of the house looked round him well pleased.

The man of the house: Any bother at all while we were gone?

Me: Not a bit of it. Quiet as the grave the whole week.

And I never told them a word about the washing machine, or the china dog, or the three hours in the garden, or the rain in the back room, only smiled and wished them well of the wedding, and was glad beyond telling to be heading back to me own kena that night, where a door is a door and stays shut when ye shut it.
