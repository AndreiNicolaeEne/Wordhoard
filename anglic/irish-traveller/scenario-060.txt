Right, I'll tell you the whole of the day now, the way it went, because sure you weren't there for any of it and you should hear it proper from someone that was.

The house is gone, that's the top and bottom of it. The grandfather passed on it, God rest him, back in the spring, and the grandmother's beyond in the home now, God spare her, and she wouldn't know one day off another no more, the poor woman. There was no sense in the world keeping a kena standing there empty with money going out on it for nothing, and none of us settled house-side this long while anyway, the whole crowd of us between the site above in Dublin and the one out past Galway. So it was only right it was let go. But you don't just misli off and lock a door on a house like that, not with what's in it, not with three generations of stuff sitting in presses and under beds. So we gathered, more than just ourselves, the aunts and the cousins and a couple of the neighbours off the old site that knew them well, and we were at it early, before the day got any heat in it.

We started in the kitchen, because sure you have to start somewhere and that's the room everyone lands in first anyway. The aunt was the one put her hand up into the press over where the range used to be, and out she comes with the old brown teapot, the one with the chip out of the spout you'd cut your lip on if you weren't minded of it, and she went very quiet over it. That was the pot, she said, that the grandmother would have the tea drawn in for anyone came to the door, feen or beoir, didn't matter who you were, there was always a sup going in that kitchen.

The aunt: God, would you have a gloke at that. I'm not after seeing that in I don't know how long. She'd have that going morning noon and night, that pot, and never once did she let it run dry on a body.

Then there was a thing nobody could account for at all, a small tin box back of the cutlery drawer, and inside it a fistful of coins none of us knew the country of, and a broken medal on a bit of ribbon gone black with age. We passed it round the whole morning and there wasn't one of us could solk a name on where it came from or whose it was at all.

The uncle: Whose is that, so? That's none of ours.

The cousin: Maybe it was the grand-uncle's, the one went for the boats.

The uncle: Not at all, he never set foot in this house in his life.

Nobody solved it. It went into a bag with the rest of the things going to nobody in particular.

The front room was next, and that's where the row started, over the dresser, the good one with the glass front the grandmother kept her delph in, the sort you'd only ever look at as a child and never touch. The eldest wanted it for herself, being the eldest, and the aunt said she'd more room for it, having the bigger trailer, and it went back and forth a good while between the two of them.

The eldest: I'm the one reared in this house half me life, I should have the say on it.

The aunt: And I'm the one with a trailer it'll fit into, so what use is the say to you if it's sitting out in the rain and the legs gone gammy off it inside a month.

In the end it was settled the eldest would have it, and the aunt's sham would run it over in the van and set it up for her himself, and that was the end of that, no bad blood in it by dinnertime.

The box room after that had the disagreement, and this one never did get settled proper at all. There was an old suitcase up on top of the wardrobe stuffed with harness leather and betting dockets off the grandfather's trotting days, half of it gone soft with the damp, and half the crowd wanted it straight out and the other half wouldn't hear of it.

My brother: That's only rubbish, bin that.

The uncle: That's your grandfather's whole life in that case, the cob and the sulky and all of it, you'll not be putting a hand near it.

It sat in the hall the whole day, that case, neither thrown nor kept, until someone's fella just solked it into the back of a car when nobody was looking, and that was the end of the argument, if you could call it settled.

It was in the same room, under a loose board the uncle only noticed because his foot went through it, that we bugged onto what nobody expected: a stocking, and inside it more gred than any of us thought the old pair ever had by them, folded notes gone soft with age, and under that a bundle of letters tied with a bit of wool, in a hand none of us knew.

The aunt: God between us and all harm, would you look what she had hid and never a word out of her, the whole of her life.

The mother: She was reared by the nuns beyond at the mission, she'd have learned it off them, hide it well and say nothing to anyone.

The gred went aside for the grandmother's own use, for whatever she'd want above in the home, and the letters nobody read out loud, they went into a box for herself if ever she'd ask for them, no moniker on the outside of the bundle at all, only the wool tied round it.

The good room was the one took the whole rest of the morning and half the afternoon besides, because that room was never emptied in its life, only added to, every holy picture, every photo off every wall, every ornament on the mantel, and not one of us could lift a single thing out of it without somebody stopping to tell a story over it, this one off a wedding, that one off a wake, the Pope above the fire since before any of us were born. We were only after starting on the photographs when the light was going soft outside already.

We broke around midday and ate together out in the yard, the aunt after bringing sandwiches and a flask big enough for the lot of us, and someone's youngster running round the wall of the site next door roaring at the ponies, and for a while nobody spoke of the house at all, only ate and slagged one another the way ye would any other day.

The youngest: Is there any more of that cake going, or has himself ate the whole of it on us?

The uncle: There's more in the bag, would ye leave a bit for the rest of us for once in your life.

After the break we got the wardrobe out of the main bedroom, the big one with the mirror gone spotted and one door hanging gammy on its hinge, and not one soul wanted it, too big for any trailer going and no use to anyone, so it went out the door between the four of us cursing it the whole way and left at the side of the road for whoever'd take scrap off it.

There wasn't a bit of argument over the grandfather's own things though. His cap and the blackthorn stick he'd have with him every day of his life, they went to the youngest without a word said, everyone only nodding like it was decided long before anyone had opened their mouth on it.

The main bedroom stood empty last of all, the two of them's own room, and it went quieter doing that one than any room in the house, nobody saying much, only folding and lifting and carrying out, and even the ones that'd be great for the slagging all day went short of words in it.

What was left after that got spread out on the kitchen table, bits and pieces nobody had claimed, and everyone took what they fancied off it, no fighting left in anyone by then, only the tiredness in them, and someone laughing that the grandfather himself would come home skimished off the tober some fair days and never miss half of what was in that house anyway, God rest him.

And when it was all out and the rooms standing bare and echoing, we stood a minute in the hall, the whole crowd of us, and someone said a prayer over it, God bless the house and all that ever lived and died in it, and the door was pulled to and locked for the last time, and we walked away and left it standing there empty behind us.
