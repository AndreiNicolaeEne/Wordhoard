The sky went a quare colour on us that evening, a greeny yellow class of light you'd never trust in your life, and not a bird stirring nor a dog barking below on the site, and I says to himself, there's something coming down the tober this day, God between us and all harm, for I never seen the like of it in all my years on that ground.

We hadn't the hour barely gone by when the wind was after rising up something shocking, and I had the eldest out with me hauling in whatever could still be hauled, the buckets off the step, the washing off the line, the good chairs from outside the kena, and himself away above trying to get the cobs settled, the poor craythurs pulling something desperate at their tethers, for they knew well what was coming before we did ourselves. No sulky trotting that evening, only the animals roaring and the whole site running about trying to solk in whatever wasn't nailed down.

Me: Get them buckets in outta that now, quick, before it lifts the head off ye.

The eldest: The shed door's after banging itself half off the hinges, will I go at it?

Me: Ye'll leave it be and get in, I'm not losing a child over a shed door.

By the time we had the last of it in, sure the wind was really rising then, a wild sound on it, and I had the whole of them gathered into the one room, the young kam under one arm and the lackeen under the other, and the youngest roaring crying into my chest, and I telling them it'd pass, it'd pass, though God knows I hadn't the first notion would it or wouldn't it.

The lights went then. One minute we had them and the next we were sat in the dark bar the one candle himself found in the press, and the wind after getting that loud you couldn't hear yourself think let alone talk, so we all only sat in against the wall, the whole lot of us pressed together the way you'd press in at a match, and not a word passed between us, for there was no word to be heard over it, the wind had the whole of the talk taken off us.

And then didn't we hear it, a great crack like the world itself splitting, and a whump after it that shook the floor under us, and I knew well that was a tree gone down, and close, closer than I'd ever want a tree to be going down. The lackeen let a screech out of her you'd hear in Galway, and I only held them tighter and said a prayer under my breath, God spare us, God spare us, for I won't tell a lie, I thought that night we might be going the same road as that tree.

Then there was a tearing sound above us, a class of a rip you'd feel in your own chest, and himself said after, quiet, that's the roof gone, and sure enough didn't a strip of it solk clean off into the dark, and the rain came in on top of us in a sheet, cold as anything, and the young ones roaring again, and I roaring at himself to get something over the gap, anything, a tarp, a door, whatever was to hand.

The husband: Bug me that bit of board there, quick, before the whole thing's away on us!

He was out in it near an hour, holding that board up against the hole with his own two arms, drenched to the skin, God love him, and not a word of complaint out of him the whole time, only working.

The one window in the back room cracked clean across, a great long crack in it like a bolt of lightning drawn on the glass, and I was sure, I was dead sure, it was going in on top of us, and I had the young ones turned away from it and my own self near half in front of it, God forgive me for not thinking straighter, but a mother doesn't think, she only does. It held though. Cracked and held, and I've never in my life been so thankful for a bit of glass.

I'll say now what I wouldn't have said that night, for you don't be saying it in front of the children, you don't be letting them see it on you. I was fierce afraid. Afraid the whole kena would go over on top of us, afraid we'd be out in that wind with nowhere to put ourselves, afraid for himself out there holding that board to the roof, afraid, God forgive me, it wouldn't be only the shed door we'd be burying before the week was out. You don't say them things at the time. You only say them after, when it's safe to say them, and even then you're near ashamed of the words.

And then, as sudden as it started, didn't it just go quiet. Not gradual, quiet, sudden quiet, the kind that's nearly worse for a minute, for you're waiting on it starting up again, and nobody moved nor spoke, we only sat there listening to nothing, the rain gone to a soft class of a patter.

The husband: I think that's her passed over us now.

We all sat another minute not believing it, and then we went out to gloke at the state of it.

God, the state of it. The big tree above the site was down clean across the tober, the only way in or out of the place blocked solid, and we'd not a hope of shifting it ourselves, that was a job for the crowd with the chainsaws in the morning. A feen from two doors down was already out with his own saw at first light, working away at it bit by bit so the cars could get through, and a beoir from further down the row came round with a flask of tea for the lot of us standing about watching him. The roof, well, you could see the sky through the one corner of it plain as day, and that was no patch job, that was real gruber ahead of us, real gred before that house would be fit again. And the window held its crack the whole night, a great scar down the glass of it, but it held.

We weren't the worst of it on the site either, I'll say that much. The neighbour's fence was flattened the whole length of it, not a post left standing.

The neighbour: Would ye gloke at that, not a post standing, the whole thing gone.

Me: Sure that's only a fence, would ye look at the roof on me.

And we near laughed, the pair of us, the way you do when you're only glad to be standing there at all looking at anything, gammy night or no gammy night.

The shades came round the second day checking was anyone hurt, for word had gone round about the tree, and I told them we were shook but standing, and that was the truth of it. The woman above from the missions called round after with bags of clothes and a few bits for the children, for we'd lost near everything that got wet through, and God bless her for it, for we needed it.

The days after were all cleanup. Sawing at the tree bit by bit to clear the tober proper, tarping the roof till the men could come at it right, himself up on a ladder a full week after, hammering away, and the young kam handing him up nails like he was the biggest help going, God love him, and the lackeen sweeping glass off the step morning and evening for weeks after we thought it was all swept.

And that's the story of it, and I'll tell ye, even now telling it I'm reaching for the words when I get to the tree coming down, or the window cracking, for it still catches in me a small bit, saying it. God, the sound of that crack. God, the state of the roof after. God between us and all harm the next time, for once was plenty, so it was.
