Sit there now and I'll tell ye, for it's the one story above all of them I get the laugh out of telling, though God knows there wasn't a laugh in it the day it happened, not one bit of one.

Myself and the husband were going up to Dublin, above to the cousins for the christening, and we had it planned a good three weeks ahead of time. Seats booked on the coach, the bags packed the night before, everything sorted so there'd be no bother on us at all. Himself says to me, the night before we went, "we'll be there for the dinner, no trouble," and I believed him, God forgive me for it.

We weren't out the gate of the site five minutes when the first delay hit, and the journey not even properly started. The coach that was meant to collect above at the road never showed at the hour it was meant to show, and there we were stood with the bags at our feet a good forty minutes before another one came for us, and the driver not a bit sorry for it either, only waving us up the steps like it was ourselves that were holding him up.

Well we got as far past Athlone as you'd spit and there was a bang up from under the floor of the thing, and it rolled itself in to the hard shoulder with smoke coming out the back of it. The driver up and out and scratching his head over it, and within the hour there was a crowd of us stood on the side of that road, feens and beoirs and childer and all, everyone that was on that coach and half the ones off the one behind it that stopped only to gawk, all asking the one question and getting no answer off anyone.

Three hours we stood there, guaranteed. Three hours, and nobody, not the driver, not the crowd, not the fella on the phone to the depot, could tell us was it moving at all or were we to misli on foot the rest of the road to the next town. There was even a couple of the shades sent out to keep what traffic there was moving round it, doing more harm than good with it if you ask me. And of course wasn't the sky only waiting for its chance, and down it came, lashing, the kind of rain that gets in under your coat no matter which way you hold it, till we were all of us stood there gammy wet and getting worse by the minute.

In the middle of all that, didn't I lose sight of the husband a good twenty minutes. One minute he's beside me holding the two bags, the next he's off looking does anyone know anything, and in that crowd, in that rain, I couldn't get sight of him for love nor money. I'm stood there with both bags and no sign of him, and I'm telling ye my heart was going like a hare, thinking all sorts, till didn't he come round the far side of the coach with a fag lit, delighted with himself, no notion at all the state he'd left me in.

But God bless the woman that was stood near me through all of that, a beoir I never had the pleasure of before nor since. She seen the state of me with the two bags, and a young one crying beside me, not mine, but roaring all the same, the whole road in bits, and she put out her hand for the one bag and I let her bug it off me without a word, and she held it till I had myself sorted, and gave the child a sweet out of her own pocket to quieten it. I never got her moniker and I never will, but I think of her still, God bless her wherever she is above.

By the time they got us onto another coach we were four hours behind and fifty gred lighter besides, for we'd to buy tea and rolls for the pair of us that we never planned on spending a cent on, and there was a fella at the far end of the crowd that must have been half skimished before he ever got on at all, roaring about missing his own function above in Dublin, worse than the rest of us put together.

And that's when himself and myself had the row. I wanted to sit tight and wait for the coach to take us the whole road, and he was set on getting off at the next town and finding a lift the rest of the way, and neither of us would give an inch to the other.

Me: We're grand where we are, will ye sit down.

The husband: We are not grand, we're after losing half the day already, and I'm not sitting on my hole another hour waiting on a coach that mightn't even come at all.

Me: And where are we going to get to, walking the tober in this weather?

The husband: I know a fella has a car above the town, he'll solk us the rest of the road for a few bob.

Well didn't I give in, more for the peace of it than the sense of it, and off we went, and the fella's directions were as much use as nothing at all, for we ended up going the wrong road entirely, out towards the bog roads and away from where we needed to be, and it cost us near on two hours finding our way back to where we should have been in the first place. I said nothing to him the whole time bar the odd gloke that would tell him plenty without a word out of me.

By that stage I was that cream crackered I could have lain down on the tober itself and slept there and never known the difference. The two of us soaked through to the skin, starved with the hunger, the christening hours gone by us already, and not a laugh left in either of us, only the two of us sat in the back of a stranger's car saying nothing at all, too tired even to be vexed anymore.

But sure didn't the luck turn on us in the end, for the fella driving knew a shortcut none of the maps would ever have told ye, and it brought us out the far side of the traffic that was after building up on the main road over an accident, and we sailed through where hundreds of others were stuck standing still.

We got in above at the cousins' nine hours after we were ever meant to arrive, the christening long over and the dinner cold, the two of us stood at the door like a pair of drowned rats. And the state of us, God, the state of us, wet through, filthy, the two of us falling asleep in the chairs before we'd even the tea drank in front of us. But we got there. And sure isn't that the whole of it, we got there in the end, God between us and all harm, and I have the laugh out of it now every time I tell it, though I'll tell ye again, there wasn't a laugh in it that day, not a bit of one, only the two of us wishing we'd never left the kena at all.
