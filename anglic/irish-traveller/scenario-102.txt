That was the main room then, straight in off the step, and it done everything at once, sitting, eating, praying, rowing, the lot. The good chairs was against the wall under the picture of the Sacred Heart and you weren't let sit in them unless it was a Sunday or someone important was in. The telly was in the corner and it never once had a proper aerial, you'd be up on a chair turning it this way and that and the whole room roaring directions at you.

There was a session in that room some nights you wouldn't forget, the father and an uncle or two with the box and somebody's fiddle, and the whole site near enough could hear it, and nobody minded, sure why would they. Through the wee gap there was the kitchen, and I can smell it yet if I close my eyes, fried bread and bacon of a morning, and tea so strong you'd stand a spoon in it. The one thing that always, always happened in that kitchen was the mother scalding the pot before she'd even think of making the tea. Scald it first, every single time, rain, hail, or shine, and if you went near making tea without scalding the pot first you'd be told about it.

The mother: You don't just throw the water in on top of a cold pot. Have some sense.

There was never enough room in that kitchen for more than the one body moving about, so whoever was cooking had the run of it and the rest of us stood in the doorway passing things in and out.

Now, the sleeping. There was the one room off the back for the parents, and us lot was split between the two bunks and the fold-out in what we called the middle room, though it was hardly the size of a room at all. Myself and my brother had the bunks, him on top because he was that bit older and claimed the height off me, and the two youngest lackeens top and tailed it in the fold-out with the granny when she'd be staying, which was more nights than not.

The granny's chair was her own and nobody sat in it, ever, even when she wasn't in it. It sat by the heater with her rosary beads over the arm of it and her glasses case on the little shelf, and that corner of the room was hers the way the tabernacle is the priest's, if you know what I mean. You'd no more sit in that chair than you'd walk into Mass and sit up in the altar.

My own spot, the one I thought of as mine and mine only, was under the back step where there was a gap you could get into if you were small enough, and I kept an old tin there with whatever bits I was collecting that year, buttons, a broken watch, coppers I was saving, a holy medal I found on the tober one day and never told a soul whose it might have been. Nobody else fit under there so it stayed mine till I got too big for it myself.

Outside the door was the yard, if you'd call it that, a patch of ground, a bit of gravel, the dog tied up of an evening and loose through the day, and the washing line strung between the kena and the next one, always something flapping on it. That's where the men worked on the trailers and the cobs got tackled up of a morning, and where I learned to hold a horse steady before I could hardly reach its head.

The neighbours was close enough that you'd hear every word out of their place same as your own. The woman two bays down, we'd hear her calling her lot in for their tea every evening near enough on the stroke of six, and you could set a clock by her.

The neighbour: Come in now, the lot of ye, it's going cold on the table!

And there was a sham a few bays up kept sulkies, and every single morning, same time, you'd hear the trot of the pony going out past on the tober for its training, click clock, click clock, before the site was hardly stirring. That was the sound the whole place woke to, more than any alarm.

The sight that passed the same time every evening was the shades' car, slow along the road at the edge of the site, never stopping, just having a look, and everyone's eyes would follow it without a head turning, if you know what that's like.

The thing that was forever broken, or forever being spoke about being broken, was the standpipe at the top of the site. It'd freeze in the winter, it'd run to nothing in the summer, and there was always a queue of buckets at it and someone giving out about the council.

The father: I've that fella from the council's number wrote down three times now and sure what good has it done.

Come the winter the whole site went quiet and hunkered down, smoke off every roof, the heater going morning to night, everyone in on top of each other. Come the summer it was the opposite altogether, doors open, chairs out, the whole site living outside near enough, and that was when the fairs would be on and there'd be talk of the road, of Galway, of who was heading where.

The street itself, if you could call the stretch between the bays a street, that's where every game there was got played. Football with a ball that was more patched than not, marbles along by the wall, the little ones on bikes with no brakes to speak of, and of an evening in the summer the whole lot of us out till the mothers started calling, one after the other down the line of bays like a relay.

The mother: Is that ye out there still? In now, the pair of ye, before I come out to ye.

The last time I mind being properly there, the real there, it was a summer evening and the whole family out the front, someone's radio going, the granny in her chair pulled out into the doorway for the air, and the smell of someone's dinner further up the site, and I remember thinking, sitting on the step, that I'd remember that exact evening, and here I am telling you I did.

It's not there now the way it was. The site's there still, but it's changed past knowing, proper units in place of half of what we had, the standpipe long gone for pipes into every bay, and half the families that was there scattered off to Dublin proper or over to Galway or further again. I go by it now and again and I'd hardly place where our own bay was only for counting up from the gate.
