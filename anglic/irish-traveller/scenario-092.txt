The house was thinned right down by then, only the five of them left in it, the rest gone home to their own trailers and the young ones put down hours since. The range was still lit and somebody had put the kettle on again though nobody had asked for it, and there was a drop of something stronger going round in the good cups because the plain ones were all washed and put away.

The mother: Do ye know what I was after thinking of, sitting here. The way he'd never eat his egg till it was near cold. Every morning of his life. I'd have it down in front of him and he'd be off out to look at the horse first and come back to it stone cold and eat it happy as anything.

The eldest: Ah now ma, that wasn't the egg, that was the tea. He never let a cup of tea near him hot, sure I've seen him leave it sit a full hour.

The mother: Was it the tea. God, I have that wrong years now.

The eldest: You have.

The mother: Sure I'd swear to it and all, and I'd be wrong the whole time. God love him anyway, hot or cold he never complained of it once.

The youngest let a laugh out of himself then that he clearly hadn't meant to, sharp and sudden, and put his hand up over his mouth after it like he'd done something on himself, but nobody minded it at all, the aunt only patted his knee and told him sure that's grand, that's grand, laugh away.

The youngest: Sorry. I don't know what came over me there.

The aunt: Don't be sorry love, he'd want the laughing in this house more than the other thing.

The eldest: D'ye remember what he used say, though, every single time anyone came in the door out of the rain. He'd gloke at ye up and down and say, would ye look at the state of ye, and who let ye out looking like that. Every time. Rain, hail, didn't matter, in ye'd come and that's what ye'd get.

The son-in-law: Would ye look at the state of ye, and who let ye out looking like that. God, I can hear him saying it now as clear as if he was sitting there.

The mother: Say it again.

The son-in-law: Would ye look at the state of ye.

The mother, quiet: That's him. That's him exactly, God rest him.

There was a small silence after that, the kind that isn't uncomfortable, just full, and then the aunt spoke up like she'd been holding something back all night and it finally worked its way loose.

The aunt: I'll tell ye something none of ye ever knew, I'd say. Before he ever looked at your mother he was mad for a girl below in Galway, a beoir from another crowd entirely, and he was near set to go for her people about it, and didn't the whole thing fall through over nothing at all, and six months after that he's after meeting your mother at the fair.

The eldest: Never! Ma, did you know that?

The mother: I knew there was some one, he never said much about it. God, I never knew it went that far.

The aunt: It did. He'd have been a different man entirely, and none of ye sitting here at all.

Nobody said anything for a minute after that, just the fire going and the kettle starting to sing low on the range, and it was the mother herself broke it, getting up without a word and going round with the plate of sandwiches again, and the bottle after it, filling cups that didn't need filling, only for something to do with her hands.

The mother: Here, take a bit more of that, the pair of ye are only picking at it.

The eldest son eating one gave her a look over the top of it and the aunt caught it and there was a small edge come up between the two of them then, quick and old.

The aunt: You could look at me like that all ye want, I still say you were wrong to sell that horse the way you did, and I said it to you at the time.

The eldest: We're not going into that tonight.

The aunt: I'm only saying.

The eldest: I know what you're only saying. Not tonight.

And that was the end of it, neither of them pushing it any further, the aunt going quiet again and the eldest letting his shoulders down, because whatever that was about it had nothing to do with the man laid out below and everyone at the table knew it.

The son-in-law: D'ye know the stubbornest I ever seen him was over that old gammy mare he wouldn't let go, the one couldn't hardly stand by the end, and every one of us telling him let her go, get a decent one for the sulky, and he wouldn't hear a word of it.

The eldest: He kept that mare a full two years past when any sense man would've let her go.

The son-in-law: He did. And I mind saying to him one time, would ye ever solk that horse to the man below and get yourself something fit to trot, and he looked at me like I'd cursed his mother. Never, he says. Never while she's got a leg to stand on.

The mother: That was him all over. Once he'd made his mind up on a thing there was no shifting him, not for love nor money nor the whole world telling him different.

The eldest: There wasn't.

The aunt: God, and wasn't he right half the time in the end, the stubborn old article.

They were all smiling now, easy smiles, no edge left anywhere in it, and it seemed like without any of them saying it straight out the whole room had landed in the same place, that if you asked any one of them the one thing to say about him it'd be that, the stubbornness, not as a fault at all by now but as the whole shape of the man, the thing that would outlast every other memory of him.

It was partway through the next round of talk that the youngest went quiet himself and they saw the tears going down him, not making a sound with it, just sitting there letting them come, and nobody stopped to make a fuss of him or ask was he alright, the aunt only put her hand on his back and left it there, and the talk kept on gentle around him the same as before.

The mother: We'll need to sort who's doing what the next few days. Someone'll have to go up and see about the priest in the morning, and someone'll want to ring round the rest that couldn't make it tonight.

The eldest: I'll see to the priest. And I'll take the trailer over, get the site sorted for after, there's people'll be coming from Dublin and from the tober out past the town both.

The son-in-law: I'll mind the young ones the next few days so, and whatever needs carrying I'll carry it.

The aunt: I'll do the food for the house, don't any of ye worry yourselves over that end of it.

The mother: Grand so. Grand.

It went quiet again after that, easy and tired, and the aunt was the last one properly awake, the others half gone in their chairs, and she lifted her cup without much ceremony at all, just what came into her head sitting there.

The aunt: Here's to him so. The stubbornest, softest man that ever wore out a good mare and a good woman's patience both, and God between us and all harm, may he be raising more hell above than he ever raised down here, and may we all get half the laugh out of life that he did.

The mother, half asleep already: Amen to that. God rest him.

And the fire went on burning down low in the grate, and none of them moved to put it out.
