Will I tell you now, my friend, the whole of it, the sulky and the state I made of meself learning it, because sure nobody ever tells you the humiliating part, only the grand finished bit at the end, and I'm not one for leaving that out on you.

I was near enough to forty before I ever sat up on one, and don't be asking me why it took so long, only that when I was a sublik running round the site meself I'd no patience at all for it, I was always off above at the gruber or under a bonnet with me hands black to the elbow, and the ones that were mad for the horses I thought were only losing their time. Then one year, God knows what came over me, I said to meself I'd learn it proper, the way me own gather could put a cob down the tober like he was born sitting in the sulky, and here was I, his own get, couldn't sit a saddle without falling off the two sides of it at the one time.

The first go at it was hopeless, my friend. Hopeless altogether. The old fella that was showing me, a sham that's been round the trotting longer than I'm alive, stood there with his arms folded and he didn't spare me one bit of it.

Instructor: Sure look at the state of ye. You've no more balance than a sack of spuds below. It'll take ye twice as long as it would a young one, and that's the God's honest truth of it, so it will, at your age.

And amn't I after hearing that and thinking, well that's grand encouragement so it is. But I stuck at it. Within the week there was subliks no higher than the cob's own knee out-driving me round the field, laughing their heads off at me, and I'm not ashamed to tell you it stung something shocking. You'd think a man my age would be well used to being shown up, but no, it cut me to the bone watching a fella of ten manage what I couldn't manage at all.

And the state of my body after, my friend, I couldn't tell you. I was after aching in places I never knew a body had, muscles that hadn't done a stroke of work in twenty years all roaring at me the minute I moved. Getting up out of the bed of a morning was its own trial, and the wife above in the trailer looking at me creeping round like an aul' one twice my years.

The wife: Would ye give it up, would ye, and save yourself the misery of it. Look at the state of ye hobbling.

Me: I will not. I'm only started.

But there was one evening I very nearly did. I went down hard off the sulky, the cob took a fright at something beyond by the wall of the site, and I was flat on my back with the breath knocked clean out of me, lying there not able to draw one breath, thinking to meself, that's it now, that's me finished with the whole notion of it, I'll not be getting up again let alone getting back up on that thing. I lay there a good while, my friend, certain in myself it was over. Told the sham as much after, that I was giving it up entirely, that I'd made me decision and there was no more to be said on it.

Instructor: Ah come back one more time. One more go at it, and if ye're still of the same mind after, sure I'll say nothing more to ye about it, I'll let ye off.

And God help me didn't I go back. It was slow after that, slow and shaming, one small thing gotten right after weeks of getting it all wrong, and a small crowd of them always leaning on the wall of the site of an evening watching, some decent enough about it and calling out well done, more of them only there for the laugh, God bless them, because sure I was a sight to be watching. The only thing that kept me from throwing the whole lot of it in the ditch was I stopped measuring meself against the sublik that could do it in an afternoon and started measuring meself against the fella I was a month back. That fella couldn't hold the reins steady to save his life. This fella could, near enough.

There was a woman beyond at the wall one evening, a neighbour off the far trailer, and she was decent about it always, no matter how gammy I was that day.

The woman beyond: Ah don't mind them laughing, love, sure you're twice the man you were last month, and that's the only comparison worth making.

And then, my friend, round about the fourth month of it, I couldn't tell you the day nor the hour of it, only that I was up on the sulky one evening and something changed in the body of me I've no words for, not in English nor in the old tongue neither. Meself and the cob were of one mind for once, and the thing that had me beat all them months was only difficult now, not impossible, and there's a world of difference between the two, let me tell you, my friend.

There was a young lad on the wall that evening, one of the very ones that had been laughing hardest at me back at the start of it.

The young lad: There's the sham getting the hang of it at last, God help us all, will wonders never cease.

And I laughed along with him, because sure he was right, and I didn't mind it near as much as I would have four months before that.

I never did catch the fastest of them, my friend, the ones that could handle a cob at ten years of age better than I could at forty odd, and I've made my peace with that entirely, please God, because sure that was never going to happen and I was an aul' fool to think it might for a minute. But I can put a sulky down the tober now without disgracing meself in front of man nor beast, and there's things I do without a thought in my head at all that I couldn't have managed one bit of when I started, and that's no small thing to a man of my years, whatever anyone tells ye.

Looking back on the whole of it now, the falling and the aching and the being laughed at off the wall of the site of an evening, I wouldn't change one hour of it. It sits fond in the memory now, the whole humiliating season of it, fonder than near anything else I've done these last years, and I'm only glad, please God, that I didn't listen to meself that evening I was flat on my back with the wind knocked clean out of me, certain I was finished with it. God between us and all harm, but I'd have missed the best of it if I had, and there'd be no story at all in it to be telling you now, only the quitting, and sure who wants to hear that.

So there you have it, the whole of it, start to finish, and the next time you're above at the site of an evening, come down to the field and I'll show ye meself, and ye can judge for yourself what an aul' one can manage when he sets his mind to it and won't be told otherwise.
