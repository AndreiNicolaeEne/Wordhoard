Sit down there now, love, and I'll tell it to ye the way it was handed to me, below at my own grandmother's fire, and she'd have had it near word for word off her own mother before that again, God rest the both of them.

Before ever there was a wheel of ours turning on this road at all, the family did be beyond in the west, in behind Roscommon way, and sure it was tin work and mending and a bit of dealing same as always, nothing grand about it, a few bits of ground they'd stop on year on year and no bother to anyone. But the ground closed up on them, the way it always does in the finish. The man that owned the bit they'd stopped on since before anyone could mind, he sold it off, and the new fella that had it wanted no traveling crowd near it, and that was the height of it. The shades were sent to move them on, and once ye're moved on from one spot ye're moved on from the next an' all, for word goes ahead of ye. So there was nothing else for it only to go.

It was the great-grandfather went first, on his own, God between him and harm, years and years before any of the rest followed. A young fella he was then, no more than that, and he took himself off east with nothing only what he could carry and a promise to send word when there was anything to send word about. My own grandmother used to say he hadn't a thing in the world only his two hands and a bit of a knife for the tin, and he walked the most of it, for there was no other way of it, sleeping in behind ditches and under hedges and asking a bed off anyone that'd give him one, and more that wouldn't. He'd tell it after, when he was an old man and I only a little one myself, sitting by him below at the fire.

The great-grandfather: There wasn't a night on that road I wasn't perished, and not a day I wasn't hungry, and I'll tell ye no lie about it, child, for there's no glory in it. I was after walking the best part of a county before ever I seen a friendly gloke.

He came in on Dublin the far side, and God love him he'd nothing, no work, no kin near him, nowhere of his own to lay his head, and he ended up stopping in with another family off the road that took pity and let him bide on the edge of their own bit of ground, for that's the way of it among our own, ye don't leave a man out in the weather. He wasn't there as an owner of anything, mind, only borrowing the shelter of them, grateful for it and paying it back any way he could, mending a pot here, minding a horse there.

It was the best part of a year before he found anything steady at all, only the odd day's labor, a day on a farm beyond, a day clearing a yard, a day's dealing if there was a beast going, and every penny of it put by. He wasn't a man to be idle, the great-grandfather, and in time he was known round the place as a sound gloke to have doing a job, and the days came a bit thicker after that, and then thicker again.

And it was two years, a bit over the two years by my grandmother's counting, before he had enough put by to send word back west that the rest were to come on after him. That was no small journey for a woman and children on their own, and it was the great-grandmother brought them, walking and getting the lift of a cart where one was going, and it wasn't an easy road for her no more than it was for him.

The great-grandmother: There's one of them never made it the length of that road at all, God rest the poor creature, and I'll say no more about it only that.

That was all she'd ever say on it, and no one pushed her further, for it wasn't a thing to be pushed on, and it's only ever her that spoke of it at all, a young cousin of hers, taken sick on the road and not strong enough for it, and no name ever put to it after, only "the poor creature," and that's how it's stayed in the telling ever since, out of respect for her, God rest her.

When they came in on Dublin at the last and the family stood whole again for the first time in that two years and more, my grandmother said there wasn't a dry eye between the two of them, the great-grandfather and the great-grandmother, and she only a small one herself holding onto her mother's skirts not understanding the half of what was going on only that everyone was crying and glad at the one time.

The one thing the great-grandmother carried the whole road from the old place and never let out of her hand, not on the ship of a cart nor asleep nor awake, was a small medal of Our Lady her own mother had given her, no bigger than a farthing, worn near smooth now with the handling of it. It's kept yet. My own mother has it wrapped in a bit of cloth in amongst her things, and it'll go to the eldest of us when her time comes, please God not for a long while yet.

Once the two of them were settled, the great-grandfather took up dealing proper, horses mostly, buying and selling and a bit of breaking, for he'd always the eye for a beast even from a young fella, and that was the trade that stayed in the family from that day to this, there's still them among us wouldn't know what else to be at only the dealing, and a good few of the men doing well out of the trotting since, God bless the sport.

They hadn't a place they could call their own for the first while, mind, only borrowing off others the same as he'd done alone at the start, but within the three or four years they'd their own bit settled on a site on the edge of the city, and that's where it stayed after, the same site near enough, the trailers changing over the years but the ground the same ground.

It was there the children started coming, my grandmother the second of them born right on that ground, and she always said it herself: it wasn't the walking nor the dealing nor even the medal made the place home at all, it was the children arriving into it. A place only gets to be home, she'd say, once there's a child born on it that never knew any other place, that never had to be told where it came from because it never came from anywhere else.

And that's the way of it still. I heard it first myself no bigger than yourself there now, below at that same fire, near enough on that same ground between here and Galway where we do be moving between the two of them the way the family always has since. And I'm after telling it to ye now the very same way it was told to me, and please God ye'll tell it on again after me, to whoever's below at your own fire, and the medal'll go on down the line the same as the story does, the two of them together, for they were never meant to be split.
