The sister: Would ye look at that now, sitting there like nothing. He told me that horse was mine. Said it to me straight, before he ever went into the hospital the last time. "That's for you when I'm gone," he said.

The brother: He never said no such thing to you and well you know it. I was in that kena morning noon and night the whole of that year and I never once heard him say one word about no brass horse.

The sister: You weren't in it half as much as you're making out.

The brother: Don't be starting that.

The sister: I'm only saying what's true.

The brother: Well it's not yours on your own say-so, it's going wherever it's decided it's going, and you're not deciding that on your lonesome.

The sister: Fine so, keep the oul thing. What about the gred then, the few thousand that was above in the post office. Because I know rightly you're after taking out more than your half of it already, don't be telling me you haven't.

The brother: I never touched a penny of that that wasn't for the funeral and you know that well.

The sister: I don't know that at all. I know you were down there with the book before the removal was hardly done.

The brother: For the coffin. For the flowers. Do you think them things pay for themselves?

The sister: I'm not saying you...

The brother: You are saying it.

The sister: It's not even about the money, is it. It's that none of ye rang me that last week. Not one of ye. I was two hour up the tober and nobody thought to lift a phone to me.

The brother: I know.

The sister: He was asking for me and nobody rang.

The brother: I know it. God forgive me, I know it.

The aunt: Would the pair of ye stop now, for the love and honour of God, stop. Roaring at each other over a bit of brass and a few pound and him not a week in the ground. Is that what he'd want off ye?

The sister: I'm sorry.

The brother: I'm sorry too.

The sister: We sound like right ones, don't we. Fighting over what's left of him like two dogs over a bone.

The brother: We do. God rest him, we do.
