Well the plot, I'll go through the whole of it for ye now, start to finish, for it was some year on it, good and bad and fierce bad in between, and I'm only after getting it all put to bed for the winter this past week so it's fresh in me head.

We took the bit of ground back in the spring, out past the site, the auld allotment yoke the council does be letting out for next to nothing if ye put your name down early enough in the year. Himself was on at me for a good while to give it a go, and I always said no, sure who has the time between the site and the fair and the youngsters, but this year I said right, we'll do it, and didn't I regret saying it about six times before May was even out.

First thing was deciding what to put down, and I wasn't for anything fancy. Spuds, always the spuds, and cabbage, and a few drills of onions, and the scallions for the pot, and I put in a row of peas up a bit of trellis himself knocked together out of pallet wood, for the kam does love picking them straight off the vine and eating them raw, the divil. Put a few sweet pea in along the fence as well, not for eating, only for the look of the thing, God love it, the plot wanted a bit of colour in it.

We didn't go near it with anything till a good few weeks after the last of the cold was out of the ground, for me own mother always said don't be trusting March, it'll turn on ye when your back's turned, and sure enough there was a hoar frost the second week of it that would've had the spuds black in the ground if I'd been foolish enough to chance them early. So we held off and went in proper the start of April, and glory be to God, didn't we get the most beautiful run of weather after that, warm and soft, sun near every day, and everything came up out of that ground like it was shot from a gun. The onions were up first, then the spuds broke through, and I was delighted with meself altogether, thinking this gardening business is only easy going.

Well it wasn't long before the first bother came on us. Little green flies all over the cabbage, thick as anything, and the leaves going all curled and sorry-looking on me. I asked about and one of the women on the site told me to mix a drop of washing-up liquid with water and give it a spray of an evening, and I did that faithful for the best part of a week, and it knocked some of it back alright, it did that much, but they never fully cleared, they'd be back again in a few days like they owned the plot. I lost the run of a few plants to them in the end but the rest held on, so it wasn't the worst that could've happened.

Then came the dry stretch, and Lord, that was the gammy part of the whole year, no two ways about it. Not a drop of rain for near enough five weeks in the middle of the summer, and the ground turned to dust under yer feet, and I was up and down to that plot every single evening after with buckets and the watering can, hauling it by the hand the whole time for there was no proper tap near enough our end, filling up at the one yoke down the far end and walking that tober back and forth, back and forth, buckets banging off me legs, till me arms were destroyed on me. The gather always used to say a garden'll test a person and he wasn't lying to me.

And that's what started the row with the sham next door, if I'm honest about it. There was one tap between the four or five of us down that end and no rota worked out at all, so it turned into whoever got there first of an evening, and more than one time I'd be standing there with me buckets going nowhere and he'd have the hose on for the guts of an hour on his own beds, not a bother on him.

Me: Are ye going to be much longer on that, only I've the whole plot still to do and the light's going on me.

The sham next door: I was here before ye, I'll be as long as I like about it.

Me: Sure that's no way to be carrying on, we're all only trying to keep things alive out here, not just yerself and yer own beds.

It went on like that a good while, sharp words of an evening between the pair of us, till in the end a few of us sat down together and worked out a proper rota, times wrote up on a bit of card and stuck to the noticeboard by the gate, half hour each and no messing after that. And once it was down in black and white it was grand between us, no more bother, and he even gave me a hand righting a fence panel later in the year that had come loose, so no harm done in the end of it, thank God for that anyway.

Now the spuds, I have to give them their due. They were the making of the whole plot, and that's the truth of it. Never seen the like, the amount that came out of that ground when I put the fork into the first drill, ye'd think I struck gold below in it. Whatever else went wrong or right the rest of the year, the spuds alone would've made the whole thing worth the doing.

But then didn't the birds get stuck into the peas just when they were coming good on us, pigeons the size of small turkeys down out of nowhere, and stripped near half the row bare before I even copped what was happening to them. I went down one morning and stood there with me mouth open, and got the kam to come and have a gloke at the state of it with me. Got a bit of netting over what was left as quick as I could and saved that much, but I lost a fair whack of them all the same, and there was no getting that back.

And in the middle of all that didn't we get the storm as well, a proper one, came through of a night in August and I went down the next morning and near cried at the gate, rows of the cabbage and the peas both flattened clean to the ground, the trellis himself made snapped clean in two, everything lying every which way ye'd look. Took the two of us the best part of a weekend staking it all back up between us, tying the peas back onto new bits of bamboo, propping what cabbage could still be saved, and half of it never came right again after that knock, but we got the most of it standing one way or another.

Come the end of it all, when I sat down and tallied what came off that plot over the whole year, it wasn't bad going at all for a first go, I'll say that much for it. Bags of spuds we're still working our way through above in the kena, onions strung up drying over the door, cabbage put by, and I gave a good few heads of it and a bag of the onions over to the woman two plots up from me for a clatter of duck eggs off her, for she keeps a few ducks up the top there.

The woman two plots up: Sure them onions'll do me grand, I've no room for growing them meself this year at all.

Me: And them eggs'll be very welcome below in ours, the young ones does go through them fast enough.

And that suited the pair of us far better than gred changing hands, and it's the way it should be done between neighbours if ye ask me, whatever about the row earlier in the year.

When the last of it was in, we dug the whole plot over, put a good bit of manure through the ground, covered the two empty beds with cardboard and black plastic to keep it down over the winter, and locked the gate on it for the year, and I wasn't right till I'd got back inside the kena and the kettle on after all that digging.

So there it is, the whole of it, start to finish. Fierce work, more of it than I bargained on when I put me name down for that bit of ground, and a row with the neighbour I could well have done without, but between the spuds and the eggs and the two of us out there of an evening working away side by side once the rota was sorted, I wouldn't have missed the year of it for anything, and please God we'll be back at it again come the spring, God willing and the weather good to us.
