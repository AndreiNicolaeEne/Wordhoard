Right, I'll tell ye the whole of it, start to finish, because everyone that hears it only hears the end, the kena standing there nice with the render on it, and they don't know what went into it at all.

It started because we were bursting out of the old place. There was myself and the wife, and the eldest kam married and his own lackeen coming along, and two more sublik still under our feet, and the whole lot of us in the one trailer and the one small build stuck onto it. You couldn't turn around in it. The mother used to say God bless the mark, we're falling over one another here, and she wasn't wrong. So we said, that's it, we'll do the job proper, put up a proper extension onto the side, make two rooms and a bit of a kitchen where the sublik could have their own space and not be under the beoir's feet all day long.

Myself and the father sat down at the start of it, the two of us, and we reckoned it out. We said four men on it steady and it'd be done in four months, sure. God, we were away with it altogether. It took eleven months in the end, start to finish, and there was six different hands went into it along the way, not four, six, counting everyone that came and put a shoulder to it at one time or another.

The wrong turn come near the very start, and it near broke my heart at the time. We were after putting down the footing wrong, the level was out on it, only a small bit but enough, and my brother-in-law's sham that does a bit of building for the county came round and took one look and said, that's gammy, that, ye'll have bother with that the whole way up. And he was right. We'd to break it out and do it again, and that was near two weeks of work gone into the ground for nothing, the digging and the shuttering and all, done over again from scratch. The father was raging, God forgive him the language that come out of him that morning.

Then there was a day, and I'll never forget it, we were only above a month into it, and I looked out and there's three feen coming down the tober toward the site, tools in the hand, that nobody had asked at all. My own brother and two of his sons-in-law, they'd heard on the wind that we were stuck for hands and they just came, no word said, no gred asked for it either, only came and started in on it like they'd always been there. That's the way of it though, isn't it, ye don't have to solk anyone by the arm, they hear ye're at it and they come.

We lost the best part of a week to the weather, the whole guts of it, rain that wouldn't lift, and the site turned to muck, you couldn't stand on it let alone work it. Every morning we'd go out and gloke up at the sky and it'd be the same grey dirt looking back down at us, and we'd go back in and sit round the fire and be raging, because every day lost is a day ye have to find again from somewhere.

We ran short of block halfway through, the order was wrong or the yard was short, one or the other, and there was no getting more for near a week. So we made do, we finished off a stretch of the inside wall with whatever was lying round the site, bits of block, a bit of timber stud where we couldn't get the block, and sure it did the job, it's inside a wall now and nobody the wiser, only ourselves that know it's a mongrel bit of building.

I had a fall off the scaffold above the gable, and I'll tell ye my heart went up into my mouth. I was reaching for a bit of timber and the board went from under me and I come down, not the full height, thank God, I caught the rail on the way and it broke it, but it was enough to leave me shaking for the rest of the day, and the mother wouldn't let me back up for a full week after, she was that put out. Nothing broke on me only my nerve, and a bruise the size of a plate on the hip, but it put the fear of God in the whole lot of us for a while.

There was a falling out too, over the roof, whether we'd pitch it the one way or the other, myself wanting it one way for the water to run off clear of the door, and the sham that was helping wanting it the other way for less timber wasted. We had some row over it, out in front of everyone, raised voices and all, and in the end we did it my way and I'll say now, looking back, he wasn't far wrong either, there was more timber went into it than there needed to.

The worst of it though was the end wall. We were three parts up it before we found the block was laid gammy from the very bottom, out of true the whole way, and there was nothing for it only to knock the whole thing and start it clean again from the ground. That was a hard day, I won't lie to ye, standing there looking at good work coming down in dust, but there was no way round it, a gammy wall is a gammy wall no matter how ye dress it after.

The good day, the day that put the heart back in us, was the day the frame stood up complete, the timber roof frame sitting up there against the sky for the first time, and we all stood back and looked at it and somebody said, God bless it, there she is now, and it was only a frame, sure, no roof on it yet, no walls closed in, but it was the shape of the thing at last and we knew then it was going to be a house and not just a hole in the ground.

The last stretch of it, the finishing, we were at it four nights running past dark, working under a lamp run off the car battery and a torch held in the teeth half the time, because we'd set our mind we'd have it done and we weren't for stopping short of it. The mother brought tea and bread out to us in the cold and stood there giving out about the state of us, working half blind in the dark, but she was proud as anything underneath it, ye could hear it in her.

And then the day it stood finished, done, painted, the door hung and all, we had the whole family round, the kam and his lackeen and the little sublik running in and out of the rooms roaring with the excitement of it, and the mother stood in the middle of the new kitchen with her hand over her mouth, and it was a great day, so it was, one of the best I've seen.

Eleven months it took us start to finish, and six hands went into it besides myself, counting every feen that ever lifted a block or held a line for us, and looking back now I couldn't tell ye where the time went, only that it went. And now when I stand inside it of an evening, the whole thing quiet, the sublik asleep in their own room at last, I think of every bit of it, the wrong footing and the rain and the fall and the row over the roof and the wall we had to knock and build again, and I think, God bless it, we did that. We did that ourselves.
