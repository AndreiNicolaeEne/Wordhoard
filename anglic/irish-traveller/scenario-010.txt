The mother come in with the bits of the good mirror in her hand, the one that hung over the fire since her own mother had it off her, and she stood there in the door of the kena with a face on her that'd stop a clock. God bless the mark, who's after doing this now, she says, and the four of them lined up on the bench like they were waiting on the priest.

The mother: Right. I left ye in here on your own not twenty minutes and I come back to this. Who broke it.

The eldest: Wasn't me, Mammy. I never went next or near it.

The mother: And you?

The middle child: 'Twas the door, Mammy, it blew open with the wind off the tober and banged the wall and sure didn't the whole thing come down with the shock of it.

The mother: The wind, is it. And the latch bolted since I went out to the shades of a message, sham, I bolted it meself.

The middle child: Well maybe 'twas the dog so, jumping up after a fly.

The eldest: The dog was tied to the step the whole time, ye can ask anyone, I seen him meself out the window.

The middle child: And what were you gloking out the window for if you weren't even in the kena.

The eldest: I was only passing through. I never touched it.

The mother: A minute ago 'twas the wind had it and now it's the dog and now the two of ye are at each other over who was where. One of ye's after telling me a story, and I'll know which of ye before the night's out.

The youngest said nothing at all, only stood there staring at the floor with her hands behind her back.

The mother: Not a word out of that one either. Right so, since not one of ye will own it, the whole four of ye can misli off now, no telly the week and no messages Saturday, and the next one that's after breaking a thing in this house can have the decency to say it to my face.
