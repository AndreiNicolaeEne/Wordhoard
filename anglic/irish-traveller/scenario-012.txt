Mother, will you sit down there a minute, I have something to tell you and I've been putting it off for twenty-odd year, so let me get it out the once, and don't be stopping me halfway through it or I'll lose the nerve I've built up for it.

You know the scar. Course you know the scar, you're the one bathed it and bandaged it and sat up half the night with me when it went gammy on us after, and you're the one still answering for it to this day, God help you, every time some sham above at a wedding or a station Mass says "what happened the leg on you so?" and you say back what I told you to say, all them years ago, and believe it besides. The bike. Coming down too fast past the site and off she went from under me and I opened the leg on the pedal or the handlebar or whatever it was I said that first night. That never happened, Mother. There was no bike in it at all. I'm ashamed to be telling you this at my age but there was no bike, and God forgive me the years I let you carry a lie for me without knowing it was one.

Do you remember the summer I went round with the three of them, God bless them all, the eldest of that crowd and the two more besides, and the one lackeen that ran with us that never let on to her own mother the half of what we got up to. We were only gasúrs then, the lot of us, sublik and herself the one exception, and we spent every evening God sent down at the water below the big tree, the one with the low branch you could get a leg up on and then it was hand over hand the rest of the way.

There was a dare going round that summer, the same dare, over and over, climb her as high as you'd go and jump from there into the water. I'd backed out of it once before that, I'd got up two branches and looked down and my nerve went from under me same as the bike never did, and I came down again and said nothing only that I didn't fancy it, and they never let that go, Mother. Not for one evening after did they let it go. "Sure he's afeared of a tree," the eldest'd say, and the other lad'd laugh, and I'd say nothing back only go redder than I was already from the sun.

So the once, I went up past where I meant to go. I want you to know that, because it matters to the whole of it. I didn't mean to go so high, I went that high because I couldn't stand another evening of being the one that backed out. I got up past the branch I'd stopped at before, and past the next one after that, till I was near the top of her, and I could hear them below me going quiet the way they do when they think you might actually do the mad thing this time.

Me: Are ye watching?

The eldest: We're watching. Go on so.

My friend: Don't be doing it if you don't want to.

The eldest: Course he wants to. Don't you.

And I jumped, Mother, before I could think better of it, because thinking better of it was the whole problem I had that summer. And I misjudged the water something shocking. I thought I'd clear it clean the way I'd seen the eldest do a dozen times, but I came down crooked and the water took the wind clean out of me, knocked it out of me the same as a fist would, and under the water there was something, a bit of an old fence post, we found out after, sunk down in the muck below where nobody'd ever swum before that summer, and I opened the leg on it before I even knew I was near it. I didn't feel the cold of the water at all after that, only the leg, and the blood coming up through the water dark the way it does, and I couldn't get a breath into me to even roar.

They pulled me out between the three of them, soaked to the skin and roaring now themselves, more frightened than I was I'd say, and we sat there on the bank, the four of us, and I bleeding into the grass, and it was the eldest said it first.

The eldest: If ye tell what we were at, we'll all be killed, the whole four of us, and you worse than any of us for going up that high.

My friend: Say you fell off the bike. Say you were coming down the tober too fast.

The lackeen: They'll believe that. Sure why wouldn't they.

And God forgive me, I agreed to it there and then, soaked and shaking and delighted in a black sort of way to have a story ready before I even got home, because the truth of it frightened me far worse than the leg did. I went home the state of me and I told you the bike story before you'd even the chance to ask twice, and you believed it, and why wouldn't you, I was after telling it steady enough, and you cleaned it and you brought me to be seen to and it was stitched under a lie, Mother, every stitch of it under a lie, and it's healed crooked under that same lie for two and twenty years now.

I'm not telling you this to be causing you any grief over it, and I'm not telling you to be getting the other three into trouble at this stage of all our lives, sure the eldest has grandchildren of his own now and wouldn't thank me for dragging it up, and my friend's abroad this long while, and the one that was a lackeen with us that summer is a grown woman with her own crowd to be minding. I'm telling you because I'm after realising I can't keep answering people with a lie you were the one first believed, and every time I do it I'm lying to you again in the telling, even you not knowing it. And I'm after having a child of my own now, and the first time she asked me about the mark on the leg I opened my mouth to say bike, same as always, and I couldn't get the word out, Mother, I just couldn't. I looked at her little face waiting on me and I thought of you all them years believing me, and I said I'd tell her when she's bigger, and I came round here today instead to tell you first, because it was you I wronged the longest.

So there it is. No bike ever touched me. It was a dare I couldn't walk away from twice, and a jump I went too high for, and a jump I got wrong, and a fence post under water none of us ever saw coming, and three children on a bank swearing a lie together because we were more afraid of being found out than we were of anything else that could happen us. And you believed every word of it because I gave you no reason not to, and that's the part sits worst with me, that it was never a hard lie to tell you, it went down that easy.

I'm sorry, Mother. I'm sorry for the fright I never let you have that evening, the real fright, the one you deserved to have same as I did, and I'm sorry for every year since that I let you carry the wrong story on my behalf and answer for it to half the country at weddings and wakes, God love you, none the wiser. You did nothing wrong in any of it. You only believed your own child, and that's all a mother's meant to do. The wrong was mine alone, and it's mine to be sorry for, and I am, from the heart of me, sorry.
