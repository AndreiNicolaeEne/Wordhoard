Sit down there now and I'll tell ye, though God knows I've hardly the legs left under me to be telling it standing.

It was the drain started the whole of it, the big one runs along under the wall by the gate, half-choked with muck and rubbish since before the Christmas and nobody after doing a hand's turn on it since. After the rain we had this past week it backed up on itself something fierce, and by the Tuesday morning the water was lapping at the step of the trailer nearest the wall and creeping in toward the day room besides. That's not a job for one household to be at alone, not if they dug from now till Sunday week.

It was the mother of that trailer went round first thing, banging on doors before half the street had the kettle even wet.

The mother: Come here now and gloke at the state of it, would any of ye misli down and give us a hand before the whole lot floats off on me.

And I'm telling ye, word went the length of that road quicker than any phone could carry it, one door to the next before the tea was properly drawn in the first house she knocked on. She'd only ever counted on the six or seven from her own end of it, and by the time I come out meself there was near on thirty stood at the gate, more coming down the tober still half dressed, her own kam among the first out, God bless him. Half of them I couldn't tell ye the moniker of even now, only the faces, they'd come down from so far along the row.

Middle of the week like that ye'd think the place near empty of men, half of them off with the cobs at the trotting most Tuesdays, but not that day, not a one of them missing. Even the missions van that does be round for the women's meeting sat outside the wall the whole morning with nobody going near it.

They had it divided up quick enough among themselves. Some on the digging, some hauling stone up from wherever it could be got, a couple of the young ones, sublik and lackeen both, sent off minding the cobs in the top field so they'd be out from under everyone's feet and the muck as well. The big feen from above the shop end took the heaviest end of it on himself and never once asked to be spelled, down in that trench to his oxters the best part of the day, the slane going the whole time, mud to his eyebrows and not a bother on him, fair play to the man.

Partway through it went wrong on us. The side of the trench gave in where they'd cut it too steep, and a full barrow of stone chippings went straight down into it. The young fella legging buckets round after it went in to his knees trying to save what he could of it.

Another one: Solk the barrow off him before he does himself an injury, would ye.

Ruined his boots on him that did, gone gammy for good after, though he wasn't bothered near as much as ye'd think, only laughing about it himself within the hour.

That's when the row started over how the water ought to be run.

The father from the top end: Ye have it running the wrong way altogether, that'll only turn round and flood the both of us again within the month.

The young fella with the shovel: Would ye let a man finish before ye go tearing at it, I know rightly what I'm at.

The father from the top end: Ye don't, or we wouldn't be standing in it to our shins this minute.

The old man: Will the pair of ye quit, for the love of God, the water doesn't care whose notion it was, it'll find its own way regardless of the two of ye.

And that settled it, more or less, though there was a bit of muttering after between them for the rest of the day.

Two of the households had food out over the course of it, one with a full fry gone round on plates about the middle of the day, sausages and rashers and all, and another kept the flask going from morning till night so there wasn't a hand went long without a mug of tea in it. Later on when the light started going a few cans come out as well, though nobody ended up properly skimished, only easy in themselves after the work of it.

The old man himself, past eighty if he's a day, wouldn't hear of sitting it out on a chair with his tea like they wanted for him. Stood there directing where the stone ought to go till they bugged him the far end of the rope to mind, just to keep him content, and content he was with it too, holding on to that rope like the whole job rested on him alone.

And there was the one bit had the whole lot of us in stitches together, when the dog took it into her head to go in after a stone somebody threw for the divilment of it, and come out the colour of the muck head to tail, shaking herself off right beside the one man on the whole site that'd kept himself clean the entire day. The state of him after. Even the old man was near doubled over with that, and he's not a man given to laughing easy, God bless him.

We were after thinking the whole of it be done by teatime, and it went on a good three hours past that, near dark down on us before the last of the stone was laid and the last barrow emptied. But by the end of it the drain was cleared the full length of that wall and relined with stone the whole way along, the day room saved, the step of that trailer dry again for the first time in a week, and every hand there after doing a fair bit of gruber that day for no gred at all, only the doing of it.

When the last of the stone went in somebody let a cheer up that had half the street joining before they even knew rightly what they were cheering for, only that it was done at long last. The mother gave the old man a gop on the cheek for his trouble and he went red as anything over it. And the street was different that evening, doors left open longer than usual, people sitting out past dark talking easy that wouldn't normally bother, even if it's only the one evening it lasts. It lasted the one evening anyway, and sure that's not nothing, is it.
