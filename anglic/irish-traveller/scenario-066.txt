Sit down there so and I'll give you the whole of it, seeing as you're only after landing on the site and you'll hear it told wrong off half of them anyway if I don't set it straight for you first.

It's above a year now since it started, and nobody paid it a bit of notice at the time, God's truth. The first one to see it was a young sublik below, no more than ten or eleven, and the whole site near laughed the head off him for it. He come in one evening, white as a sheet, saying there was a big yoke moving along the tober at the back of the kenas, low down, quick, with a great brush of a tail on him, and didn't he swear the thing turned and looked him straight in the eye. Sure everyone said he was only dreaming, that he'd his head full of some film or other, and away with him to his bed and no more about it. Even his own mother told him he was gammy in the head that night. He took some slagging over it for weeks after.

Only it wasn't a fortnight later that the same story come again, and this time it was the mother two kenas up from me that seen it, and there isn't a soul on this site would call that woman a liar, not if she told you the sky was falling. She's after seeing it plain as day in the grey of the evening, going along by the wall of the site, same as the sublik said, low and quick with the tail on him. That's when the talk really started, because if she's after seeing it too, well, then there's something in it.

After that it was seen regular enough. It does be poking round the bins of a night, tipping the lids off them, and into the gardens at the back tearing at whatever was growing there. One woman lost near her whole row of cabbage to it. It got into a coop as well and there was feathers everywhere the next morning, and that's when the whole site split clean in two over what to do.

One side of it, mostly the older ones, said leave the creature be, sure isn't he only looking for his dinner the same as the rest of us, and doing no great harm only rooting in bins that shouldn't be left open in the first place. The other side, and there was a good few strong on it, said it was only a matter of time before it took the leg off a child or gave some poor beoir the fright of her life, and that the shades should be called on it, or a trap put down, before it got worse.

Then the washing went. A full line of it, pulled down in the one night and torn to bits, and a good coat along with it that cost gred nobody had to spare. Straight off it was blamed on the yoke, no question asked, though there wasn't a scrap of proof it was him at all and not some young fellas out skimished and causing bother for the divilment of it. That argument alone ran for days, half the site swearing it was the animal and the other half saying sure a fox doesn't pull washing off a line for spite.

It all came to a head at a gathering out by the wall one evening, and I'll never forget the shouting that went on.

The man below: I'm telling ye now, that thing is going to hurt somebody and then where will we be. Somebody needs to solk a trap and put an end to it before it's too late.

The eldest: Ah would you stop. He's a wild animal that wandered in same as anything else does, and he's done nothing only what a fox does. Ye want to kill the poor divil over a few hens and a line of washing that mightn't even have been him at all.

A woman from the top of the site: My own child seen him at the bins not three foot from her and I near dropped dead with the fright. Don't tell me there's nothing in it.

The man below: There, you see. That's what I'm saying.

The eldest: And what harm did he do her, only give her a fright? Sure half of ye would run a mile off your own shadow.

The sham beside me: Would the two of ye stop shouting and think for a minute. There's no call to be poisoning anything till we know for certain what we're dealing with.

That night broke up with nothing settled only bad blood between neighbours that should've known better.

One household went and tried to sort it themselves without a word to anyone. Set some manner of trap they'd rigged up out of an old crate and a bit of wire, gammy looking yoke it was, and stayed up half the night waiting on it with a torch. Didn't work at all, only the crate got knocked over and the bait gone and not a whisker of the animal to show for it. They were the laugh of the site for a week over that one.

It was the night after that things really turned, when one of the women went out to the toilet block round the back near midnight and walked straight into it coming the other way along the wall. She let out a scream that woke the whole site and had men out in their bare feet with torches and hurleys before they even knew what they were looking for. She was shook bad, shaking for an hour after, though the animal itself only bolted the second it seen the light and was gone over the ditch before anyone got near it.

That settled the argument that it had to be dealt with one way or the other, but it opened a new row over whether the thing was dangerous at all or only unlucky in where it had wandered to. Some said any wild animal that'll walk within a foot of a grown woman in the dark has no fear in him and that's the dangerous part. Others said sure he was only passing through same as always and it was her that walked into him, not the other way round, and a frightened animal running from a torch is hardly the same as a savage one.

In the end it was an old fella that used to run dogs, lurchers and the like, half the country over for the coursing, that got brought in to have a look at the situation, on account of him knowing the ways of animals better than any of us did. He walked the site slow, had a good gloke at the bins and the gap in the ditch where it come and went, and told us straight it was only a young fox that had lost its own ground somewhere with all the building going on beyond, and was in nobody's way that kept their business closed up at night. He said there wasn't a thing dangerous about him only that he was hungry and bold with it, and that any animal will get bold if you leave food sitting for him.

The terms the site settled on after that were simple enough. No more traps, no more talk of the shades, and every bin on the site got itself a proper lid and a block or a bungee to hold it down. One or two of the men put wire round the base of the coops as well so there'd be no more feathers in the morning.

It's quiet enough about it now. The bins stay shut, the lackeen up the top keeps half an eye out for him still out of habit more than fear, and now and again of a summer evening you'll catch a glimpse of him going along by the wall same as always, and nobody so much as stops talking to look. The alarm's long faded out of it. These days it's more the story that gets told at a gathering than anything anyone loses sleep over, the night the whole street near came to blows over a fox that only ever wanted the run of the bins and a quiet corner to be left alone in.
