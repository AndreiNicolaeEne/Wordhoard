I'll tell it to ye now the once, and I won't be making it any bigger than it was, for it wasn't much of a thing in the end, only it felt like plenty at the time and there's no call to be dressing it up either way.

We were after leaving out from beside the mission hall on the site, myself and the sham, the two young ones asleep in the back with the blanket over them, and the cob box hitched on behind with the pony in it, for we were misli-ing down to beyond in Galway, an uncle of mine after dying below in the town and the removal that same evening. Long road it is when ye've a box on the back and a small one wanting the toilet every twenty mile, but sure that's the way of it always.

There'd been a right run of thieving off the sites that back end, tack lifted out of two trailers above in the midlands and a good cob took clean out of a field belonging to people we'd know well, so the shades had checks up along that stretch of the tober, stopping every trailer and every box that come through, papers and all wanted off everyone, no matter who ye were.

The queue of us was a good way back by the time we come on it, cars and vans and one big low-loader ahead of us all, and I'd say it was the best part of half an hour we sat there before we were near the front of it at all, the engine ticking over and the sham not saying much either, and I'll be honest with ye now, my stomach was turning the whole time, though there wasn't a thing wrong with us, not a thing, and still the nerves had me. That's the way of it though, isn't it, ye could have every paper in the country in order and still your mouth goes dry going up to them.

When it come to our turn the guard put his head down at the window and I handed him out the licence and the insurance and the log book all together, the way ye'd have them ready in your hand before he even asked.

Guard: Where are ye headed tonight?

Me: Down to Galway, sir, there's a removal on for an uncle of mine.

That one I answered straight off, no bother in it, for it was only the truth and there's no shame in a person going to bury their own.

Guard: And is the trailer registered to yourself, or to the man there beside ye?

That one I had to mind how I answered, for the box was in the sham's name still though it was myself driving it most of the time, and ye wouldn't want to be tripping over your own words in front of a guard, so I told him plain and slow that it was his, that we were only after swapping over the driving between us on the long road, and that seemed to satisfy him well enough.

He went quiet then over the papers, turning them this way and that under the little torch, and there was a stretch of it, only a minute maybe, but it felt longer, where nobody said a word at all, only the engine going and the small one stirring in the back. And then he asked was there a passport for the pony in the box, and God help me I'd nearly forgot the thing entirely, it was down under the seat where I'd put it safe going out the door that morning, and I had to lean right across the sham to root it out from under the mats, my hands not as steady as I'd have liked while I was at it, and hand it up to him through the window.

There was a fellow two cars ahead of us the whole time, one of our own crowd by the look of the box he had, and they'd him out standing at the side for a good while, going through everything he had twice over, and I'll not say what I thought looking at that, only that I watched it the same as anyone watches, out the corner of the eye, and never said a thing about it to the sham nor him to me, for what would be the good of saying it.

It took some doing to keep the face on me steady through the whole of it, and the voice too, for a person's voice will give ye away quicker than the face will if ye let it wobble, so I kept it flat and easy the way ye'd talk to anyone, please God, and didn't let on there was a bother in me at all.

The one small kindness in it was, when the small one in the back let out a bit of a cry, half woken by the light of the torch, the guard stood back a step and lowered it down out of her eyes himself, and said there was no need to be waking her over the like of this, and that was decent of him, I'll say that much for him, decent enough.

Then he handed the whole lot back to me, the licence and the insurance and the pony's paper on top, and gave a small nod of the head and said, on ye go now, safe home, and that was the whole of it, no more asked, and we pulled away from him easy and quiet, not wanting to look like we were in any great rush of it either.

It was a good twenty minutes down the tober after that before the shaking in my hands let go of me at all, and I do be honest and say I only felt then the toll it had took out of me, the whole of the tightness in the chest that I hadn't let myself feel while it was going on, for ye can't be feeling it there in front of them, ye have to hold it till after. Thank God we were only delayed and nothing more, and the removal not missed after all.

And that's why even still I tell it careful, the once, no bigger and no smaller than it was, for it wasn't a big thing that happened to us that night, only a checkpoint and a queue and a fright that come and went, but it was ours, and I'd not have anyone hear it and think it was more, nor less, than the small hard thing it was.
