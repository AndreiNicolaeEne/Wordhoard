Sit down there now and I'll give you the whole of it, because you missed some carry-on this year, so you did.

It was my turn to have the dinner. Herself said to me back before Christmas, "You're after ducking it three year running, it's you this time," and I said grand, no bother, sure how hard could it be. God forgive the thick I was that day, because I hadn't a clue what I was after taking on.

First thing I done was sit down with the pen and paper of a Sunday and I drew up a menu that'd feed a wedding. Turkey and a ham on top of it, and I said I'd do a goose as well for the look of the table, God help me, as if any gloke was going to eat three roasts off the one plate. Spuds three ways, the sprouts nobody touches only they'd be raging if they weren't there, a trifle, a pudding lit up with the whiskey on it, and I after telling the mother I'd have starters an' all like we were beoirs off the telly. She looked at me like I'd two heads. "Who do you think is coming to you," she says, "the President?"

That was the other thing. I'd it in my head it'd be the usual crowd, myself and herself and the children, the mother and gather, maybe the brother if he wasn't above at the gruber. Then the aunt rings to say she's coming down from Galway with the two of hers, and the sham that's married on the cousin says sure they'll call in, and before I know it I'm counting heads on my fingers and running out of fingers. Sixteen, in the end, and half of them lackeens and sublik under ten that'd eat nothing only the roast spuds anyway, but you still have to feed them.

The shop was its own disaster. I was above in the big Tesco with a trolley the size of a sulky, God bless the mark, and I couldn't get a fresh turkey for love nor gred anywhere in the town, only frozen ones the size of a suitcase, and I wanted the fresh one so I did, and I went to three shops and a butcher's after that and not a feather of one to be had. In the end I solked what I could get and said a prayer over it.

The night before I was up till all hours. The spuds peeled and sat in the water, the stuffing made, the ham scored and studded, and myself with a cup of tea going cold beside me every time I turned round. I wrote out a schedule an' all, God forgive my ambition, turkey in at half seven, ham basted at nine, spuds on for half ten, table set by eleven, dinner on the table for half twelve on the dot. I was very proud of that bit of paper.

That schedule didn't survive contact with the actual morning, I'll tell you that for nothing. I was up at six because the youngest was up at six, and by the time I got the turkey in it was gone eight, and then the oven decided it wanted to run cold on one side so I'm turning the tray round every twenty minutes like a lunatic.

And then the disaster hit. I'd the ham in as well by this stage and I was that busy with the spuds boiling over and the youngest under my feet that I forgot the goose was even in, and when I went to check it the thing was black as the grate, ruined outright, no saving it, and the whole kena filled up with smoke till I'd the window open and the youngest coughing and myself half crying with the head done in on me.

Guest that arrived early, the aunt from Galway, God bless her, walked in the door to that very scene, smoke everywhere and me roaring, and she didn't even take her coat off.

The aunt: "What in the name of God is after happening in here?"

Me: "Don't ask me. The goose is gone up in smoke and I've sixteen coming."

The aunt: "Right, give here the peeler and stop your snivelling, we'll sort it."

And sort it we did between us, she got straight into the spuds and the gravy while I legged it out to see was there any chance of another bit of meat, and thank God the butcher's was open a small bit longer and I got a bit of bacon off him would do instead of the goose, so we weren't left short.

The oven after that was like Grafton Street of a Saturday, everything wanting in at the one time, the ham fighting the bacon for room, the pudding wanting steaming on the hob, the two rings going for the spuds and the sprouts, and me trying to time it all so nothing come out stone cold waiting on the rest.

And do you know the one thing that came out lovely, better than I even hoped? The trifle. I only threw it together the night before out of what was left in the press, no plan behind it at all, and it come out gorgeous, better than any year I fussed over it.

We didn't sit down till near two, hours after what I'd planned, but sure nobody minded, they were all above in the front room with a drop taken and the young ones roaring round the yard.

First bite and the reactions started coming.

The eldest: "That ham is out of this world, so it is."

The uncle: "Better than shop bought, and that's saying something."

Then the blunt one, the sham that's married on the cousin, God love his honesty:

The cousin's husband: "I'll say nothing about them sprouts only I won't be having them again."

Me: "Sure nobody's making you, eat round them."

The bacon that saved the day disappeared first off the plate, gone before the ham was half touched, and the aunt was delighted with herself over it, telling everyone how she'd the sense to send me back out for it.

By the end of it we were all sat round half asleep, the youngest ones flaked out on the good chairs, and herself turns to me and says, "Well, will you be hosting again next year so?"

Me: "Not if you paid me. Never again."

The mother: "Sure you said that last year an' all, when it was mine."

And I probably will host it again, God help me, because sure who else would.
