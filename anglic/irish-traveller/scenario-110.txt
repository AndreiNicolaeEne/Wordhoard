The mother: Ah here she is, look what's blew in on us. Sit down there now, love, and get that tea into ye before ye say one word to anyone. She's not two minutes in the door and yer one below is already roaring at the young fella to go put the kettle on again.

Me: I'm grand, Mammy, honest to God, I only landed an hour ago.

The eldest: Well go on then, tell us. What's it like above there at all? Is it big?

Me: Big isn't the word for it. You'd want to see the size of it, it'd put the fear of God in ye. Here you'd be across the site and back inside of five minutes, there you could be walking the guts of an hour and still not be where you're going.

The aunt: An hour! God between us and all harm, an hour to go where?

Me: To cross the one side of it to the other, aunt. I'm not codding ye. There's days I'm on that bus so long I do be after falling asleep on it and missing my stop altogether.

The uncle: And the noise of it, I'd say. Is it like here at all, with the cobs roaring below at the wall of a morning and that's the height of it?

Me: Sure there's no comparing it, Uncle. Horns going, sirens going, some crowd shouting below the window half the night. You'd near forget what quiet sounds like. First few weeks I couldn't sleep a wink with it, and now amn't I after getting so used to it I can't sleep proper when I'm back here, the quiet has me lying awake.

The cousin: God love ye. And where are ye actually living, so? Above in one of them big places we do be seeing on the telly?

Me: Not at all, it's only a small aul flat, two rooms and a bit of a kitchen, you'd swing a cat in it and that's your lot. Smaller than what I have here even, if ye can believe that. I know yous are picturing some grand yoke with stairs and all, but no, it's tight enough.

The eldest: And what's the rent on a place the like of that? Gred, I mean, what's it costing ye?

Me: More than what it should, I'll tell ye that for nothing. What ye'd pay here for a lovely house with a garden on it, above that's barely covering the two rooms. Everything's dear on top of it and all, the messages, the bus, the lot. Ye do be paying through the nose just to stand still.

The mother: And are the people friendly at all, or are they lookin' down their noses at ye?

Me: Ah now, that's not a simple one to answer, Mammy. Some of them are as good as gold, I'll say that. The one next door to me, God bless her, she'd give ye the shirt off her back, always knocking with a bit of dinner if she's made too much. But then you'd get another crowd wouldn't give ye the time of day, wouldn't look at ye crossing the road. It's mixed. You'd meet the best and the worst of them both, same as anywhere I suppose, only above it's that many of them ye meet all the sorts in the one week.

The cousin: And where do ye be doing yer shopping, is there a market the like of home?

Me: There is, a great big one of a Saturday, stalls the length of the street, everything going for a song if ye know where to look and what to say. I do be down there most weeks, ye'd get yer bits cheaper than the shops if ye're not soft about it.

The uncle: And the work, how's that going for ye? Is it easy got?

Me: It's there if ye go looking hard enough for it. I'm after doing a bit of tarmac with a crowd from home who's above there years now, and a bit of driving besides. Ye do have to go asking round, knock on a few doors, put the word out, same as always. Nothing's handed to ye, but there's a start in it if ye're willing to graft.

The aunt: Did ye get lost much, when ye first went? I'd be lost in a place like that meself, God help me.

Me: Lost! I was lost three or four times the first fortnight, walking around in circles like an aul eejit, asking every sham I passed which way to go and half of them not knowing themselves. I do laugh at it now. It took me the guts of a month before I had the shape of it in my head, which road brings ye where, which turn ye don't take of an evening. I have it well got now though, I could near do it with my eyes shut.

The youngest: What about the food above, is it nice?

Me: Better in some ways, worse in others, I'd have to say. There's every class of thing going, food from every corner of the world if ye want it, and I do love a bit of that now and again. But God, the bacon and cabbage isn't the same at all, and don't ask me about the brown bread, ye'd be lucky to get a decent bit of it anywhere. I do be missing me dinners here something shocking.

The eldest: So which is it then, better or worse than home? Straight out now, no beating round it.

Me: Ah stop, would ye. Sure they're two different things altogether, ye can't be putting the one against the other like that. There's things above I wouldn't swap for the world, and there's things here I'd never get above no matter how long I stopped. I'll leave it at that.

The mother: And will ye come back to us for good, love, is that ever in it?

Me: Please God, sure who knows what's ahead of anyone. I do think about it, I won't lie to ye. There's days above I'd give anything to be sat here with the kettle on and the cobs grazing below and not a bother on me. And there's other days it suits me grand where I am. We'll see what God has planned, won't we. I'm not saying never, and I'm not saying soon either.

The mother: Well, ye're home now anyway, and that's the main of it. Drink that tea before it goes stone cold on ye, and give us the rest of it after, there's more of us wants to hear it yet.

The aunt: There is, God bless her, sure we've the whole evening for it. The kettle's not going anywhere.
