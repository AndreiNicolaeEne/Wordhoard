Set down here a minute and let me tell you something, on account of I can see it on you, the way you're a-carrying yourself here lately, and I've carried it too. There was one year, I ain't gonna put a number on it, don't need to, you'uns'll know the kind of year I mean when I say it plain, it was the worst one I ever come through, and I've come through a right smart of hard ones.

It started the way these things do, quiet, and then all at once. I'd been doing framing and roof work for two, three contractors round the county, steady as you please for years, and then the building just quit. Wasn't no one thing you could point at. Phone quit ringing is all. One month you're working six days, next month you're working two, and the month after that you're setting on the porch of a morning wondering what in the world you're gonna do with your hands all day.

First thing to go was the little stuff, the stuff you don't hardly notice you're giving up till it's gone. We quit going in to town for supper of a Friday. Quit buying the good coffee, went back to the can kind. I quit dipping the brand I liked and got whatever was cheap. The wife stopped getting her hair done and started cutting the young'uns' hair herself at the kitchen table with a poke over their shoulders to catch it. Little comforts. You tell yourself it don't matter, hit don't matter, and for a while it don't.

Then it got to the bigger things. We sold the second truck, the one I hauled lumber in, sold it for near nothing to a fella two hollers over just to have something coming in. Let the insurance lapse on it first, which liked to killed me, I never in my life went without being covered on something I drove. Quit buying meat from the store altogether and lived off the garden and what deer I could get and what my daddy could spare off his own place. Cut every stick of our own firewood instead of buying a load, out there of a Saturday a-swinging that maul till my hands wouldn't hardly close.

And then it come to where I had to do the thing I'd never done, not one time in my whole grown life, and that was ask. I went and asked my wife's brother for enough to get us through, I reckon it was near two months' worth, just to keep the lights on and something in the pantry. Setting across that man's table asking for money was about the hardest thing I ever done, harder in its way than the work itself. You feel like less of a man doing it, even when the fella handing it over don't think one thing less of you. He didn't. But I did, for a good while.

That stretch run on close to six months with barely a lick of work to show for it. Six months is a long time to watch a young'un ask why we can't do this or that and have to tell them we'll see. Six months of waking up before daylight out of habit and having nowhere to be.

There was one day in there, dead of winter, that I still think on as the bottom of it. The propane was low and there wasn't money to fill it, and it was cold, I mean cold, the kind that gets down in the floorboards of an old sigogglin house like ours and just sets there. I remember standing at the fireboard looking at a picture of my mamaw and papaw up there and thinking, I have let this family down. That's the day I'd point to if you asked me straight out, that's the bottom.

But right about then, and I believe the Lord has a hand in timing like this even if He don't get credit for it, a neighbor come up the drive with half a hog he said he had "too much of" and couldn't use it all, which was a lie, he didn't have too much of nothing, he just knew. Didn't ask, didn't make a thing of it, just set it on the porch and hollered was I home and then was gone before I could hardly thank him proper. That hog carried us near a month. I've never forgot it and I never will.

Not long after, a fella I used to work for called needing one little job, just a porch rebuilt for his mother, small money, nothing to speak of, but it was work, and word travels in these hollers faster than you'd think. One little job turned into another, and then another, and it built back slow, slow as anything, I'd say the better part of eight months before I felt like I was standing on my own two feet again instead of hit standing on me.

What I learned out of all that, if you want to know, is who you can count on ain't always who you'd have guessed beforehand. Some kin I'd have swore would be there for me weren't, not because they're bad people, just because that's not where their strength lays. And some folks I hardly knew well, a neighbor, a fella from church I'd maybe spoke twelve words to before that winter, they was the ones showed up with a poke of groceries or a word of work when it counted. I learned that about people. And I learned about myself that I could bend a right smart further than I ever thought I could without breaking clean in two, that I might could stand more than I give myself credit for, and that asking for help don't make you less of nothing, it just makes you a man in a hard spot, same as anybody.

It started feeling survivable, if I'm honest with you, about the time I paid my wife's brother back the last of what I owed him. Wasn't the money itself, it was handing it over and watching his face, and him saying it wasn't never a worry to him in the first place. That's when I knew we was gonna be all right, not just getting by, but all right.

Now. I know good and well what kind of year you're in right now, love, I ain't blind to it and I ain't gonna sit here and pretend I don't see it just because you're too proud to say the words out loud. I've been where you're standing, near enough. So I ain't offering you advice, I've said my piece and that's the end of that. What I'm offering is plainer than that. I'm offering you my time, whatever of it you need, and my two hands if there's work wants doing, and a chair at my table any evening you don't feel like being alone in your own house. You call on me. Don't you dare go through this quiet the way I near about did. I'm right up the holler, and I ain't going nowhere.
