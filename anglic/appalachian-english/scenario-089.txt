Well, you asked me to tell it to you proper, seeing as you wasn't there for none of it, so I'll try and lay it out the way it happened.

I don't rightly know what got into me, taking up skating at my age, up there at the rink past the holler where ever young'un around here learns it before they're big enough to reach the counter. Everbody else picked it up little bitty, out on that floor same as breathing, and here I come along wanting to learn it grown, with a body that had done forgot how to be young. I toted my rented socks in a poke same as anybody, feeling foolish before I'd even got the skates laced.

The instructor down there didn't mince nothing about it neither, first night I strapped them on. She was an older woman, overalls and galluses same as her daddy probably wore, and she looked at me plain.

The instructor: You're liable to find this comes a good bit harder at your age than it would've twenty year back. Bones don't forgive like they used to.

She wasn't ugly about it, just honest, the way folks around here'll tell you a thing to your face instead of behind your back. And she was right. That first lesson I could not get my feet to do one thing my head was telling them to do. I'd think roll forward easy and my legs would go every which way, all sigogglin, like somebody had built a fence crooked and never come back to fix it.

Within a few days I seed young'uns half my age flying past me like the floor belonged to them, which I reckon by rights it did, more than it did me. That stung worse than I let on to anybody. You'd think a grown person could shrug off getting shown up by a seven-year-old, but hit don't work like that, not one bit.

The instructor: You'uns line up along the rail there and watch how she keeps her knees bent.

She meant the other learners, but I heared it and felt about the size of a nickel.

And lord, the soreness that whole first week. I ached in places I'd plumb forgot I even had. Muscles up the back of my legs, along my sides, someplace under my arm I couldn't even put a name to, all of it a-hollering at me ever time I moved wrong, which was constant. I'd be a-reaching for a coffee cup of a morning and feel it clean down to my ankle. Used to could bend over and tie my boot without thinking on it twice in my life; that whole week I thought on it plenty, and groaned besides.

Then come the fall. I want to tell you about the fall proper, because it's the one thing I still see clear as anything when I shut my eyes on it. I'd got a little speed up, more than I had any business having, and my feet went out from under me sudden as anything, and I come down hard, flat on my back, and it knocked ever bit of breath clean out of me. I laid there on that floor a good little while, not able to draw air at all, staring up at them lights, certain in that one moment that I was done with the whole fool notion for good.

One of the boys that worked the counter come over and stood above me.

The one at the counter: You alright down there?

Me: I don't believe I'm doing this no more.

I meant it too, right then, plumb serious. I told my ride home the same thing, that I was quitting for good, that a grown woman had no business getting broke half in two trying to do what babies do without hardly thinking. But she talked me back into it.

Her workmate: One more time. Just come one more time, and if you still feel that way after, you can quit with a clear conscience and I won't say another word about it.

I don't know why that worked on me, but it did, so I went back.

After the fall, the climb was slow, and I mean slow in a way that'll test a body's patience plumb out. We was leveling out a little more every week, but hit never felt that way while I was in the middle of it, only looking back after. Learning to just stop without grabbing that rail like my life depended on it. Learning to turn a corner without my arms flailing about like I was a-fighting off a swarm of yellow jackets. And there was always a little bunch of folks standing along the rail watching, the way there always is at a place like that. Some was kindly about it.

The instructor: You're doing better than you was Tuesday. That's the only comparing that counts for anything.

And some was plainly tickled by the whole show.

The young'un: Watch out, she's fixing to go down again!

I heared that more than once, and it stung about the same as the first time mostly, but I got where I could let it roll off some. What kept me at it was what the instructor kept telling me, that I might could get somewhere if I'd quit measuring myself against them young'uns that had picked it up in an afternoon like it wasn't nothing, and start measuring against my own self a month back instead. Against the fast ones I wasn't never going to look like nothing but a fool no matter how long I stuck with it. Against myself a month gone, I could see it, little bit by little bit, a turn that didn't scare me no more, a stop that didn't send me straight into the rail.

Then, right around the fourth month, something clicked. I can't hardly put words to it even now. It wasn't a thing I thought my way into, it was just there of a sudden in the body, like it had been a-waiting the whole time for the rest of me to catch up to it. One evening I pushed off and my feet just knew where to go on their own, and I wasn't thinking on every single motion no more, I was just skating. The thing that had been plumb impossible for months was only difficult now, which felt like a right smart of a gift after what I'd put myself through to get there.

I never did catch the fast ones, and I made my peace with that a good while back. There was a boy couldn't have been more than nine or ten could skate rings around me backwards without hardly trying, and I wasn't out to beat him, never was. By the end of that year I could do things without thinking on them at all that I couldn't have done to save my life when I started, turn a corner, stop clean, keep my feet under me when somebody bumped into me, and that was enough. More than enough, if you want the truth of it.

Looking back on the whole humiliating season of it now, hit sits fond in my mind, not sour a bit. The fall, the soreness that had me a-groaning getting out of bed of a morning, the young'uns snickering along that rail, all of it's folded up together now into something I'm right glad I done. I don't know as I'd tell anybody my age to go and try the same, only because most wouldn't stick it out past that first hard fall. But I'm proud I did. Got a little trophy sets on the fireboard now that don't mean a thing to nobody but me, and I'm proud of a thing I can't hardly explain to a soul who wasn't standing there watching me learn it the hard way, later than I had any business learning it at all.
