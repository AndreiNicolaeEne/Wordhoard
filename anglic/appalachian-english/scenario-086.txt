Well, my friend, I got to tell you about that wedding out at the aunt's place up the holler, since you wasn't there and I still ain't right thinking on it, days off from it now.

Hit was my cousin's wedding, tables drug out under the maples and enough food laid out to feed three counties, dishes lined up clear down the fireboard on the porch and more still coming out the kitchen door. I told myself before I ever left the house I'd eat a plate and be gone by dark. Hit didn't happen thataway, not even a little.

The aunt kept coming round with more of everything, "just a little more, you'uns need a little more," and I kept saying yes on account of it was good and everbody else was still at it too. One more helping of the beans, one more piece of ham, one more little taste out of that jar the uncle brought up special from wherever he keeps it hid, and I never did keep count after the second glass of that. Me and the uncle was leveling about old times for a while there before it got too loud to hear ourselves think, and after that I couldn't tell you what all we said. We was all a-laughing and a-carrying on out under them lights somebody had strung up in the trees, and the music never did quit, and I told myself I'd leave after this song, then after the next one. I used to could dance all night and get up the next day fresh as anything, not no more I reckon, but that night I didn't know it yet, I was right smart into it, a-dancing with ever one that asked, spinning till the whole yard went to a blur of faces and the porch railing looked plumb sigogglin from where I was standing, though I expect it was me and not the railing.

The last thing I can call up clear is standing by the fire barrel with my glass still in my hand, a-hollering the words to some song along with the rest of them. Past that, hit all goes to pieces on me. I get flashes here and there. I seed myself out there dancing with the uncle, his galluses hanging off one shoulder by then. I heared myself hollering for one more song when the band was fixing to quit on us. Beyond that I couldn't rightly tell you what all went on, and that does bother me some, if I'm honest with you.

I woke up the next morning and Lord have mercy. My head was pounding like somebody had took up residence in there with a hammer, and the light coming in that window might as well have been the sun set right down on the bed with me, hit was that bright. Ever sound in that house, the dog's toenails clicking on the floor, the coffee pot a-perking away in the kitchen, come at me like it was right up against my ear. Just sitting myself up took ever bit of strength I had left in me. My stomach turned clean over at the thought of food, any food a-tall, even just the smell of bacon drifting down the hall was about more than I could stand.

Somebody else was up already and moving round like hit was any regular old morning, which I reckon for her it was.

The aunt: Well, look what finally decided to wake up. You look like something the dog wouldn't even claim.

Me: Don't. Please just don't, not right now.

The aunt: I ain't said one word yet about all that hollering you was doing round midnight.

Me: I'm begging you.

The aunt: You danced with ever soul at that wedding, some of them twice. Might could've charged folks admission at the gate.

She wasn't ugly about it, just had that little grin on her she gets, and I couldn't hardly get mad because I knowed good and well she was right, and I didn't have the strength in me to argue noway.

I laid back down right there on top of the covers and made myself a promise, plain and flat as I could make it: I was never letting a night get away from me like that again, not never again. And I laid there knowing full well I'd told myself that same exact thing before, more than the once, and it hadn't took yet, and it probably wasn't fixing to take this time neither, but I meant it anyhow, lying there in the dim with my eyes shut tight against the light.

Come midmorning I thought I'd walk on down to the mailbox just to get myself up and moving some, and I want you to know that little bit of a walk down the holler road took it out of me like I'd hauled a poke of feed clean up the mountain. My legs was fine, it was everthing else on me that had quit working. I tried to straighten the front room some, just one little chore, move a chair back where it went, and I had to sit right back down on the porch steps after, plumb tuckered out from that alone. Whole morning went thataway, me starting some little thing or another and giving out before I ever got it finished.

I got myself some dry toast round about noon, nothing on it, no butter even, just enough to put something down there and settle my stomach some. Sat on the porch with a glass of water and didn't say ten words to anybody the whole rest of the morning, just kept still and let it pass over me like weather does.

And in amongst all that quiet, pieces kept a-coming back to me on their own. I recall now, plain as anything, hollering something ugly across the yard at the uncle's boy, something about him calling the tune too slow for dancing, and him just laughing it off at the time, but I know good and well I owe him an apology for the way I said it. I turn it over in my head more than I care to say, feeling about two inches tall ever time it comes back round on me. I mean to catch him next I see him and set it right, tell him I don't even rightly know what got into me that night, which is the plain truth of it.

By evening I was coming back around to myself some. Had a real supper, nothing rich to it, but I could set at the table and eat like a regular person and hold up my end of a conversation without the words a-swimming round on me no more. My head had cleared up enough I could think straight again, and I set out on the porch after and watched the light go down slow over the ridge and felt about human again for the first time all that whole day.

But I'll tell you plain, my friend, sitting here writing you days off from it: I know myself too well by now. I expect I'll let a night get away from me again someday, some other celebration, some other reason to say yes just one more time, and I'll wake up sorry all over again, swearing off it the same old way I already broke a dozen times before this one. Hit's just how it goes with me, I reckon, and there ain't no promise made lying flat on your back with your head splitting that's worth a plug nickel come the next time the fiddle strikes up. But I did mean it, that morning, laying there. I always do mean it. Right up till the next time comes along and asks.
