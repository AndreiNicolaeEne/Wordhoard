I was up before hit ever thought about getting light, on account of my daddy a-holering up the stairwell the way he done every haying morning of my life. I like to never got my eyes open right. Come down to the kitchen and the fireboard clock said quarter to five, and Granddad was already setting there with his galluses hanging off his shoulders and his boots on, drinking coffee black as a stove lid. The hay down in the bottom field had laid cut two days, and he allowed if the Lord didn't send us rain we ought to get her baled before dinner.

The mother: "You'uns eat something first. Hit ain't going nowhere."

Granddad give me the first job of the day, which was to walk on down and feel was the windrows dry enough, on account of dew sets in a bale and heats it and can burn a whole barn down before anybody's the wiser. I went down barefoot near about, wetting my britches to the knee in that grass, and come back and told him hit was still tacked down good with dew, we'd want to hold off another hour.

The youngest was let to come along that day, being big enough now to be some use, and they got put on the easiest chore they was, which was driving the old truck in granny gear down along the windrows while the rest of us walked and pitched, and totin the water jug back and forth so nobody had to stop what they was doing. They took to it proud as a peacock, like they'd been handed the whole operation.

The heaviest part of it fell on my uncle, on account of he's built like the side of the barn itself, and once the baler started throwing bales he was the one up in the mow stacking, which is hot work like you would not believe, ninety-some degrees up under that tin roof with hay dust a-fighting its way down every breath you draw. Nobody else could keep up with him up there noway, so hit was settled without much said about it.

Once the bottom field was cleared we shifted around some. The ones that had been pitching bales onto the wagon moved over to drive, and the truck driving got handed off, and my uncle come down out of the mow soaked through and red as a boiled ham to go set the next load while somebody spelled him up top.

Along about midmorning I noticed Granddad had fell off the pace some, on his bad hip, and wasn't nobody said a word to him about it. The rest of us just naturally took a bale or two more apiece off his side of the row without making no show of it, and my uncle slowed his own self down some coming back from the wagon so as Granddad wouldn't feel hisself the reason we was behind. Granddad noticed, I believe, but he never let on, just kept right on working at the pace he could hold.

The mother and grandmother brought dinner out to the field partway through, in a poke and a couple of baskets both, cornbread and soup beans and a jug of tea sweet enough to pucker your mouth, and we set down in what shade there was under the fence row trees to eat it.

The eldest: "Lord, if that ain't the best meal I've had since Sunday."

My uncle: "Hit'd be better if somebody'd quit tying them bales so tight I like to throwed my back out."

The eldest: "Tight bales hold their shape. You just don't want to work is all."

My uncle: "I've worked this bottom since before you was borned, don't tell me I don't want to work."

Granddad: "Both you'uns hush and eat. They's a right smart of hay left and not a right smart of day."

Along about one o'clock the sky over toward the ridge went the color of a bruise, and thunder commenced a-rumbling off in the gap. Granddad stood up and looked at it a long minute.

Granddad: "We might could still beat that if we go on and do the far field first and leave the close one for last. Hit'll hold longer being up out of the bottom."

So we changed the whole order around, on his say-so, and drove on up to the far field while that cloud set there threatening and never did nothing else. It teased us the better part of an hour, rumbling and going dark and then easing back off, and we just kept working through it, watching it out the corner of our eye the whole time.

Along about the middle of the afternoon, once it looked like the weather had done decided to hold off after all, we pushed hard, on account of we'd lost time to it setting there staring at us. The baler was throwing bales steady and the wagon was filling quick, and everybody had a rhythm going.

Then the baler sheared a pin, right in the middle of the far field, and just quit throwing altogether, dropping loose hay in a heap instead of a bale. My uncle come down off the wagon and had a look and said hit wasn't nothing but the shear pin done its job protecting the gears, same as it's built to do, and he sent the youngest a-running to the shop for a spare while the rest of us raked that loose hay back up by hand so it wouldn't go to waste. Had her fixed and running again inside twenty minutes, though it felt a good deal longer standing there watching that cloud.

By the time we got back to steady baling the light had started leaning long and gold across the field, and Granddad hollered down the row for everybody to pick it up, we was leveling off too much for how much daylight we had left.

Granddad: "Come on now, they's daylight burning and a barn to fill yet!"

That last close field, the one we'd saved back, everybody converged on it together toward the end, even the youngest quit driving and got down to help stack the last wagon by hand, and my uncle come down out of the mow one final time so we could all six of us load together and get her in before dark shut us out entire.

We got the tally of it setting on the porch after, Granddad counting wagon loads on his fingers the way he does, and it come to better than four hundred bales off them two fields, which he allowed was a right smart crop for a summer that started out so dry.

Once the last bale went up into that old barn, sigogglin as it stands and has stood since before I was borned, we all just set down wherever we happened to be standing, on the porch steps and the tailgate and the ground itself, wore plumb out, not a one of us saying much of anything, just breathing.

Granddad let us set about ten minutes before he looked over at the youngest.

Granddad: "Go on and shut up them chickens 'fore something gets in on em. Then you can rest same as the rest of us."

And that was the whole of it, start to finish, the day the hay come in.
