Lord, let me tell you about this whole mess, since you wasn't there to see it with your own two eyes.

It started back a couple months before, when I got it in my head that I was gonna be the one hosting the big meal this year. Every year it's been the mother's house, or my sister's, and I always just showed up with a poke of rolls or a pie and let somebody else run theirself plumb into the ground. But this year I said, no, I'm doing it, I'm hosting, and soon as the words was out of my mouth I got a knot in my stomach the size of a fist that didn't go away till it was all over and done with.

I set down and drew up a menu that, looking back on it, wadn't sensible a-tall. I wanted a whole ham AND a turkey both, I wanted three kinds of dressing because I couldn't decide between the cornbread and the biscuit, I wanted greens and beans and a candied sweet potato thing I seed on the television one time, and about four desserts because I couldn't narrow it down. My aunt heared what all I had planned and just laughed at me.

The aunt: Honey, you cain't feed the whole holler off of one stove. You're fixin to kill yourself before Thanksgiving Day even gets here.

I told her hit didn't matter, I had it handled. I did not have it handled.

Then the guest count got away from me. First it was gonna be just us, maybe eight or nine. Then the mother said her cousin was coming through and could she bring him, and then my sister's man's whole family didn't have nowhere to go this year, and then the neighbor from down the holler, the one whose porch is all sigogglin and been needing fixed for two years, she got wind of it and asked was there room, and by the end I was cooking for twenty-some people instead of nine. I was up half the night just doing figures on a napkin trying to work out did I have enough chairs, let alone enough ham.

The shopping was its own trial. I went to ever store in three counties, I swear, looking for self-rising White Lily cornmeal, the good kind, and there was not a bag of it to be found nowhere. Shelves plumb empty. I asked the one at the counter did they have any in the back and she looked at me like I'd asked her for gold.

The one at the counter: Ma'am, we been out of that since before Halloween. Everybody's making the same dressing you are.

I ended up having to drive clear to the next town over and buy the last two bags off a shelf before some other desperate woman got to it first. Felt like I'd won something.

The night before, I did not sleep hardly at all. I was up past midnight peeling potatoes and chopping onions till my eyes was burning, setting the table, writing out on a piece of paper a whole schedule, hour by hour, what needed to go in the oven when, so the morning would run smooth as anything. I was right proud of that paper. Had it taped up on the fireboard where I could see it.

That paper did not survive contact with the actual morning. Not even a little bit.

Come daylight I was already behind, because the ham took longer to come up to temperature than I'd figured, and the oven wadn't big enough to hold the ham and the turkey both at once, so I had one setting on the counter getting cold while the other one finished, and the dressing was supposed to go in after the turkey come out but there wadn't room because I had a sweet potato casserole in there too, and everything was just fighting for space and time like a bunch of young'uns fighting over the last piece of pie.

And then it happened. I got to talking on the phone with my sister, just for two minutes, I swear it wadn't but two minutes, and I plumb forgot about the rolls I had going. By the time I smelled it, they was black on the bottom, hard as little rocks. I liked to have cried right there in the kitchen.

The mother, bless her, she was already there helping and she just looked at them rolls and shook her head.

The mother: Well, hit ain't the end of the world. Scrape the bottoms off, nobody's gonna know the difference once there's butter on em.

We did scrape the bottoms off about half of em, and the other half I just flat had to throw out and start a new batch, which meant more oven space I did not have.

Right about then the neighbor from down the holler showed up a whole hour early, before I was anywhere close to ready, hair still wet from her shower and a green bean casserole in her hands, and I like to have died of embarrassment, kitchen looking the way it did. But she just took one look at me and set her dish down and rolled her sleeves up.

The neighbor: Give me something to do or I'm liable to just stand here and get in your way. What needs peeled.

She ended up mashing potatoes and setting the table proper while I was a-fighting with the stove, and I don't know what I'd have done without her, honest truth.

The stove itself was a disaster the whole morning through. Four eyes going, the oven packed full, dishes just a-hollering to get cooked and no room to put nothing. I had green beans setting in a cold pot on the porch for twenty minutes waiting on a spot on the stovetop to open back up.

But out of all that mess, one thing come out better than I had any right to expect, and that was the candied sweet potatoes, the ones off the television. I'd never made em before, wadn't sure they'd even set up right, and they come out of that oven bubbling and golden and smelling like heaven itself. Small mercy in a morning full of none.

We was supposed to eat at one o'clock. We did not sit down till near on three. By the time everybody got a plate fixed and a seat found, my nerves was shot, but the noise in that house, all them people talking over each other, you'uns hollering from one end of the table to the other, it was something.

First bite, the room went quiet a second and then everybody started talking at once about how good everything was, which is about the best sound a body can hear after a morning like mine. The ham was tender, the greens was right, the sweet potatoes disappeared like they'd never existed, first dish gone and folks was still asking was there more.

But my sister's man, he don't hold back, never has. He took one bite of them dressing rolls and made a face he didn't even try to hide.

Her man: I don't mean no disrespect, but this dressing's a mite dry. You might could've put another stick of butter in it.

Everybody laughed, and he wadn't wrong, truth be told, I'd rushed it same as everything else that morning.

By the end of the meal there wadn't a scrap of them sweet potatoes left in the dish, somebody had even wiped it with a roll, and that was the one everybody was still talking about while we cleared the table, saying I had to make it again come Christmas.

Setting there after, plumb wore slap out, feet aching, kitchen looking like a hurricane come through it, the mother asked would I ever host again.

The mother: Well? You gonna do this again next year, or was that a one time thing?

I told her ask me again in about a month once I've forgot how tired I am, cause right then I wadn't promising nothing, but deep down I already knowed I would. Wouldn't be a holiday without the whole mess of it.
