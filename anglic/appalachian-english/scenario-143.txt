Well, I don't know as I ought to even get into it, but you'uns asked, so I'll lay the whole thing out for ye the way it happened.

We was all setting around the table of a Sunday, good and full, several courses already worked through, beans and taters and a mess of fried okra and a ham that had done been picked half down to the bone, and ever body had loosened their belt a notch and was just leaning back sort of easy, the way you do when the worst of the eating's behind ye and it's just picking now, just talk. Coffee going round. Guard down, like, the way it gets of an evening when the dishes ain't even been cleared yet.

Now there is one thing at that table this family has knowed for years and years not to bring up. Ain't nobody ever set down and said it in so many words, ain't no rule wrote down nowhere, but ever soul at that table has felt what it does when it gets stirred, and so mostly it just don't get stirred. Ever body's learned it the hard way one time or another, borned into knowing it near about, and they've learned to let it lay.

Only it didn't lay, not that evening.

It come up sideways, like it does. Somebody wadn't even trying to start nothing, just made a little comment off to the side, half under their breath near about, the kind of thing you'd say and forget you'd said two minutes later. Wadn't aimed at nobody in particular. Just fell out of their mouth the way things do at a full table when the tongue gets a right smart looser than the mind.

And I want you to have heared how quiet it got right then, for just that one second. Wadn't a word of argument said yet, but you could feel it setting down over that table like a dampness, ever one of us a-knowing exactly what was fixing to come, the way you know a storm's fixing to break before the first drop's ever hit ye. I seed it coming plain as day, same as I always do.

The eldest couldn't let it go by. Come in mild at first, just testing, the way you'd put a foot out on ice to see would it hold.

The eldest: Now I don't reckon that's altogether fair to say.

And that might could've been the end of it right there, if the one across from her had just let it be. But he didn't.

The uncle: Fair or not, hit's true, and you know hit is.

Well, that done it. Voices commenced climbing after that, not full-out hollering yet but getting up there toward it, that hard edge coming into em both, galluses and all, him leaning forward into it like he meant to make a point with his whole body.

The eldest: You don't even understand what you're a-talking about. Never have.

The uncle: I understand it a right smart better'n you give me credit for.

The eldest: You don't. You ain't never understood the first thing about it, and you won't hear different from nobody living, so what in the world's the use of even saying it to ye.

The uncle: I ain't wrong about it and I ain't fixing to say I am just to make this table easier to set at.

No ground given on his end at all, flat denial, and you could hear plain it wadn't going nowhere good from there, both of em plumb dug in now.

The mother tried to head it off, bless her heart, reached for whatever she could grab a-hold of.

The mother: Anybody want some more of that cornbread? I believe there's another pan of it back on the stove yet.

Didn't take. Wadn't but two seconds before it was right back on the same thing, like the cornbread had never even been offered, ever body's attention drug back to it same as a fishing line got a bite on it.

Then the grandmother, she come in with something from way back, some old fight from I don't know how many years gone, same subject exactly, drug right up out of the ground where it'd been buried.

The grandmother: This is the same as that time at Christmas, you recall, and look how that ended up. Same as it always does with you two.

Which didn't help nothing a bit, just proved to ever body there that this had done happened before and it'd happen again, plain as day, both sides holding it up like it was proof they'd been right all along.

Somebody, the youngest I believe it was, tried to call it back to the rule, the one nobody ever wrote down but ever body knowed just the same.

The youngest: We said we wadn't gonna get into this here, of all places. We agreed on that a long time back.

Didn't matter one bit. The argument just kept right on a-going, a-hollering some now, right over top of what the youngest had said, like it hadn't even been spoke.

And that's when the uncle pushed his chair back some from the table. Didn't get up and leave the room, mind you, didn't storm off nowhere, just scooted back a foot or so, put a little distance between hisself and the rest of us, arms crossed over them galluses, looking off toward the fireboard like there was something awful interesting setting up there on it.

Lord, the quiet that come down after that. You could hear ever fork against ever plate like it was near about a gunshot. Nobody looking at nobody. Just chewing, if anybody was even still chewing at all.

After a while the mother tried again, something about the weather turning or one of the young'uns starting school come fall, I don't rightly recall which now, just something that hadn't one thing to do with what'd just happened. And it helped, some. Table loosened back up a little, folks talking again, low. But it wadn't the same as before. You could feel plain it wadn't fixing to get back to how easy it was setting there an hour before. That ease was gone for the rest of the evening, plumb gone.

And when it was all done and ever body was clearing plates and putting up leftovers in a poke to send home with whoever needed sent home with some, wadn't nobody said sorry, not outright, not one word of it. Just let it go quiet-like, the way this family has always done, ever body agreeing without a soul saying so out loud that we ain't gonna bring that back up again, not at this table. And ever one of us there knowed good and well, plain as anything, that we would. Not that evening. But some meal down the road, some Sunday or some Thanksgiving or whatever it'll turn out to be, it'll come up sideways again same as it always has, and we'll go right back through the whole sigogglin mess of it again like we ain't never learned a blessed thing from doing it the last hundred times before.

That's just how that family does. Always has, and I reckon it always will, long as there's a table for us all to set around.
