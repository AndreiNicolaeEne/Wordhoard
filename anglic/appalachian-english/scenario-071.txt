Me: Well, I'll tell you what kind of day it was. You recollect that barn roof down past the second bridge, the one got tore half off in that big wind back in the spring? Hit wasn't but two-three sheets of tin left a-hanging when the wind got done with it, a-flapping and a-banging ever time a breeze come through, and ever soul that drove by knowed that roof was fixing to go altogether, decking and all, first good rain that come through it. That was too big a job for one household to lay hands to, specially with the man of that house laid up with his back the way it was, couldn't hardly bend over to tie his own boots let alone climb no ladder.

It was her brother-in-law that first put the word out. He come up the road of a Saturday morning early, stood out in the yard and hollered up at the house next to his, and by the time he'd hollered up two more houses word was already gone on ahead of him, quicker than he could walk it hisself. Somebody got on the phone, somebody else just walked it down the holler house to house the old way, and inside of an hour there wasn't a soul up and down that road hadn't heared about it.

We was thinking maybe six or eight would turn out, the regular ones that always does. Instead there come better than thirty. Trucks parked clean down both sides of the road, folks a-walking in from the far end of the holler carrying their own ladders and their own hammers, more hands than anybody had counted on by a right smart. Somebody had to go back and figure the whole work over again just to keep that many folks busy without them standing on top of one another.

So they split it up. Some got up top stripping the old tin, careful where it was rotted through. Some was down below cutting new decking to length and passing it up. The young ones and the ones that wasn't fit to climb was hauling and sorting, keeping the nail buckets full, keeping the whole mess of it moving along. And it was her workmate's husband, the big fellow, that took on the heaviest of it, hauling them tin bundles up the ladder two and three at a time on his own shoulder when ever other man there was struggling to manage one between the two of them. He never did stop the whole day, might could have laid that whole roof by himself if you'd have let him, plumb tireless.

Wasn't but about the middle of the morning we had us a little disaster. That old ladder they'd leaned up against the back corner, hit was sigogglin before anybody ever set a foot to it, everybody'd seed hit and nobody said a word about it, and sure enough it kicked out from under one of the men up top and he come down right into the loose hay they had piled up for the stock. Scared the whole crowd of us half to death, folks a-hollering and a-running for him, but he come up out of that hay pile cussing and laughing both at the same time, not hurt nowhere but his pride, and his wife stood there with her hand over her mouth not knowing whether to cry or take a switch to him.

Right after that is when the argument got going, over whether to rip all the old sheathing off or just lay the new tin right over what was sound underneath. One fella was dead set they ought to strip it clean down to the rafters or it'd leak again within the year, and another was just as dead set that stripping it all was a waste of good daylight they didn't have to spare. Got a little hot there for a minute, voices raised up over the hammering, till the old grandmother of the place come out on the porch and hollered that they could argue over supper with a plate in front of them same as they could empty-handed, and somehow that settled hit quicker than anything anybody else had said.

Wasn't but three or four households by dinnertime had already brought something out. One brought a great pot of pinto beans and a stack of cornbread wrapped up in a dishtowel, another brought fried chicken enough for twice the crowd, one of the young wives brought a poke of biscuits still warm off the stove, and somebody's wife had made a jam cake besides. The cornbread pans got set up on the fireboard inside the house to cool while ever body kept right on working. There was sweet tea by the gallon setting out in the shade, and somebody's uncle had a jug set back for after, for them that wanted it once the hammering was done for the day.

The children got put to pulling the old nails out of the tin that come off, so it could get hauled off and sold for scrap, and gathering up every loose nail and screw that got dropped so nobody'd step on one later barefoot. Kept them busy and out from underfoot both.

And the old man of the place, must be near on ninety, wasn't about to sit up on that porch and just watch. Nobody was fixing to let him up any ladder and he knowed it, so he set himself a chair right by the barn door in his galluses and his hat and he held the chalk line and hollered up whether a row was running true or not, hearing things the rest of us couldn't half make out, used to could hear a truck coming up that road before anybody else could and hadn't lost the knack of it yet. Told him to hush and go set down and he'd tell you right quick he was borned in that house and helped his own daddy nail the first roof on that barn, and he'd be dogged if he'd sit idle while a bunch of you'uns put a new one on it.

Onliest time the whole crowd of us laughed at the same thing together was when that hound of theirs somehow got up on the porch roof after a chicken and got hisself stuck up there, standing there a-hollering like the world was ending, and here was a dozen grown men up ladders working a barn roof that every one of them stopped dead to go figure how to get a dog down off a porch. Took longer to get that dog down than it did to lay two whole rows of tin, and ever time somebody brings it up still, folks laughs the same way all over again.

We'd told ourselves we'd be done and cleaned up by suppertime. Wasn't till near on nine o'clock, a good three hours past when anybody had figured, headlights off two-three trucks turned round to shine up on that roof so the last of the ridge cap could get nailed down in the dark. But by the time we quit, that whole roof was decked and covered new end to end, gutters hung on the one side, and the old tin hauled off and stacked up ready to sell. When that last nail went in somebody hollered it was done and the whole yard let out a cheer you could probably have heared clean down to the church.

Folks didn't hardly want to leave after that. Sat around on tailgates and the porch steps a good while longer than they needed to, plumb wore out but not wanting the evening to end just yet. Whole street felt different that night, closer somehow, even knowing come morning ever body'd be back to their own selves and their own troubles same as always. But that one evening, hit wasn't like that at all.

That's about the whole of it.
