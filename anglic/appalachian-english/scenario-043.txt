I'm still half not believing the night we had, and I've told it three times a'ready and it don't get no shorter in the telling.

Come in at a quarter to eleven, poke with my supper in it under my arm, and right off the line lead's standing at the clock looking like he'd already had enough and the shift ain't even started.

The line lead: We're two short tonight. Ain't nobody called in, they just ain't here.

That right there ought to have told me how it was fixing to go. Two hands down before the first bird even hit the scalder, and no telling was they sick or just wore plumb out from the week and decided not to show. Hit don't matter which now.

Eleven o'clock, we clocked in anyhow, them of us that was there, and started spreading out to cover what them two would've done. First hour went along fine enough, or fine enough that I didn't think nothing of the belt on the chiller side running a hair slower than usual. Just a hair. I heared it dragging some, a kind of a moan in it it don't normally have, and I said something to my workmate about it.

Me: You hear that? Sounds off.

My workmate: Probably just cold. Belt gets stiff this time of year.

We let it go. That's always how it starts, ain't it, something so little you talk yourself right out of worrying on it.

Along about half past midnight is when it quit playing at being fine and just quit. The compressor on the chiller tripped out altogether, alarm going, and the whole chill room started creeping up in temperature right there in front of us on the board. That's the first real one, the first thing that night that wadn't gonna fix itself no matter how long we stood there a-looking at it.

We called the mechanic on call. Phone rung and rung. Nobody picked up for the better part of forty-five minutes, and every one of them minutes the temperature's climbing and product's backing up on the line because you can't push it through into a chiller that ain't chilling.

The line lead: Somebody try him again.

Me: Tried him twice already.

The line lead: Try him a third time then.

We was leveling the whole while, trying to hold the line moving slow so's we wasn't just standing dead, shuffling product into what cold storage we still had holding, a-hollering back and forth over the noise about where to put it all. Hit was a mess, but it was a mess we was managing, is how I seed it then.

Finally, going on one-thirty, the mechanic answered, half asleep sounding, said he might could look at it but he wasn't but a hair from home and would come. Then the line lead had to go on and wake the plant manager too, since a whole chill room down is past what any of us was cleared to decide on our own.

The plant manager, he come in maybe twenty minutes later, and he was not a happy man about it.

The plant manager: This better be as bad as they said it was, 'cause I was asleep.

The line lead: It's bad. Compressor's down and we got product backing up with nowhere cold to put it.

The plant manager: Lord. Alright. Show me.

He come through still buttoning his coat, looked like he'd throwed clothes on in the dark, and I don't guess I blame him none for being put out, getting hollered out of bed at that hour for a compressor. But he settled some once he seen it for hisself and started giving orders instead of grumbling, which I reckon is what senior folks are for.

Now right there, while the plant manager and the mechanic's both got their heads in the compressor unit, is exactly when the second thing stacked right up on top of the first, like it was waiting on it. The forklift on the dock end quit taking a charge, just dead, right when a delivery truck's backed up wanting unloaded and we got nowhere in the chiller to even receive it into besides.

My workmate, trying to hand-truck a pallet round the dead forklift 'cause we was in a hurry and shorthanded to boot, caught her ankle on the pallet jack rail and went down hard, drug the load half over with her. Scared me worse than it hurt her, I believe, but she come up limping and her wrist all scraped raw where she'd caught herself.

Me: You alright?

My workmate: I'm alright, just banged it good. Let me set a minute.

She set a minute is all, wouldn't hardly let nobody fuss over her, but the line lead made the call right then that we wasn't gonna keep running that dock with a dead forklift and everybody rushing around a busted pallet jack in the dark trying not to break their necks. We stopped that whole side of it, told the delivery driver he'd have to hold at the gate, and just worked what we could work with hands and the one working jack we had left. Wadn't the fast way, but it was the way that didn't get nobody hurt worse than a scraped wrist.

Round three o'clock the compressor come back up, the mechanic had it running again, temperature started dropping back where it belonged, and for about forty minutes there it looked like maybe, just maybe, we was gonna come out the other side of this thing in one piece. Forklift got a jump enough to limp along on. Line picked back up to near regular speed. Somebody even laughed about something, first laughing I'd heared all night.

The young one: I believe we might could still make quota.

Wasn't fifteen minutes past him saying that when the power flickered once, flickered twice, and a whole bank of lights over the packing end just went out altogether, breaker tripped somewhere they couldn't get to quick. Third thing, right on the tail of the second, right when we'd let ourselves start believing it was turning.

The plant manager, standing there in the half dark with his hands on his hips, didn't even holler that time. Just shook his head.

The plant manager: 'Course it did.

We worked the packing end by flashlight and whatever spilled over from the next section while somebody run to find the breaker, and by then the tally of what was ruined had already got to be a right smart of a list. A whole rack of product that never made it cold in time and had to be pulled. Boxes crushed under the drug pallet. Hours of line time just gone, plain gone, that ain't never coming back no matter how hard we push the rest of the night. You start keeping that list in your head somewhere round two in the morning and by five it's got long and heavy and you quit even wanting to add it up.

Last two hours, four to six, wasn't nothing but pushing. Everybody plumb wore down to nothing, running on the last of whatever we come in with, not talking much, just moving, trying to get what could still be saved out the door right and everything else set aside for the day shift to sort through. My workmate's wrist had swole some by then but she kept on, said hit'd keep till she got home.

Along about six the windows over the dock started going grey instead of black, first light I'd seen since I walked in, and by the time we clocked out proper the sky had that pale early color to it, not morning yet but not night no more either.

I walked out to the truck and just stood there a second with the poke still under my arm, empty now, and I never did feel that lift you're supposed to feel when a bad shift's finally over. Just tired. Plumb tired, all the way down, too tired even to be glad about it yet.
