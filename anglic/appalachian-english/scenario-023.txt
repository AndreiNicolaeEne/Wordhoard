Well, I told you I'd write it all down for you since you couldn't be there, and I mean to do it right, start to finish, so pour you some coffee and set a spell, this is liable to run long.

I woke up before daylight even thought about coming, laying there in the dark just a-staring at the ceiling with my heart going like a rabbit. Didn't sleep hardly a wink past four. Mama had done told me the night before, "You best get you some rest," and I said I would, and I didn't, and she knew good and well I wouldn't. Come morning I couldn't eat a bite. Had a biscuit sitting right there and I picked at the edge of it and set it down. My stomach was plumb done with the notion of food. My sister kept a-hollering up the stairs for me to come eat something before I fell out, and I hollered back I would in a minute, and that minute never did come.

Then here right before we had to walk out the door, biggest kind of a scramble broke loose, because we like to forgot the rings. I mean plumb forgot them, sitting in a little poke up on the fireboard where Daddy had put them for safekeeping so nobody would lose them, and of course that meant everybody forgot where they was. My sister was a-fighting through every purse in the house, and Mama was hollering was they in the truck, was they in my bag, and I was standing there in my dress about to come apart at the seams, and it was the littlest cousin, the one that don't hardly say two words all day, who walked in and pointed right up at the fireboard and said there they was, plain as anything. I could have kissed that child.

Getting there, both families in the one place together, that was something I don't guess I'll forget as long as I live. Now his people and mine, we'd met here and there, a supper once, a ballgame, but never all of us together in the one room at the one time, and it was something to see them all a-milling around trying to figure who went with who. His granny found my granny inside of about five minutes and they had their heads together over quilting like they'd known each other forty years.

Now who cried first, that would be my daddy. The second he laid eyes on me coming, before I'd even got halfway to him, his face just crumpled right up. Big old man, hands like a bear, and he stood there and cried like a young'un. I never in my life seen him cry, not once, and there he went, right in front of God and everybody. That set my mother off, and then it set me off, and by the time I got to where I was going I don't guess there was three dry eyes left standing.

I lost my place in the vows, right in the middle, the part about for richer for poorer, and my mind just went white as a sheet, hit was gone plumb out of my head. I stood there with my mouth open and no words coming, and everybody waiting, and I heard my groom's mama, quiet, from the front row, say the next bit under her breath just enough for me to catch it, and it come right back to me and I got through the rest of it fine. I liked to have died of embarrassment for about ten seconds there, but nobody seemed to mind it none.

Then come the part that sealed it, the rings back and forth, him a-shaking so bad he like to dropped mine, and the fella marrying us said the words over us, and that was hit, that was done, we was married. Right after that we signed the papers on a little table somebody had drug out, my aunt standing witness for my side and his uncle standing for his, both of them signing their names slow and careful like it was the most important thing they'd ever put pen to.

Then come the photographs, and Lord, if that wasn't the longest part of the whole day. The fella taking pictures kept trying to line up cluster after cluster of kin just right, all his people over here, all my people over there, then everybody together, then just the young'uns, then just the old folks, and half of them wandering off before he got them set, and him hollering "hold on now, hold on, you'uns hold still" over and over till I thought we'd be there till dark. My little cousin got restless and started up a-hollering he was hungry, and somebody's granny fell plumb asleep standing up leaned against a post.

Inside, there was a seating mix-up that liked to have caused a scene, on account of somebody had put my aunt and my groom's aunt at the very same table, and them two have not spoke a civil word to each other in going on fifteen years over some feud nobody rightly remembers the start of anymore. We caught it just in time and slid one of them down a chair before it come to anything, but there was some looks passed across that table all night, I'll tell you.

Now the food, one dish was gone inside ten minutes flat, my mother's chicken and dumplings, gone, not a lick left, people scraping the pot. And then there was a dish, some kind of a fancy little something his aunt had brought, nobody so much as touched it. Sat there the whole night looking pretty and untouched, and I felt bad about it but I reckon everybody wanted what they knew.

The toast, now that was something. His brother stood up to give it and got about two sentences in before his voice broke clean in half, and he had to stop and wipe his face and start again, and got another sentence in and broke down again, and by the end half the room was crying right along with him and he never did finish it proper, just sort of trailed off and raised his glass and everybody hollered and that was that.

There was one old boy, a cousin of his, would not sit down the whole night long. Every single song, slow or fast, didn't matter, he was up there a-dancing, by himself if he had to be, sweated clean through his shirt, galluses hanging off his shoulders by the end of it, and still going when we finally called it a night.

And somewhere in there my uncle got to telling that old story again, the one about my grandaddy and the mule and the church roof, the one every single soul at that table has heard a hundred times if they've heard it once, and he told it anyway, full length, all the way through, and everybody laughed in all the same spots they always do, like it was the first time.

By the time it was over and the last car pulled out down the holler, hit was just the two of us standing there in all that quiet, tables still setting sigogglin where folks had shoved them around all crooked, cake mashed into the floor, chairs every whichaway, and we just stood there a minute in the mess of it not saying nothing, just holding on to each other, plumb wore out and about the happiest I believe I have ever been in my whole life. We might could have cleaned a little of it up right then but we never did, just left it all setting there till morning and went on to bed. I wanted you to have all of it, every bit, on account of I know you'd have wanted to be standing right there in it with us.
