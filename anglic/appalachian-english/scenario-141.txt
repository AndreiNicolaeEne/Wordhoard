I want you'uns all setting down and not up under me, because I ain't got but the one way through this, and I mean to say it plain, all of it, and then I'm done with hit. This ain't come on me sudden. I have kept hit back going on near sixty year, and hit is time, past time, for the truth of hit to be told before I go in the ground still hauling hit around like some kind of a poke I never once set down.

This happened before some of you'uns was even borned into this world, and long before the rest of you had good sense enough to recollect it right, so let me lay hit out the way hit truly was, and then let me lay out the way hit got told after, because them two things ain't never once been the same thing, not in sixty year.

That was the year we had the best stand of burley this family ever put in the ground, right smart of acres, more than daddy'd ever cropped by hisself, and we was needing every dollar hit was fixing to bring, on account of the mules had took sick that spring and near wiped us out before we even started. Firing season come on, and somebody had to set up with that barn all night, every night, however many nights hit took, keeping the fire low and even so the leaf'd cure right and the barn wouldn't take. That was serious work. A body could burn a whole year's living to the ground in under a hour if they let that fire get away from itself, and mamma and daddy both was scairt of hit the whole season through.

My oldest brother had the watch that night. He wadn't but nineteen, but daddy trusted him with hit, on account of he was the oldest and he'd always been steady. And somewhere in the middle of the night, the barn caught. By the time anybody got down there hit was already agoing up through the tier poles, and there weren't nothing to be done but stand back off of hit and watch. We lost the whole crop. We lost the barn. We come plumb close to losing the home place too, on the money owed after hit.

And when daddy went looking for my brother, he weren't at the barn. He was up to the house, asleep in the bed, wore plumb out, and hadn't been down there in better than an hour.

Well. Some of you'uns has heared bits of hit before, but you ain't heared the whole of hit, not till tonight. Word got round the whole holler inside a week, the way word does down in these hollers, and folks give my brother a name for hit, quiet like, behind his back, and hit wadn't a kind one. Daddy hollered at him something terrible, and daddy's mouth never did shut all the way again neither, not for years. My brother wadn't never trusted with running nothing by hisself again after that, not the crop, not the money, not nothing, not for the rest of daddy's life, and he has carried that sixty year, and hit was never his to carry.

Hit was not him.

I am going to say who done it, and I ain't going to soften hit none, because I have softened hit long enough already just by keeping shut. Hit was our youngest brother.

I know because I seed hit with my own two eyes. I was eleven year old, and I had took a poke of biscuit down to the barn for whoever was setting up, same as I done most every night that season, and I come up on him, our little brother, not fourteen year old hisself, up at that barn when he had no business being there at all, agoing at that fire with a stick of green wood he'd drug up, trying to make hit burn hotter and faster than what hit was supposed to, showing off to hisself I reckon, or just being a boy that thought he knowed better than what he was told. I seen the sparks catch up in the tier poles right then, right in front of me, and I seen him run.

I never said a word. Not that night, not the next morning when daddy was a-hollering fit to wake the dead, not one time in sixty year, till right now.

I want to tell you'uns why, because hit deserves a why and not just a sorry.

I was eleven. I was scairt plumb to death of what daddy would do if he knowed hit was his baby, the one he doted on hardest. I told myself I was protecting him, and I reckon part of that was even true. But the bigger part of it, if I'm leveling with you'uns the way I ought to be, was I was scairt of daddy's anger my own self, scairt of what hit might do to this family if hit had somewheres new to land, and by the time that fear went off some, the lie had already set up hard, like something left too long before hit was worked proper. I used to could tell myself keeping shut was a mercy I was doing him. I don't know as I believe that no more.

I might could have told hit the day my oldest brother's own daughter got married and he wadn't allowed to stand up and say a word at hit, on account of this family still didn't trust him to speak for us in front of folks. I come within a breath of hit that day. I had hit right up in my throat. And I swallowed hit back down, because I told myself hit was too late by then, that hit'd only hurt worse dug up after all them years than left buried. I have told myself that a right smart of times since. I don't know as I believe hit no more neither.

I have carried this a long time, you'uns. I have set at this very table, right under that fireboard yonder with the picture of the old barn still setting up on hit, and watched my brother get talked over and talked around and talked down to on the strength of a thing he never done, one time in his whole life, and I have said nothing, every single time, and don't you think that ain't cost me something too. I am plumb wore slap out from carrying hit.

There. Hit's said.

Nobody says a word. Not a chair creaks, not a breath comes out too loud. The whole room's gone still as a held one.

I look over at his own daughter, and I can see the disbelief plain on her face, like I've took leave of my senses in my old age, like I couldn't possibly know a thing like this and have set on it. I want to say hit to you again so's there ain't no mistaking hit: I am certain. I stood not thirty foot off and seen hit with my own two eyes, and I have never for one single day since doubted what I seen.

And you, my youngest brother's own boy, setting there already getting your back up, I see hit rising in you before you've said a syllable of hit, some notion that I'm fixing to make your daddy pay now for what your other uncle's paid all these years. I ain't. This ain't about running up a new account against nobody. I am only telling what happened. What this family does with hit is between you'uns and your own hearts, not mine to settle here tonight.

My oldest brother, the one that's carried hit sixty year, hooked his thumbs up under his galluses the way he does when he don't rightly know what to do with his hands, and I watched something cross his face I ain't never seen on him before in all my years knowing him. Some of hit was plain relief, the kind that comes up off a man like breath coming easy for the first time in longer than he can even recollect. But right up under that relief come the anger, and hit is a righteous anger, and I don't fault him one bit of it, not one bit. Sixty year is a right smart of years to give up to something that was never his to give.

My youngest brother ain't said a word neither. He don't need to. His face has told hit all, plain shame, the kind that's been a-waiting sixty year to be let loose, and under that, I reckon, something like relief too, at not having to carry hit his own self no more, even if what's took its place ain't no easier a load.

I can feel the old anger coming up in this room now, finally, at last, aimed at the right place after sixty year of being aimed wrong. Let hit come up. Hit has earned the right.

And before hit does, I owe an apology of my own, and I mean to give hit plain, not as no excuse, because there ain't one to give. I am sorry. I let a wrong stand for sixty year that I could have set right, and every year I let hit stand, I done my brother a fresh wrong all over again, quieter than the first one but a wrong all the same. That is mine to answer for, and I ain't asking nobody in this room to tell me hit's alright, because hit ain't, and I don't reckon hit ever will be, not all the way.

I don't know what this family looks like from here on out. I ain't going to stand here and tell you'uns hit's all fixed now just on account of the truth finally got said, because hit ain't, and I'd be lying to you'uns all over again if I told hit that way. But we are done standing around this thing like hit was some old sigogglin shed nobody wanted to look at straight on. We are standing on hit now, plain, however hit feels under our feet. That is the most I have got to give you'uns tonight, and hit is more than I have give in sixty year, so I reckon hit will just have to do.
