Well come on in, don't stand there a-lettin the cold in, watch that first step, hit's higher than it looks and it's caught many a body off guard. This here's the porch, and I want you to know, arrivin here never felt like just gettin somewhere. You'd come up the holler road and see the smoke a-comin off the chimney and somethin in you just eased down, like your shoulders quit tryin to hold your ears up. The dog, whichever one we had that year, always beat you to the steps, a-hollerin the whole way like the world was endin, and somebody'd always holler back through the screen, "hush now, hit's just family."

The screen door, now, that thing squealed so bad you could hear it two hollers over, and Daddy kept sayin he'd oil it and never once did in all them years, so it stayed part of the sound of the place, like the house itself was a-tellin you who'd come and gone all day.

You step on in here and this is the front room, what we mostly called the front room though some of the town kin called it a living room like they was puttin on airs. This is where everbody ended up of an evenin. The fireboard sat right up over the hearth there, and on it was pictures and a clock that run slow and a little tin box a nobody remembers what was s'posed to go in. Grandaddy's chair set right there by the stove, and hit was understood, plain as day, that chair was his, wasn't nobody gonna sit in it even if he was out in the far field all afternoon. We'd set of an evenin and the radio'd be goin, or later the television, and the menfolk'd be a-talkin low about the weather or the crop or somebody's trouble, and us young'uns was s'posed to be quiet and mostly wasn't.

The mother: Get on away from that stove, you'll catch your dress afire.

The grandmother: Let the child be, she ain't gonna catch nothin, she's just a-warmin her feet.

Right through there's the kitchen, and I reckon if you shut your eyes right now you could still smell it, cause some smells don't never leave a body. Woodsmoke and coffee and somethin fried, and under all that, always, the smell of beans a-cookin, cause there was near about always a pot of pinto beans on that stove, low and slow, all day long, and the one habit that happened in that kitchen without fail was the mother standin at that stove of a mornin stirrin them beans and hollerin for whoever was still abed to get up, that the day wasn't gonna wait on nobody. She'd say the beans had to set right or they'd never be fit, and she was right about that same as she was right about most things.

The mother: If you'uns don't get up right now you'll not get a lick of breakfast, and don't think I'm foolin.

The kitchen table had one leg shorter'n the rest, propped up with a flat rock somebody'd carried in off the creek years back, and every single time somebody new set down at it and it rocked, Daddy'd say the same thing.

The daddy: Table's a mite sigogglin, don't lean on it too hard now.

And everbody'd laugh like it was the first time anybody ever heard it, even though he said it plumb near every day of his life.

Back through here's where we all slept, and hit weren't near as many rooms as folks, so you learned to share. Us girls was three to a bed in the back room, and the boys had the loft up over, where it was hot as blazes in summer and you could see your breath in winter, and they'd be up there half the night a-fightin and a-hollerin till somebody hollered up the stairs for em to hush. The baby slept in with the mother and daddy long as there was a baby, which there mostly was.

The eldest: Quit your kickin, you'uns act like the bed's twice the size it is.

The youngest: Well move over then, you're a-hoggin the whole quilt.

That little room off the kitchen there, that was Grandmother's, and wasn't nobody else's, not even when she was poorly and down in Knoxville at the hospital a spell, it just set there waitin on her, her things right where she left em, and you didn't go in there to play even though the quilt on that bed was the prettiest one in the house, pieced by her own mother befores she was even borned. That room had a smell all its own too, camphor and dried flowers, and it was hers the way the front room chair was Grandaddy's.

Now the corner I claimed for my own, if you want to know, was up under the back porch, a little dug-out spot where I kept things a body wouldn't think to look for, a cigar box with rocks and a bird's egg shell and a knife I wasn't s'posed to have. Wasn't hardly big enough for me to set in even then, but I thought hit was the safest place in the whole world, and I reckon for them years, it was.

Out the back door there's the yard, if you can call it a yard, mostly hard dirt where the chickens run and a garden off to the side that fed us near about all year. The clothesline run from the corner of the house out to a locust post, and there was always somethin a-flappin on it. The privy set out past the woodpile them first years befores we got indoor plumbin put in, and lord, in January that was a trip you didn't take unless you had to.

The neighbors, well, you could see their smoke same as they could see ours, close enough you'd hear their dog answer ours of a night, close enough of a summer evenin you could hear em on their own porch a-talkin, though you couldn't make out the words, just the shape of the voices going back and forth.

Ever mornin, right about the same time, you'd hear the school bus a-grindin its way up the holler road, same low growl every day, and you could set your watch by it if anybody'd had a watch worth settin. And of an evenin, right at dusk, old Mister up the road drove his mule and wagon back down past the house, same time, same slow clop, headed home from wherever he'd been all day, and you'd see him go by the window same as you'd see the sun go down.

That porch step I mentioned, the high one, that thing was always broke in some fashion or another, always a board loose or a nail a-workin its way up through, and Daddy was forever sayin he'd get to it.

The daddy: I'll get to that step here directly.

He never did get to it directly, not in all the years we lived there, and it's a family joke to this day, somebody'll say "directly" and everbody knows exactly what that means.

Come spring the whole place changed its feel, windows throwed open and the smell of the ground a-turnin, and by summer the porch was where you lived, shelling beans of an evenin with whoever was around. Fall brought the smell of woodsmoke back strong and the sound of somebody splittin kindlin all afternoon, and winter closed the house up tight and small and dark by four in the evenin, everbody drawed in close to that stove.

Right out front on the road, on a summer evenin, all the young'uns up and down the holler'd gather and play till it was too dark to see, some kind of tag or hide-and-go-seek that went on for hours, and the mothers'd holler out one by one for their own to come in, and you learned the shape of your own name hollered out over ever'body else's.

Last clear time I recollect bein there proper, it was an evenin much like that, settin on that porch step, the broke one, with the family all around, beans done, dishes done, somebody's radio a-playin low, and I remember thinkin, not in words exact, just a feelin, that I ought to remember this. And I have.

The house still stands, if you want to know, though it ain't ours no more and it's sigogglin bad now, leanin the way an old body leans, and the holler road got paved some years back so you don't hear tires on gravel no more comin up it. I drove by here here here not too long ago and near about didn't know it was the same porch, till I seen that step, still broke, still a-waitin on somebody to get to it directly.

Anyhow. That's the whole of it, far as I can walk you through it. Come on, I'll show you where the garden used to set.
