I'll tell it to you'uns the way it was told to me, for that's the only way I know it, and I don't reckon I've got the straight of every last bit of it, but this here's as near as we've kept it down through the years.

Before ever my people was here in this holler, they was over in the old country, up in the north of Ireland, renting ground they never owned a foot of, same as their daddies before them had done. Times got hard there, the rent went up and the crop failed two years running, and there wasn't nothing left for a body to do but leave or starve, and my great-great-grandfather, he wasn't but a young man then, said he'd go on first and see could he make a way, and if he could, he'd send back for the rest.

He went alone. Didn't have nobody with him but a cousin that come along, and I'll get to that directly, for it's the hard part of the telling.

The crossing was worse than anybody tells it now. Six weeks on the water, packed down in the hold with more souls than that boat had any business carrying, and sick the whole way, half of them, with a fever going through like fire through dry broom sedge. His cousin took sick somewhere past the middle of it and didn't never get up again, and they buried him at sea, wrapped down in a piece of sailcloth, and that's the only way my great-aunt would ever speak of it, "the cousin we lost on the water," never by name, like the name itself was too much to say out loud anymore. I don't know as anybody living today could tell you what that cousin was even called. It just wasn't spoke, and nobody asked.

He landed with plumb near nothing to his name. What he'd carried fit in one poke slung over his shoulder, and down in that poke, wrapped in a shirt so it wouldn't get knocked about, was a little fiddle that had been his own daddy's before him. That same fiddle is kept in this family right now, today, hanging up over the fireboard at my uncle's place, and nobody plays it much anymore for fear of breaking it, but it's there, and it come every mile of that whole way in a poke on a young man's back.

There wasn't no work waiting on him when he got here, and it was a right smart while before there was. He tried this and that, went where he heared hands was wanted, and for the longest he had no place of his own at all, not a room, not a roof, nothing he could rightly call his. A family up the way took him in and let him bed down in a corner of their place same as kin, though he wasn't no kin to them at all, just a stranger they'd took pity on, and he stayed on there better than a year, working off what he owed them in trade, splitting rails, hauling, whatever a body needed doing that season.

Then a man over the ridge was looking for help at his forge, and my great-great-grandfather went to him and hired on, day work at the start, a day here and a day there, nothing steady, but it was cash money and it was the first real work he'd found since he'd stepped off that boat. He'd stand there in his shirtsleeves and his galluses sweating over that fire twelve hours at a stretch and be glad of the chance to do it. He took to it, learned the trade off that man same as if he'd been prenticed to him proper, and blacksmithing is what he done the whole rest of his life, and there's them in this family blacksmithing yet, right down to my own uncle, on account of it.

He saved back what he could, a little at a time, and it took him the better part of two years, two years and some months over, is how it's always been told, before he'd put by enough to send passage money home for his mother and the rest of them. Two years he was over here by himself, working and saying nothing to nobody about how hard it was, and them over there not knowing from one day to the next was he living or dead. I have thought on that a right smart, what that must have been like on both ends of it, him a-working alone in a strange place and them a-waiting on a letter that might could take months to come if it come at all.

When they did finally get here, wasn't but a few years after that the family got ground of its own, up this same holler, not fine ground by any means, a sigogglin old cabin already standing on it when they first come to it, leaning so bad you'd have swore it would go clean over in the next hard wind, but it was theirs, and that's the whole difference right there. Hit don't matter how poor a place is if it's your own. They fixed what needed fixing on it and built onto it besides, and it's that same ground, near enough, that some of us is on to this day.

And it was there the children was borned. That's the plain truth of what made it home and not just a stopping place a body was passing through. You can live somewhere a long while and still be only halfway there in your own mind, but once there was children a-hollering their first holler right there in that cabin, in this holler, if you don't mind me saying it thataway, then it quit being the place they'd landed and it become the place they was from. That's when the old country stopped being home for good and this one started being it.

That's the shape of it, near as I ever heared it, and that's how I'm passing it on to you'uns now, the same way my grandmother passed it to me, sitting by the fire of an evening, her a-telling it the very same as I'm a-telling it to you, word for word near as I can keep it. She'd tell it slow and she'd stop at the same places every time, same as if the stopping was part of the story too, and I do the same now without hardly meaning to.

The grandbaby: Did they never go back? Not even the one time?

Me: Not the one time. Wasn't nothing left back there to go back to, and by the time there might could have been, there was children here that had never knowed anywhere else in their whole lives, and you don't pull children up by the roots for the sake of a place their granddaddy used to could call home.

The grandbaby: Is the fiddle still up there at his place?

Me: Still up there, hanging right over the fireboard same as always. Go on and look at it sometime when we're up that way, but don't you go a-taking it down without you ask first. Some things you keep just for the keeping of them, and that fiddle's one.
