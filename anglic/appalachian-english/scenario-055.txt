Lord, where do I even start with you. You missed a day, I'll tell you that much, you missed a whole day.

We was fixing to be loaded and gone by eight, had it all planned out the night before, and here it come nine-thirty and we ain't even had coffee good yet. That's how it goes though, ain't it. You lay it all out plain as day the night before and the morning just laughs at you. First thing, I go out to back the truck up to the porch and hit won't turn over. Just a-clicking at me, click click click, like hit's mocking me. Battery plumb dead, and we hadn't even carried the first box out the door yet. So there I am under the hood in my good shirt, the one I meant to keep clean all day, cussing at cables, and the day ain't even started.

Then here comes my brother-in-law, didn't nobody call him, didn't nobody ask him to come, just pulled up in that old rattly truck of his with jumper cables already in the bed like he knowed.

Brother-in-law: Heared you was moving today. Figured you'd need another set of hands and you're too hard-headed to call and ask for one.

He weren't wrong. We got the truck jumped and going and by then it was near on ten o'clock and the mother was already out on the porch a-hollering that we was burning daylight.

The mother: You'uns gonna stand around jawing all morning or are we gonna get this house loaded?

So we got after it. Boxes first, the easy stuff, lamps and linens and the kitchen things all wrapped up in towels. And it was going good, going right along, till the eldest was carrying that big mirror, the one that hung over the fireboard since before any of us was borned nearly, been in the family I don't know how many years, and hit slipped. Just slipped right out of her hands on the porch steps and hit that concrete and shattered ever which way.

The eldest: Oh Lord! Oh no, no no no...

And the whole yard went up hollering at once, everybody a-yelling different things, the mother hollering don't touch it you'll cut yourself, my brother-in-law hollering stand back, and the eldest just standing there with her hands over her mouth like she'd broke her own leg instead of a mirror. We swept it up best we could and didn't nobody say much for a good while after that. Some things you can't replace and everybody knowed it and didn't nobody need to say it out loud.

We kept on loading through the middle of the day, and somewhere in there a box went missing. To this day I couldn't tell you what happened to it. It had the good dishes towels wrapped around, some of the canning jars, a few things off the fireboard that didn't get broke. We had it counted, we know we had it, and then it just weren't there when we got to the new place. Might could have left it setting in the yard and somebody drove off without seeing it, might could have got left in the truck bed and bounced out on the highway for all I know. We looked and looked. Never did turn up. Right smart of good stuff in that box too, and it's just gone, like the mountain opened up and swallowed it.

Then come the couch. Lord, that couch. It weren't nothing special to look at but it was heavy as a coffin and near about as awkward, and my brother-in-law wanted to carry it one way and I wanted to carry it another and we stood there in the yard a-fighting about it for a good ten minutes.

Brother-in-law: You got to go feet first through a doorway, everybody knows that.

Me: Not through this doorway you don't, this doorway's sigogglin, it ain't square, you got to angle it or hit won't go a-tall.

Brother-in-law: Hit'll go fine if you'd just listen to me for once...

We tried it his way first, feet first, straight on, and it hung up in the frame and wouldn't budge no matter how we shoved. Tried turning it on its end. Tried tipping it sideways. That stairwell down to the new place weren't no better, narrow as a gun barrel and the ceiling low, and we turned that couch every way a couch can be turned, this way, that way, upside down near about, and it just would not go through. We liked to have tore the door frame off trying. Finally had to take the legs off it right there in the yard, both of us on our knees with a screwdriver, hot and wore out and not speaking to each other hardly, before it would slide through at all.

And about the time we got that settled the sky went dark on us. Been building all afternoon, that heavy gray building up over the ridge, and it broke open right while we had the mattresses half in half out of the truck. Come down a hard rain, not no little sprinkle, a real gulley-washer, and we was a-running boxes in under it, everybody soaked through, cardboard just going to mush in our hands.

By the time the rain let up some the mother had sandwiches made, just made them up on the tailgate out of whatever was left in the cooler, and we stood right there in the yard eating among all them wet boxes, nobody sitting down, nobody talking much, just standing there chewing and looking wore slap out.

The mother: Best sandwich you'll ever eat, standing up in the mud like a heathen.

And she weren't wrong about that either. Saved the whole afternoon, that did.

The last loads was the worst. That's always how it goes, ain't it, the last little bit of nothing that don't weigh nothing wears you out worse than the furniture did. My arms was shaking just carrying lamps by then. My brother-in-law had done gone quiet, just a-trudging back and forth to the truck not saying a word, and the eldest was sitting on the tailgate rubbing her hands like they'd cramped up on her.

Before we locked up I walked back through the old place one more time, all through them empty rooms, our steps just a-echoing off the bare walls, and it don't look like nothing when it's empty, don't look like the house you lived in at all. Stood a minute in the front room where the fireboard still had the pale square on the wall where that mirror hung, and I don't mind telling you it got me a little, standing there.

We didn't pull into the new place till way past dark, headlights the only light we had to unload by, and we was short a box of good dishes and canning, short one whole mirror that was in the family longer than I been alive, and about wore down to nothing ourselves. Went to bed that night on a bare mattress with boxes stacked to the ceiling all around us. But we made it. Hit was a long day, I'll tell you what, a long day.
