Set your coffee up there on the fireboard and get you comfortable, this here takes a minute to tell right. Everybody's been a-hinting around about it since you'uns moved onto the street, but ain't nobody heared it told proper start to finish, so I'll give it to you the way it actually went.

It commenced back in the spring, or that's when the first word of it come round anyhow. The one that lives down at the bottom of the holler, where the crick bends, said she'd seed something big and dark a-moving through her side yard of an evening, low to the ground and quick, gone into the laurel before she got a good look at it. She told it around some, but folks mostly waved it off. Said she'd been up too late, said her eyes was playing tricks on her, said hit was likely just somebody's dog got loose. Nobody put much stock in it at the time. First telling of a thing like that, everybody's got a reason it ain't so.

Wasn't but two, three weeks after when the one two doors up from the church, been on this street since she was borned and the one everybody trusts to tell a thing straight without dressing it up none, seed it too. Middle of the day, no less, right out past her woodpile. Said hit come up out of the tree line big and easy, moving like hit owned the ground hit walked on, and just looked at her a good long minute before hit turned back into the trees. Wasn't nobody calling it imagination after that. If she seed it, hit was real, and the whole street knowed it inside a day.

From there on hit got seen right smart. Somebody'd catch hit of a night with its head down in a poke of trash somebody'd set out, tipping the bins besides. Somebody else caught hit down in the garden rows, tore up the better part of a row of corn and left tracks the size of your hand in the soft dirt. We used to could leave a scrap poke out on the back step overnight without no trouble at all, but not no more, not since hit come around.

That's when the street split, plain as anything. One camp, and I'd say they was the bigger part, held hit wasn't doing nothing but what a wild thing does, looking to eat, and if you kept your bins shut up tight and your garden fenced decent hit'd move on through same as it come. Leave hit be, was their whole argument, plumb simple. The other camp, and they was loud about it, held hit was bound to get bolder the closer in it come, and somebody was going to get hurt or lose something dear before it was over, and the thing needed dealt with one way or another.

Then the little one's dog turned up missing off its chain down at the far end. Two days gone before it come dragging back half starved and tore up some, and wasn't a soul could say for certain what got it into that state. Could've been a fight with another dog, could've been near anything out in them woods. But once folks got to talking, the not-knowing didn't slow nobody down none, and most everybody's guess landed square on the thing living up in the trees.

It come to a head proper at the get-together we have of an evening down at the community building, when the weather allows. Wasn't meant to be about the animal at all, just the regular sit and visit, but it got there quick.

The one wanting it left alone: Hit ain't done nothing but be hungry, same as any of us would be. We might could just fix our bins right and be shed of the whole trouble without hunting nothing down.

The one wanting it dealt with: Hungry's fine till hit's your grandbaby standing at that bin door of an evening. I ain't waiting on that to find out.

The one wanting it left alone: You ain't got a lick of proof hit's dangerous. You got a torn-up dog and a guess, and that's all you got.

The one wanting it dealt with: And what if I'm right, and we set here a-arguing about proof till somebody gets hurt bad?

It went on that way a good while, voices up, a couple of them near shouting, till the one that generally keeps the peace around here had to step in and say we wasn't getting nowhere a-hollering at each other in the dark, and if we was leveling with each other, half what got said in the heat of it wasn't nothing nobody truly meant.

One household didn't wait on the rest of the street to decide nothing. The man of that house, the one always in his galluses even of an evening, said he might could handle it his own self, no need to trouble the whole street over it, and put out bait of some kind to draw it off away from the houses. Whatever he done, it didn't take. Hit come back around same as before, and all he got for his trouble was a shed door left plumb sigogglin on its hinges and no less bother than he started with.

Wasn't long after that the whole business shook loose for good. One of the men on the street, coming in late off work, walked right up on it in the dark by his own back step, close enough he heared hit breathing before he ever seed it. Said his heart about come up out of him right there. Hit didn't come at him, just stood a second and ambled off easy as you please, but the fright of it stayed with him a good while, and he wasn't shy about telling it to anybody that'd listen.

That done it. Even some that had been a-holding out for leaving it be started saying maybe hit was time to get somebody in that knowed what they was doing, because the street couldn't agree on nothing amongst itself, half saying what they'd heared was proof enough hit was dangerous, half saying hit hadn't hurt a soul yet and was just unlucky in where hit'd wandered to, same as any wild thing pushed out of its own ground by houses a-creeping up on it year after year.

So the street got somebody in, a fella that works with the wildlife folks, been at it for years, knows the ways of such animals better than any of us setting around arguing about it of an evening. He come and looked the ground over good, looked at where hit'd been getting in at the bins, walked the tree line a spell, and told the street plain: hit wasn't after nobody, hit was after easy eating, and the way to be shed of the trouble for good wasn't to hunt the thing down, hit was to quit feeding it without meaning to.

The street settled on his terms without much more fuss after that. Bins got latched down proper, some of them with a strap and a clip so hit couldn't work them open no more. Gardens got fenced better than they'd ever been fenced before in all the years this street's stood. And it was agreed general, without nobody writing it down nowhere, that if hit come around you let it be and let it go on its way, and you don't leave nothing out that'd draw it back to try you again.

Since then hit's calmed down to about nothing. Somebody'll still catch a glimpse of hit crossing through of an evening, and there's always somebody keeping half an eye toward the tree line out of habit now, more than out of worry. The alarm wore off same as it come on, little by little, till most folks quit thinking on it much at all except when somebody new moves onto the street and wants the whole of it told to them proper, same as I'm a-telling it to you right now. These days it ain't hardly spoke of as trouble no more. Hit's just the tale about the year that thing moved in, and folks tell it more for the telling of it than for any fear that's left.
