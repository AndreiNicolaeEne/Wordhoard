It generally comes on of an evenin', long about the time the light's gone thin behind the ridge and everbody's quit their supper and got to settin'. Somebody new to the holler'll ask why we don't walk the bend below the falls after dark, and that's when it starts, same as it's always started, long as I can recollect. I ain't one to go huntin' the tellin' of it. Hit finds its own way round to me.

Now I'll tell it to you the one time, straight through, and you just listen, the way you'uns has always done for me.

Whatever it is, ain't nobody round here ever laid eyes on it proper. You don't see it first. You hear it first, and by the time you'd be seein' anything hit's already got your feet movin' the wrong direction. Some says hit's got a shape like a man, all sigogglin and bent up wrong at the joints, and some says hit ain't got no shape a-tall, just a black spot where the dark gets darker. I don't hold much with either one. What I hold with is hit takes a voice long before hit ever takes a shape, and the voice is the whole of the danger.

Hit calls out. And the meanness of it, the real meanness, is hit don't call in no stranger's voice. Hit calls in the voice of whoever you'd come runnin' for. Your mother. Your own young'un. Whoever you love enough to leave the path for. Hit'll holler your name in that voice, out from the trees where ain't nobody rightly standin', and the whole aim of it is to walk you off away from the road and off away from anybody could help you when you got there. That's the shape of the meanness in it. Not teeth. Not claws. Just gettin' you alone.

Hit don't come whenever hit pleases, neither. Hit comes after midnight and not a minute before, and hit only comes on the nights with no moon a-tall, when you can't see your own hand out past your wrist. A night with any moon showin', you could walk that bend a-whistlin' and never hear a thing.

The bend below the falls, that's where hit's tied to, here. Not the whole crick, not the whole mountain, just that one stretch where the road doubles back on itself along the water and the trees lean in close enough to touch overhead. I've heared tell there's other hollers got their own callin' thing, their own bend or their own stretch of road, and I expect that's true, but I ain't puttin' claim on nothin' past what's ours. This one belongs to us and to that bend and nowhere else.

I first heared it myself when I wasn't but knee high, from my own grandmother, settin' by the fireboard of a winter night with her hands goin' at some mendin'. She told it plain, the way you'd tell somebody the crick was up or the corn was in. Wasn't no laughin' in it when she got to the callin' part, but I recall she'd smile some at the end of it, and that smile never did climb all the way up into her eyes. I was too young then to know what that meant. I know now.

First thing anybody'll show you, was you to go lookin', is the ground right at that bend. Ain't nothin' ever growed there. Not grass, not briar, not the ragweed that'll take a-hold anywhere else on God's earth. My own daddy tried plantin' somethin' there once, just to see, and it come up wrong and died inside the week, same as everthing before it. That ground's been bare longer than anybody livin' can account for.

Second thing, and I'll tell you plain hit's the kind of thing that's always heared secondhand, is talk of folks passin' that bend of a night and hearin' somethin' a-hollerin' off in the trees that weren't no owl and weren't no cat, somethin' that trailed off into a name, half a name, before it quit. Ain't nobody I know seed it themselves. It's always somebody's cousin, or somebody that used to live down past the forks, or a fella that worked timber through there one season and wouldn't go back. That's how it's always come to me, and I expect that's how it'll keep comin'.

But the one that gets told whole, every single time, front to back, is what happened to my own uncle, back when he wasn't much more'n a young man himself. He was comin' up the bend late, past midnight, no moon showin' a-tall, and he heared his name called out clear as anything, in a voice he swore up and down was his own mother's, and she two years in the ground already by then. He said he took three, maybe four steps off the road toward where the voice had come from before somethin' stopped him, he never could rightly say what, and turned him back around toward the road again.

To this day, and he lived a right long life after, he'd tell you the very same words if you asked him: hit weren't her. I knowed her voice, and hit weren't her, but hit was close enough I'd already started walkin' before I knowed that. Word for word, that's what he'd say, ever time, same as the last.

The detail that'll quiet even the hardest doubter is that he never could say what stopped him them three or four steps in. Not a sound, not a light, nothin' he could point to. Somethin' just stopped his feet, and that's a thing folks don't have no easy answer for, so mostly they just go quiet on it a spell.

But the detail that gives the doubters their openin' is that he'd been up at a gatherin' most the evenin' and had took a right smart of drink before he ever started up that road, and there's them that'll say a man that's had all that in him might could hear his own mother callin' in a bullfrog if he was of a mind to. I don't argue that point with nobody. I just tell what he told.

Now I'll say this much honest, the story ain't never come down the same from every mouth that's told it. My grandmother told it soft, like she was handin' you somethin' fragile. Others in the family has told it hard, plumb mean about it, like they was tryin' to scare the breath out of you a-purpose. I've heared it a dozen ways from a dozen kin and I ain't never minded the difference. A story that's worth keepin' bends a little in ever hand it passes through. That don't make it less true. Might make it more.

What it's left us with, all these years on, is a plain little rule, and I expect you'uns'll keep it same as I have. Was your name to get called from off in the dark, from the trees, from anywhere you can't see, you don't answer. Not a word. Not till you've looked with your own two eyes and know for certain who's a-callin' you. Might be nothin'. Might be somebody that really does need you. But you look first, ever time.

I'll tell you plain, I don't believe it, not the whole of it, not in the daylight anyhow. But I ain't never once, not one single time in all my years, answered a callin' voice in the dark without I seed who it was first. And I don't reckon I ever will.
