Mamaw: Well, look what the cat drug in. Get on up here, you'uns, and let her set down 'fore you all start in on her. Set your coat there on the fireboard and tell us how it is off in that city.

The Cousin: Hey Mamaw. It's a lot, I'll tell you that. Big. Bigger than anybody round here could hardly picture, and loud, loud in a way that don't never let up, not even of a night.

Me: Bigger than home, I reckon that goes without saying. But how big is what I want to know. Bigger than from the holler up to town, or bigger than that?

The Cousin: Aw, it ain't nothing like from the holler to town. Some days it takes the better part of a hour just to get from one side of it clean over to the other, and that's if don't nothing go wrong. A wreck, or ever body trying to get somewhere the same time as you, and you're setting there not moving a inch.

The Aunt: A hour?? Lord have mercy, we can get from here to the Food City and back in twenty minutes and think that's a far piece.

The Cousin: I know it. First while I was up there I like to never got used to the racket of it. Ever direction you look they's cars, and horns a-honking, and folks a-hollering out the windows at you if you don't move quick enough to suit em. Home you might hear a dog bark of a night, or the crick running high after a rain. Up there they's always something going. Sireens, and music blaring out of somebody's car, and I don't know what all.

The Uncle: Hit don't never hush up, is what you're telling us.

The Cousin: Hit surely don't. First few months I couldn't hardly sleep for it. Now I don't hardly notice, which I reckon is worse in its own way.

Me: Well where do you even live at, up there? Cause when you said "city" I pictured you off in one of them tall buildings stacked plumb up to the sky.

The Cousin: Naw, not like that. I'm in a little apartment, smaller than Mamaw's front room and kitchen put together, if you can believe it. And crooked-built too, the whole staircase up to it's sigogglin, one step higher than the next like whoever built it never owned a level. Costs more a month than what Daddy's old house note used to run, and that's just for that one little box.

Mamaw: Lord.

The Cousin: Groceries the same way. A poke of what you'd get down at the IGA for fifteen dollars runs you thirty up there, easy, and half of it not even good.

The Aunt: For the same stuff?

The Cousin: Same stuff, or worse, half the time.

Me: Are the people up there friendly, at least? Or is it like they say, ever body just a-rushing past with their head down?

The Cousin: That ain't a plain yes or no kind of a question, if I'm honest with you. They's friendly in their own way, just a different way than here. Won't nobody stop and talk to you at the bus stop like they would at the store here, and don't nobody come knock on your door with a plate of something cause they heared you was poorly. You got to go looking for it. But you get to knowing your neighbors some, over time, and they'll help you out if you need it bad enough.

The Uncle: Don't sound like friendly to me. Sounds like they's just polite.

The Cousin: Might could be some truth in that.

The Aunt: We wasn't sure if you was leveling with us or just being smart, saying the sugar thing about the cornbread. Was you leveling?

The Cousin: Oh I was dead level about that one, don't you worry.

Mamaw: Well tell us about the market, where you go to get your things. Is it like the Food City, or bigger?

The Cousin: They's little markets ever few blocks, and then great big ones too, bigger than anything we got round here, with about ever kind of food you could name, stuff I never even heared tell of before I went. But ain't nobody bagging your groceries and asking after your mama. You just get in and get on out.

Me: And how'd you find work up there? Was it hard on you?

The Cousin: Harder than I'd figured on. Took me the better part of two months, sending things off and setting through what they call interviews, over and over, same questions ever time. Home you know somebody that knows somebody and you're working by the next week. Up there don't nobody know you from Adam, so you got to prove the whole of it on paper first, and prove it again in person.

The Aunt: What is it you're doing, then?

The Cousin: Office work, mostly. Setting at a desk on a computer most the day. Nothing like what I growed up doing here, that's the truth.

Mamaw: Setting at a desk all day'd about kill me.

The Cousin: It about killed me the first while too.

The Uncle: Did you get lost much, first getting up there? My own sense of direction ain't worth a hill of beans even round here where I was borned and raised.

The Cousin: Lost ain't hardly the word for it. I got turned clean around three or four times that first month, worse than being lost in the woods behind the barn, cause up there ever street looks about the same as the next and don't none of em run straight like you'd expect. I'd get off at the wrong stop and end up clean on the other side of everything, having to backtrack the whole way I come. Took me a good while to learn the lay of it. I used to could get lost just walking to the store and back. Now I could about get around blindfolded, but hit took some time getting there.

Me: Might could ask somebody, though, when you're turned around like that.

The Cousin: You might could, if you could find somebody that'd stop long enough to tell you, and half of em walking too fast to even look at you.

The Aunt: What about the food? I know you always did love your cornbread and beans.

The Cousin: Food's better in some ways, worse in others, if I'm honest. They's food up there from ever corner of the world, stuff I never heared the name of before, and some of it's real good, I'll give it that. But you cain't hardly find a decent pot of pintos nowhere up there, and don't nobody make cornbread right. They put sugar in it. Right smart of sugar too, not just a little.

Mamaw: Sugar in cornbread. That ain't cornbread, that's cake by another name.

The Cousin: That's just exactly what I told em.

The Uncle: Your Papaw wore galluses to church his whole life and never thought twice on it. I bet don't nobody up there even know what galluses is.

The Cousin: They do not. I tried to tell a fellow at work about em and he looked at me like I'd growed a second head.

The Uncle: So is it better up there, or is it better here? Plain out, now.

The Cousin: Aw, now, that ain't a plain question neither, whatever you think. They's things I like about both, and things I cain't stand about both. Depends on the day you catch me, I reckon.

The Aunt: That ain't no kind of a answer and you know it.

The Cousin: Hit's the only one I got.

Me: Well, you fixing to come on back home someday, for good? Or you plumb settled up there now?

The Cousin: I don't rightly know. Might. I think on it right smart, especially of a evening when it's quiet here and I can hear myself think for the first time all week. You was borned in that back bedroom, Mamaw always says, like that ought to settle it. But I got a good bit built up there now too, and hit ain't nothing you just walk off from easy, not after this long. We'll see, I reckon. Ask me again next time I'm home.

Mamaw: I aim to ask you ever single time, till you give me a answer I like.

The Cousin: I don't doubt it one bit, Mamaw.
