I keep the month money in a coffee tin back of the flour on the pantry shelf, ever since the bank in town got to be more trouble than hit's worth. Wadn't but a hundred and some odd dollars set back in there for what all the month needs - the propane, the feed, whatever the truck throws a fit over next. I went to count it of a evening and it come up short. Counted hit twice, laid the bills out flat on the table and went through ever one slow. Still short. Checked the other place too, the tin box up under the bed ticking, case I'd moved it my own self and forgot, but hit wadn't there neither.

So I commenced asking, easy at first, going round the house.

Me: Has anybody been in my coffee tin? I ain't fussing yet, I just need to know did somebody move it.

The husband: Not me. Ain't laid a hand on it.

The eldest: I never even knowed you kept it in the flour.

Me: Well somebody's knowed it, cause hit's a right smart lighter than it ought to be.

Ever one of them give the same flat no, quick and easy. But it come to me then that only one soul had been left alone in this house the day I recollect it going missing - the rest was down to the holler at church that whole morning, and the youngest stayed back sick.

Me: You was the only one here that Thursday. Where'd my money get to?

The youngest: I never touched a dime of it, and I don't like what you're saying to me.

Me: I ain't saying nothing I don't mean by it. Money don't just up and walk off on its own hit.

The youngest: Well it didn't walk off with me! You can go on thinking what you want, I reckon you already have your mind made up.

Me: I think what the plain fact tells me to think, and the fact is you was the only one here!

Voices got up loud enough the dog commenced a-hollering out on the porch, and neither one of us backed off a inch. The youngest stood there red in the face swearing up and down, and I stood there knowing what I knowed, and that's about where hit was left, both of us just as sure as the other and not one bit closer to the truth of it.
