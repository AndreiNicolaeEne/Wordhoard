Me: Well, since you'uns are new up the holler and bound to hear talk of it before the week's out, you might as well get it from me and not from somebody a-carrying tales both ways. I'll tell you how it was, and after that you can make of it what you want, for I ain't got but the one side, mine.

There was a time, and hit weren't so long ago neither, when them next door and us was as close as kin. Closer, some years, for kin can live three states off and never lift a finger, and them was right there. We swapped garden truck back and forth over that fence all summer, corn for beans, beans for tomatoes, and if her canner run short she'd send the boy over for mine and I never thought twice on it. Her man used to could fix about anything with his hands, and he's the one got our truck out of the ditch that January it iced over so bad, worked at it near two hours in the dark and wouldn't take a dime for it. She kept a picture of both our families setting up on her fireboard for years, right in the middle where anybody coming in the door would see it first. We set on one porch or the other most every evening warm enough to allow it, and the young'uns run back and forth between the two yards like there weren't no line there at all. I mean that plain. Years of it. Right smart of years.

Then he built that shed.

Wasn't nothing to it, to hear him tell it after, just a little lean-to for his tools and his mower, but he set it near on top of the line, close enough the eave dripped onto our side ever time it rained, and the runoff cut a little gully right along where I had my flowers put in, plumb ruined the bed by June. I let it go the first spring. Told myself hit was a small thing and not worth making bad blood over, and anyhow I figured he'd move it once he seed what the water was doing to my ground. He didn't. Second spring I mentioned it to him direct, kept it easy, said something like, "you might could see to that eave 'fore it washes my whole bed out," and he allowed he would. Never did. I let a summer go by and brought it up again, same easy way, and got the same answer and the same nothing after. Third time I asked, I won't lie to you, I had an edge on it by then. Told him plain I was plumb tired of asking pretty.

Word of it went back and forth some, the way things do on a short street, mostly carried by the woman two doors down, for she was in and out of both houses regular and thought herself a peacemaker, though I've come to believe she done more stirring than settling. She come to me and said the woman next door was saying I'd gone up to the fence a-hollering and cussed at the man in front of his own children, which I never did, not once, not a raised word in it till maybe that third asking, and even then hit was nothing you could rightly call cussing. By the time it traveled back to me the story had growed itself a whole new shape, like a snowball rolled down a long hill picks up whatever's laying loose in the yard.

And when I finally went to the woman herself and asked her straight had her man ever heared me ask about that shed, she looked at me like I'd lost my mind and said nobody had ever once ignored me about a thing, that I must be thinking of somebody else, that they'd have fixed it the first time I ever opened my mouth about it if I'd truly asked. Flat denied the whole of it, like them two springs of me asking and him nodding and doing nothing never happened at all.

I won't say I done right by what I done next, but I'll own it. Her tomatoes always come in good on account of the morning sun hit her side of the garden first, and there was an old maple limb hung over from our yard that shaded her rows some afternoons, not much, but some. I had my husband take that limb down "for safety," I told him, on account of ice, and he didn't ask me twice on it, and I let her tomatoes bake in that full sun all that July without a word said.

Then the comment come back to me, through the same go-between, and it stung worse than any of the rest put together. The woman next door had told somebody, and it got carried straight to me like these things always do, that our house had always been sigogglin from the day it was built and everybody up and down had known it, meaning not the house itself so much as the people in it, meaning us, meaning we was crooked people off crooked stock, borned that way and no help for it. I heared a lot in my time and let most of it slide off my back like water, but that one set with me. Still does, whether I like owning it or not.

I tried, after that, to put it right. Took a poke of dried apples up to her door that fall, the kind she used to ask after every year when we was close, and stood there and told her I didn't want this a-running on between us no more. She took the poke, I'll say that much, but she stood in that door and told me she didn't know what I expected to fix with a bag of apples after everything that had been said, and shut it soft, which somehow was worse than if she'd slammed it.

The street took sides after that, near enough down the middle as makes no difference. The Sunday school folks tended toward her, on account of she sung in the choir and I never did care for it, and the ones that worked shift work tended toward us, I couldn't rightly tell you why except maybe we'd helped more of them move furniture over the years. There was a couple old men on the block, the kind that still wear galluses even to sit on their own porch, that wouldn't take a side at all and just shook their heads when either name come up. It got to where you could stand in the road and tell by which yard a body's truck was parked in front of, of an evening, which side of it they'd landed on.

The blowup, when it come, come out in the open where the whole block heared it, right out at the mailboxes of a Saturday morning. Her man said something about our dog getting in his trash, and I said something back about that shed and that eave and the flowers it ruined, and it went on from there, both of us a-hollering by the end of it, no use pretending otherwise, half the street standing on their porches watching like it was a show put on just for them. Somewhere in the middle of it she accused my husband of taking a chainsaw off her back porch that had gone missing that spring, said she knew it was him on account of who else would've wanted it, and he denied it flat, said he hadn't set foot on her property since the day of that maple limb, and far as I know that's the truth of it, for I'd have knowed if a strange chainsaw turned up in our shed.

Hit's been going on nine years now, near ten, since any word has passed between them two houses that weren't spoke through gritted teeth at the mailboxes. Nine years of two porches sitting empty of each other of an evening, when they used to be full most nights the weather allowed. You'll see it yourself, time you're here a while, how her young'uns won't cut through our yard no more even though it'd save them the whole way round, how my husband still won't borrow so much as a wrench off that man even when it'd save him a trip clear to town, how neither one of us goes to the other's kin's funerals no more, and there's been a couple we ought to have gone to.

And I'll tell you the plain truth of it, now that you've heared the whole of my side. Some nights I lay there and try to run it back from the start, that shed and that eave and that gully in my flower bed, and I ain't rightly sure no more which one of us started it first, him with the building or me with the asking, or maybe it was further back than either of them and I've just plumb forgot. I know my side of it. I ain't never claimed to know the whole of it.
