Well I'll tell you how the whole thing went, start to finish, on account of you asked and I ain't one to leave a story half told.

It was a bad night, awful bad, wind a-coming down off the ridge like it meant something personal by it, and I heared it a-banging around out there most of the night but didn't think nothing of it beyond the usual, on account of we get some right smart wind up here most every spring when the fronts come through. Went to bed, woke up early like always, and went out to let the dog do his business, and I seed it before I was hardly off the porch steps. The whole west stretch of fence laid out flat in the yard like somebody'd took a hand to it, rails busted, posts snapped off right at the ground, and a big old oak a-laying right across the top of it, limbs everywhere, dirt and roots pulled up out the ground where hit had stood.

First thing I done was walk the line to see whose tree that even was, on account of you got to know that before you know what's what. Now that tree had stood right along the property line for as long as anybody remembered, close enough to it that you couldn't hardly tell whose ground hit was borned on, but when I got down and looked at the stump, hit was clear enough. The trunk sat maybe two foot over onto the neighbor's side, growing crooked the whole way up, sigogglin off toward my place like hit had been reaching for the light on our side for forty years. So hit was their tree. Wasn't no argument to be had about that part, or wouldn't ought to have been.

So I walked on over there, and I want you to know I was civil about it, I wasn't out for blood, I just wanted to talk about getting the fence back up, on account of we run a few head of cattle back then and a downed fence line is a downed fence line, don't matter whose fault, the cows don't care.

The neighbor: Well now, that's just the wind, ain't it. Can't nobody help what the wind does.

Me: I hear you, but that was your tree stood right on the line, and I reckon a body's still responsible for what falls off their own ground onto somebody else's fence.

The neighbor: Hit's an act of God is what hit is. You can't fault a man for a windstorm.

Me: I ain't faulting you for the wind. I'm faulting the tree, and that tree had been leaning that way a long while before last night ever come along. Wind just finished what was already halfway done.

That's when the wife spoke up from the porch, on account of she'd been listening the whole while with the coffee going cold in her hand.

The wife: You recall two summers back, when the county boy come around checking lines for the right-of-way, and he told you'uns that tree ought to come down before it went on its own? I was standing right there when he said it.

The neighbor didn't have a whole lot to say to that. He knowed it was true, I could see it working on his face, but knowing a thing's true and being willing to hand over money for it are two different animals entirely, and he wasn't ready to be the one that backed down first. Neither was I, if I'm honest about it, though I ain't overly proud of that now.

So we stood there a good while, plumb stubborn, both of us, going round and round on who ought to pay what, him saying wind, me saying warned and ignored, back and forth like two dogs a-circling the same bone and neither one willing to let go first for fear of looking soft. We might could have stood there till Christmas the way we was going, and for about a week and a half after, that's about what we done, just not speaking much beyond a nod when we passed on the road, both of us stiff-necked as fence posts ourselves. Before all that we used to could borrow a wrench or a cup of sugar off one another without a second thought, and it was a poor feeling having that gone over one durn tree.

What broke it was the old feller that lives up the holler above us both, been there longer than either one of our families, galluses holding his pants up same as they done since before I was borned, and he come down one evening without nobody asking him to, just walked up between the two yards like it was his own business, which I reckon in a way he made it his business.

The old man: Now I ain't here to say who's right, on account of you're both a little bit right and a little bit hardheaded, which is most disputes I ever seen in fifty year up this holler. But I'll tell you what my daddy done one time when a tree of ours took out the Perrys' smokehouse. We split the cost of materials down the middle and the man whose tree it was done the extra labor. Ain't nobody had to admit nothing. The wood don't keep score.

Wasn't nobody expecting that, and I believe that's exactly why it worked, on account of neither one of us had to be the one that give in to the other, we could both give in to him instead, which don't feel like losing near as much. The neighbor allowed as how that sounded fair, and I allowed the same, and just like that the money part of it was done and over, quick as it had drug on slow before.

Rebuilding it was its own kind of work though. First morning we was out there together, it was stiff as you please, hardly two words between us past what the job needed, him a-hauling limbs on his side, me a-digging post holes on mine, working the same fence line like two men that had never met, which is a strange thing when you've knowed a fella twenty year. Went on like that most of the morning, just the sound of the saw and the digging and the wind, which had gone gentle as a lamb by then like it wanted no more to do with the whole affair.

Along about noon his boy come out carrying a poke, and in it was a mess of biscuits and some cold ham his mother had put up, and he just set it down on the sawhorse between us without a word about it, like it wasn't nothing, like that's just what you done. Wasn't till later I understood what that was. Then a little after, when I come up short a good level, I hollered over and he handed his across the line before I'd even finished asking, didn't make me come get it neither, walked it over himself. Small as them things was, that's what turned it. You can hand a man money and still be at odds with him. You hand him a tool or a plate of food you didn't have to, and something in it just lets the air out of the whole quarrel.

By the time we had that fence back up, new posts set deep and the rails doubled up better than what was there before, we was both of us talking easy again, a-laughing some about how hardheaded we'd both been over a durn tree, and he told me his granddaddy had put that stump's granddaddy in the ground right there on the line near a hundred year back, never figuring on what it'd grow up to do to two families that hadn't even been thought of yet.

That fence stands sturdier now than the one the wind took down, I'll say that plain. And him and me, we're better than even now, I believe, better than we was before that tree ever come down at all. Funny how a wind that near tore up a friendship ended up being the thing that built it back stronger than what it found.
