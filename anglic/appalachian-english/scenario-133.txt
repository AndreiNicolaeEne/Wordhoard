Well I'll tell you how the garden went this year, start to finish, on account of ever'body up the holler kept asking me about it come fall like I done something special. I never done nothing but what my own mother done and her mother before that, but I'll tell it anyhow.

Come early spring I set down at the fireboard of an evening and studied on what all to put in. Didn't have but the one plot down below the barn, the flat piece where the crick bends round, and you got to make your mind up on it before you know you're doing it, or you'll be dithering plumb into June. I settled on beans, half runners same as always, and some corn, and tomatoes, and a mess of squash and cucumber, and taters in the far corner where the ground stays a little cooler. My man wanted okra in again and I said fine, put it in, though I ain't never cared for it myself.

We didn't put nothing in the ground till a few weeks after the last cold snap broke, on account of my granddaddy always said don't you trust April, hit'll lie to you. Sure enough we had one more frost scare after Easter that had ever'body covering their tomato starts with buckets of an evening, but after that it turned off warm and stayed warm, and I mean we had us a run of weather then, sun about every day and a soft rain coming through every few nights just when things needed it, and I ain't never seen beans come up that fast in my life. Corn was knee high before I hardly knowed it. Felt like the whole garden was a-growing while you watched it.

First trouble I had was them bean beetles, the yellow spotted kind, showed up on the half runners about the time they commenced to blossom. I tried the soap water first, spraying it of a morning before the sun got up good, and I want to tell you it helped some, cut the worst of it back, but they kept a-coming anyhow, little bit every week, and I never did get shed of them entire. Just learned to live alongside them and pick the worst leaves off by hand of an evening.

Then along about the middle of July it quit raining altogether. Didn't get a drop for I believe five weeks straight, and the crick down below the plot dropped so low you could about walk across it dry-shod. I was hauling water by hand near every single day of that whole stretch, two buckets at a time up from the pump, and my shoulders ached so bad of a night I couldn't hardly lift my arm to comb my own hair. But you don't let a garden die on you after all that early work, so I kept at it.

That's about when the trouble started up with the plot-holder next to mine, over whose turn it was on the spigot down at the end of the row, the one we was both meant to share out fair. He'd get down there of a morning and run it a good hour before I ever got a chance, and I got to where I was fit to be tied over it.

Me: I don't mean no harm by it, but you had that water running near on to an hour and a half this morning and I ain't had a lick of it yet, and my squash is a-wilting something awful.

The neighbor: I didn't have it that long. Maybe forty minutes.

Me: Hit was closer to a hour and I can show you the time, I was watching the sun.

The neighbor: Well I got my own dry spell to deal with too, same as you.

We went back and forth on it a few days, both of us a little short with one another, till finally we set down on his tailgate one evening and hashed out a schedule, him mornings on the even days and me on the odd, and either one could have the evening if the other one wasn't down there using it. Worked out fine after that. He ended up giving me a start of his fall greens later on, so I reckon there wasn't no hard feelings kept.

Best thing I grew all year, hands down, was them half runners, beetles and all. Once they got going good they never did quit, I was picking a mess every three days plumb into September and still had more than I could put up. Canned twenty-some quart, gave away twice that much, and could've kept on picking if I hadn't run out of jars.

The squash was another matter. Birds got to pecking the young ones before they hardly set, and what the birds didn't get took to rotting on the vine from laying in the wet ground after them rains finally come back. Lost near half that crop between the two, and I never did find a good fix for it, just moved what fruit I could find early and let the rest go.

Right in the thick of all that, about early August, we caught us a storm come through of an afternoon, wind laid the corn down flat in three whole rows and knocked the tomato stakes every which way, plants a-hanging half out of the ground with their roots showing. Spent the better part of two days after that a-staking corn back up one stalk at a time and tying tomatoes back to what stakes I could salvage, hands blistered up something fierce. Lost a right smart of corn to it, more than I'd have liked, but saved most of what was standing.

When it all come down to tallying it up at the end, I had more beans than any two families could eat, a decent corn crop considering, taters that done real well being they never seemed to mind the dry near as much as everything else, and enough tomatoes to can sauce clean through winter. Traded off a good bit of the extra corn and half a bushel of taters to a woman up the road who keeps hens, for eggs all through the cold months, on account of we needed them a sight more than we needed another ear of corn setting in the crib. Pulled the dead vines end of October, turned the ground over, laid some leaves down over the tater bed to keep it from freezing hard, and called it done for the year.

Looking back on the whole of it, I'd call it a fine year for a garden, wins and losses both counted. You lose some to bugs and you lose some to birds and you lose some to weather no matter what you do, that's just how it goes, but you come out the other end with jars on the shelf and eggs in the spring on account of it, and that's about all a body can ask.
