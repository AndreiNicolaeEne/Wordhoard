The elder: I don't know what you're getting so worked up over. Hit's just a clock.

The younger: Hit ain't just a clock and you know it ain't. Mommy set it right there on the fireboard and told me to my face, "this one's yours when I'm gone." I heared her say it plain.

The elder: I never heared no such a thing, and I was in that house same as you was.

The younger: You was in the kitchen half the time, a-hollering at your own young'uns, you wasn't listening to nothing she said.

The elder: Don't you start with me now. I've drove her to the doctor and seen after her pills and sat up with her of a night for going on five years, and you want to fuss over a clock that don't even keep good time.

The younger: Well since we're on it, let's talk on the money too then. Three thousand and some, and I know good and well you'uns already took out more than your half before you ever called to tell me she'd passed.

The elder: That is a flat lie. I ain't touched a dime weren't mine to touch.

The younger: Might could be you just forgot to mention it.

The elder: Hit ain't about the clock and hit ain't about the money, and you know that same as I do.

The younger: No. Reckon it ain't.

The elder: She ain't but three weeks in the ground and here we're a-fighting over a fireboard clock like we was strangers.

The husband: Now you two hush a minute. Ain't a one of you going to feel a bit better hollering over this. Set it down a spell, both of you.

The younger: I don't want to lose you too, on top of her.

The elder: I don't want that neither.

Neither one of them says nothing more after that, just stand there looking plumb ashamed, like they can hear their own selves for the first time, fighting over what she left like she wasn't somebody they'd loved their whole life.
