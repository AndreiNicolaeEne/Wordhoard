I don't tell this one much, and when I do I try and tell it straight, no bigger and no smaller than it come, on account of blowing it up wouldn't be honest and shrinking it down wouldn't be neither.

It was the year the high water took the bridge out down below, and for a good long spell there wasn't but the one way acrost for anybody, an old logging road cutting over the ridge that come down through a checkpoint before it ever let out on the far side where the hospital was at. My mother had took sick up there, bad enough the doctor sent word for kin to come on, and with the bridge gone and no truck any good to us, hit meant walking. The road used to could take a truck through fine before the water come up, but not no more, not for a good while yet.

I put what little I needed in a poke over my shoulder and set out early of a morning, her admission letter folded up small and tucked in close where I wouldn't lose it. One of the young fellas working the line told the man ahead of me, and me listening right along, that hit was on account of the bridge being washed plumb out and there weren't no way to get equipment or supplies through excepting on foot, and that's how come they had it set up thataway, checking who all had business on the yonder side before they'd let you'uns on through. There was a little sigogglin shack throwed up alongside the road for the men to get out of the weather, and the line backed up from it further than I cared to see, folks a-standing in the cold shifting from one foot to the other, the wind a-whipping down off the ridge something fierce.

I ain't ashamed to say my stomach was a-churning the whole time I stood in that line. I'd heared talk of fellas turned back for one thing or another, a paper not signed right or a name spelled different on one than the other, and my mind kept on running to all the ways mine might come up short, even knowing in my head it was all in order. I thought I might could talk my way through easier if I just kept quiet and let the paper do most of the talking, and that's about what I aimed to do.

It was a right smart of a wait, better than half an hour standing on that hard-packed road before I ever got up to the front of it, and by then my feet had gone half numb and my nerve wasn't much better than my feet.

There was a fella two spots up ahead of me in the line, a stranger to those parts far as anybody could tell, and they pulled him off to the side and had him standing there a good while, going back through what little he had with him and asking him things over again he'd already answered once. Wasn't a soul in that line said one word about it. We just watched him out the corner of our eyes and kept our own faces still and went on minding what was in front of us.

When it come my turn at last, I passed my letter over.

The man at the checkpoint: Where you bound.

Me: Over to the hospital yonder, to see about my mother. She's took bad sick.

That one I answered plain, no trouble to it, on account of it was the plain truth and nothing more needed saying.

The man at the checkpoint: How's she kin to you, and you got anything else with her name on it besides that letter.

That one I took more care with, because I knowed if I left something out or said it crossways he'd have cause to hold me there longer same as that fella before me, so I made sure I was leveling with him, plain and slow, her full name and mine and how the two of us run together, her borned and raised back in the holler same as the rest of us, and I watched his face the whole time to see was it landing right.

Then he didn't say nothing a while, just stood there a-looking that letter over, turning it and turning it like he expected something different on the back of it, and the quiet of that stretched out longer to me than the whole half hour in line had.

The man at the checkpoint: You got the paper from the doctor his own self, the one saying she's been admitted.

For a second my heart about quit on me, because I couldn't call to mind right off where I'd put it. Then it come to me, it was folded up small inside my Bible where I'd tucked it so as not to lose it along the way, and I dug it out right there in front of him, my hands none too steady, and passed it acrost.

I'll own it took some doing to keep myself level through all of that. My voice wanted to shake when I answered him and I had to bear down and keep it even, and I stood there with my hands still at my sides when what I wanted was to be a-wringing them.

When he'd got done looking everything over, he handed the papers back to me careful, like a man who knowed what they meant to somebody, and he said, low, that he hoped she come through it alright. Wasn't nothing he had to say, but he said it anyhow.

The man at the checkpoint: Go on.

And that was hit. No more to it than that word and a nod of his head on up the road.

I went on same as before, one foot after the other, and it wasn't till I'd gone a good twenty minutes or better, round past where the ridge cuts back and the shack was out of sight behind me, that whatever had been holding me up tight that whole while let go of me all of a sudden, and my legs went to shaking so bad I had to set myself down on a rock a spell before I could go on any further. Hit took something out of me I didn't feel the cost of till I was already safe past it and didn't need to be strong no more.

And that's why I tell it careful when I tell it at all. Wasn't nothing happened to me there worse than a wait and some hard questions and a bad half hour of nerves, and I won't make it into more than that. But it wasn't nothing either, and I reckon it deserves telling straight through, the whole of it, same size it was and not one inch different.
