By the middle of the night hit was down to just the four of us, everybody else done gone on home to their own beds, and the coffee gone cold in the pot with nobody caring enough to get up and make more. The eldest had her shoes off and her feet up under her in the corner of the couch. The neighbor woman was still in her funeral dress with her cardigan pulled tight around her like the house was colder than hit really was. The widow set down in Daddy's chair without even seeming to notice she'd done it, and nobody was fixing to say nothing about that.

The widow: He'd be up afore daylight ever single morning of his life, out there in the cold a-feeding them chickens like he had forty of 'em stead of just the four.

The eldest: It was three, Mama. Old Red died back in the spring, don't you remember, the fox got her.

The widow: Well, I always get that part wrong. I don't know why I cain't never keep it straight in my own head no more.

Me: I don't know why you two are sitting here squabbling over dead chickens, of all the things.

I hadn't meant to laugh when I said it, it just come out of me sideways, but nobody minded. The neighbor even smiled a little down into her cup.

The neighbor: He used to say the same thing ever time hit rained on past a day. Same words, word for word, never varied it none.

The eldest, saying it out just like him: "Lord willing and the creek don't rise, I might could get to it directly."

Me: That's him. That's him right there, I can hear it plain as if he was setting in that chair still.

Quiet a minute after that, everybody hearing him say it in their own head, in his own voice, and nobody wanting to be the one to talk over it.

The neighbor: I don't know as I ever told you'uns this, and I don't rightly know if I ought to be the one telling it now, but he give his good coat away one January, right off his own back, to some man he didn't even know from over at the co-op. Come on home in his shirt sleeves and told your mama he must have left it somewheres.

The widow: He never.

The neighbor: I swear it. The man that seed him do it told me hisself, years on after, at the funeral home of all places.

Quiet again after that, the kind that sets down heavy in a room, till the eldest got up sudden and went and got the plate of ham biscuits off the kitchen counter and set it back down amongst us, like there was something needed doing with her hands right then.

The eldest: Y'all eat something. There's a whole plate here fixing to go to waste.

Me: I ain't hardly hungry, but I'll take one just to be polite about it.

The widow, turning on the neighbor with an old needle coming back up out of nowhere: You never did give me back that quilt frame, you know. Been going on three year now.

The neighbor: I told you I'd bring it directly.

The widow: Directly since two years back is what you told me.

Neither one of them pushed it no further, not tonight of all nights, and it passed on by same as a cloud does over a field, there and then gone.

Me: Y'all recall the bench out on the porch? Sigogglin as the day is long, one leg shorter than the other three put together, and he built the thing hisself and would not let a living soul lay a hand to it.

The eldest: I offered to fix it about ten times if I offered once.

Me: He'd say hit had stood forty year and hit'd stand forty more, and if you didn't care for the look of it crooked you was welcome to go set on the ground.

The widow, laughing despite herself, wiping under her eye with the back of her hand: That man. Once he'd made his mind up on a thing, the Lord Himself might could not have moved him off it.

The neighbor: Stubborn as a mule that's already decided the barn's on fire and there ain't no talking him down off it.

A little laugh went round at that, easy and true, no edge left in it at all anymore, the kind of stubborn you can love a man for now that he's gone, that you'd have fussed at him something right smart over while he was still living and setting right there to hear it.

Me: I reckon that's just how he was made, though. Once he set his mind on a thing, hit was set, and that was the whole of it.

Nobody said outright that was the one thing they'd carry him by, but the talk kept circling back around to it anyhow, same as water finds the same low place in a field ever time it rains, and everybody seemed content to leave it setting there without nobody having to say it plain.

Partway on through all that the widow's shoulders had took up shaking, quiet, no sound to it hardly at all, just the tears running down while she held her coffee cup steady in her lap same as before. Nobody stopped the talk on her account. The eldest reached over without even looking her way and laid her hand down over her mama's hand, and kept right on talking to the neighbor like nothing at all was happening, which was about the kindest thing anybody could have done for her right then, letting her cry without making a show of it in front of her.

The eldest: Somebody's got to call about the stone this week. And Mama, you cain't be out there a-feeding them chickens by yourself of a morning, not till you get steadier on that hip.

Me: I'll come by of a morning and do it. Ain't no trouble to me at all.

The neighbor: I can bring supper by of a Tuesday and a Thursday, save you the cooking for a spell.

The widow, quieter now: Y'all don't have to go and do all that for me.

The eldest: We're a-doing it anyhow, so there ain't no use in you arguing it.

That settled easy enough, the way practical things will when there's already been a right smart of crying done and everybody's tired plumb down to the bone. The neighbor was the last one still up with any words left in her, the rest of us gone quiet and heavy-eyed, and she raised her coffee cup up a little, not much, just enough to matter.

The neighbor: Well. Here's to him, then. Cranky old fool that he was, and I mean that with my whole heart, God rest him.

Me: To him.

The widow: To him.

And that was about the size of it, the cups not even touching, just held up together a second in the low lamplight, and then set back down soft on the table, and the house went quiet around the four of us the way a house will when it's finally ready to let a body rest.
