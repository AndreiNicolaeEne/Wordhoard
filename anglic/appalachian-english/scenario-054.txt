Well, I'll tell you how the whole thing come about, since you wasn't here for none of it and you keep asking.

We'd done outgrowed that old place plumb through. Started out just me and the wife and it was fine, more than fine, but then here come the young'uns one after another and my wife's mother needing a room of her own after her hip give out, and directly we had four people sleeping where two ought to been, and the boy still bunking on a fold-out in the kitchen like we was living in a poke sack stood on end. Hit got to where you couldn't hardly turn around of a morning without stepping on somebody. So I says to the wife, I says, we got to build on, or build new, one or the other, and she says build new and do it right this time, so that's what we set to doing.

I set down at the fireboard one evening with a pencil and a scrap of paper and I reckoned on it. I figured it'd take me and maybe one other body the better part of six months if we was steady at it, working evenings and Saturdays both. I told the wife six months, maybe seven if the weather turned mean on us. Lord, was I off. Took eleven months start to finish, and it wasn't just me and one other, it come to six different folks lending a hand at one time or another before it was done, my brother-in-law, my cousin down the holler, a neighbor man, the preacher's boy, my own son once he got old enough to be trusted with a hammer, and a fellow from over the ridge that used to could frame a house faster than any man I ever seen, when his back allowed it.

Now the very first thing we done, we like to messed up bad. We got the foundation lines run and started in on the footers before we'd even got the ground good and level, just eyeballing it, thinking we'd square it up later. Well hit didn't square up later. My brother-in-law come out about a week and a half in and stood there a minute looking at it and shook his head.

Brother-in-law: That whole side's sigogglin. You see it? Runs downhill a good three inch from that corner to this'n.

Me: Ain't that bad.

Brother-in-law: Hit's that bad. You pour a slab on that you'll be living in a house that leans like an old barn the rest of your life.

He was right and I knowed it soon as he said it, I just didn't want to hear it. We tore up near two weeks of work, dug it back out, brought in the transit level my cousin had, and done it over right. We was leveling that ground three whole days before either one of us was satisfied, checking it over and over, and I recall thinking, if this is how the first two weeks goes, we are in for a long haul. I didn't know the half of it yet.

Then one Saturday morning, and I hadn't asked a soul, hadn't even mentioned it after church, I looked up from the framing and there's five extra trucks pulled up along the fence and near a dozen men and boys standing there with their own tools, my cousin's whole side of the family and two neighbors besides. My cousin just grins at me.

Cousin: Heared you was framing this weekend. Figured we'd come on.

Me: You didn't have to do that.

Cousin: Didn't have to, wanted to. That's what folks does.

We got more done that one day than I'd got in the two weeks before it. That's the holler for you, though, word gets around and hands just show up.

Along about the middle of it, deep into summer, we got a stretch of weather that stopped us dead near a week. Rained of a morning, cleared off just enough to fool you into thinking you could get something done, then rained again by dinnertime, six days running like that. Roads down in the holler washed out some too, so half the help couldn't even have got up to the place if they'd wanted to. I set on the porch of the old house and watched water run off where the new roof was fixing to go and there wasn't a blessed thing to do about it but wait.

Round about the time we was closing in the walls we run short on lumber. Had figured wrong on the board count, or the fellow at the yard had, one, and there wasn't going to be no more delivered for near two weeks on account of a backorder. So we made do. Used what scrap and odd-length boards we had laying around from an old shed we'd tore down, mixed it right in where it wouldn't show or wouldn't matter, saved the good new lumber for where it counted. Wasn't pretty in every spot but it held, and holding's the main thing.

I had my fall along in there too, up on the second-floor joists before the subfloor was down good. Foot slipped on a wet board and I went through, caught myself on a joist with one arm and dropped the rest of the way to the ground floor, maybe eight foot. Knocked the wind clean out of me and I laid there a minute not able to say nothing while everybody come a-hollering and a-running.

Neighbor: You hurt? Say something, you hurt?

Me: I'm. I'm alright. Just knocked the breath out.

Neighbor: Lord God, I thought you'd broke your neck.

Didn't break nothing, thank the Lord, just bruised up something awful and scared the whole crew half to death, myself included. We put a temporary floor down after that before anybody set foot up there again.

We had us a real argument too, over how to frame the roof on the back porch. The fellow from over the ridge wanted it done one way, said a shed roof tied into the main roof was the only way that'd shed water right, and my brother-in-law wanted a full gable back there to match the front.

Ridge fellow: You do it his way, you're going to have ice damming up against that wall every winter. I've seen it.

Brother-in-law: And I've seen plenty a shed roof looks like an afterthought stuck on the back of a house.

They went back and forth on it two evenings running, near got heated, but in the end we went with the shed roof, and I'll say the ridge fellow was right about it. Ain't had a lick of trouble with ice.

The worst of it, though, was the back bedroom wall. We'd already got it framed, sheathed, half the wiring run through it, and my cousin caught it, said the studs was on the wrong spacing for the window we'd ordered, and worse, the whole wall was out of plumb near an inch top to bottom. Had to tear the whole thing down to the sill plate and start that wall over from scratch. That set us back the better part of three weeks, and I won't lie, I liked to give up right there. But we done it.

Day the frame finally stood up whole, every wall square, every rafter set, we all just stood back and looked at it a good while, didn't say much, just looked. Somebody handed round a jug of sweet tea and we drank to it right there in the yard. That was the first day in months felt like a win instead of a fight.

Last four nights before we finished, we worked on past dark every one of them, running a generator for light, hanging the last of the doors, setting the fireboard in the front room, getting the water hooked up right. Might could have stopped and finished up the next week, but we was all of us wore out and wanting it done, so we pushed on through.

And then it was done. Stood there finished, whole, ours. Eleven months, six people helping besides me and the wife, more setbacks than I care to count and more good will than I rightly deserved. I stand in that front room some evenings yet and just look around at it, at the fireboard I set with my own hands, the wall we built twice, the porch roof we near come to blows over, and hit don't feel like just a house to me. Feels like every one of them people is still standing in it with me.
