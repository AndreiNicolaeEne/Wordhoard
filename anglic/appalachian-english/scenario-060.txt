We had put off clearing that house two winters running, on account of nobody wanting to be the one to say hit was time. But the taxes was coming due again and the roof had done gone to caving in on the back porch, and my uncle said hit plain one Sunday after church, said the county wasn't going to wait on our grieving, and a family down the holler had already asked about the land for their boy to build on. So we set a day. Near on fifteen of us showed, kin from three counties, some I hadn't seed since the burying.

We started in the kitchen because that was where everbody's feet took them first, same as it always was when Grandmother was living and you'd come in the back door hollering for her before you even got your coat off. My aunt was the one opened the flour bin and found the old butter mold down in the bottom of it, the wood one with the acorn cut in the face, and she just stood there a minute holding hit.

Aunt: Lord, I ain't thought about this thing in thirty year. She used to let me press the pattern while she held my hand steady. I was little-bitty. I remember the smell of hit more'n I remember looking at hit.

Nobody rushed her. We let her have her minute and went on sorting canning jars into pokes to carry out.

It was in that same kitchen, up in the top of the pie safe, that we come across the thing nobody could place. A little tin box, rusted shut near about, and when my cousin finally got hit open with a knife there wasn't nothing in hit but a handful of buttons that didn't match nothing, a bent spoon, and a folded scrap of oilcloth with numbers wrote on hit in pencil, no names, no dates, just figures added up three different ways like somebody was ciphering on something and give up.

Cousin: Any of you'uns know what this is?

The eldest: Might could be from the store days, when she kept books for the feed store. Or might could not. I don't rightly know.

We passed hit around the room same as if passing hit around would make hit tell us something, and in the end my aunt wrapped hit back in the oilcloth and set hit in her poke to keep, on account of somebody ought to, even not knowing.

The front room was where the argument come, over the rocker by the fireboard. It had been Grandfather's chair, the cane seat gone through and mended twice, and both my uncle and my mother wanted hit, each one saying the other one had got the good dresser last time somebody divided things up, years back when the aunt on the other side passed. They went at hit a good while, not ugly, just circling.

Mother: I ain't asking for much off this house. I'm asking for the chair he set in of an evening. That's all.

My uncle: And I set in it plenty a-growing up, same as you. Hit ain't like you're the only one got memory of him in hit.

The eldest finally settled hit, being the oldest one there and the only one nobody argued with.

The eldest: Give hit to whichever one of you has got a porch hit'll set on where the grandbabies can see hit. That's what he'd want, not which one of you loved him harder.

My mother had the porch. My uncle didn't fuss no more after that, though he grumbled a little getting hit loaded, said hit was sigogglin anyhow and would fall over first good wind, which wasn't true and everbody knowed hit.

The disagreement that stuck, that took longer than the chair, was over the old organ against the far wall, the pump kind with half the ivory gone off the keys. Half of us wanted hit hauled off, said hit hadn't played right since before I was borned and was just taking up space somebody could be sweeping. The other half couldn't stand the thought of hit going to the dump.

My uncle: That thing ain't worked in forty year. We ain't got room for hit and can't nobody here play a lick anyway.

Aunt: Don't matter if hit's played. She used to have us singing around hit of a Sunday evening, off-key as we all was. You get rid of that and you might as well get rid of the whole notion of this being her house.

They went back and forth a long spell, plumb near an hour by the time I quit paying hit mind, and in the end nobody decided nothing outright, they just sort of quit talking about hit and hit stayed in the front room while we cleared everthing else around hit, which I reckon was its own kind of answer.

Upstairs is where somebody found what nobody was looking for. My cousin was up under the eave in the back bedroom, pulling loose a board that had come sigogglin off the wall, and behind hit was a little cloth poke, tied shut, with near forty dollars in old bills folded up inside and a ring wrapped in a handkerchief. Whole room went quiet.

Cousin: Ain't nobody going to believe this.

The eldest: She used to could tell you where every dime in this house was hid, back when she had her mind fully about her. I reckon this was one she forgot to tell.

That back bedroom was the one that took us the whole rest of the morning and on past dinner, longer than any other room in the house put together. Hit had been used for storing since before most of us was grown, every relation's leavings pushed up in there over the years, trunks stacked on trunks, a whole wall of galluses and old coats nobody could say whose they'd been, boxes of letters, a sewing machine, quilt frames leaned in the corner. We was still a-digging through hit when my mother hollered up the stairs that dinner was on.

We ate out under the big tree in the yard, everbody's dish spread on the tailgates and a card table somebody had drug out, fried chicken and beans and half a dozen kinds of cake, and for a good while nobody talked about the house at all, just talked about who all was there and who wasn't and whose baby had got big.

After dinner was when we tackled the wood stove in the kitchen, the big black cookstove she'd cooked on sixty year, and not one soul there had a place for hit nor a truck strong enough to be trusted with the weight of hit. We stood around hit a good while like we was fixing to have a funeral for hit too.

My uncle: Can't nobody use hit and can't nobody haul hit. I hate to say hit but hit's got to go to scrap.

Wasn't a person argued. Some things you just let go of because there ain't another way, and everbody standing there knowed hit.

The one thing we agreed on without a single cross word was the quilt off the back bedroom bed, the double-wedding-ring one she'd pieced by hand. Soon as somebody pulled hit out my aunt's daughter said she wanted hit for her own girl someday, and everbody just nodded, on account of that girl had been the one setting with Grandmother most in her last years, and nobody felt a lick of argument in them about hit.

By late afternoon the back bedroom stood empty at last, swept plumb clean, and somebody's voice echoed different in there than hit had all day, the way a room does when hit's finally got nothing left in hit.

We split up what remained out in the yard, the smaller things, dishes and tools and what furniture was left, everbody taking a turn saying what they'd like and nobody grabbing before their turn, and hit went easier than any of us expected, considering how the morning had gone.

Evening come on and we stood on the porch a spell before anybody could make theirself shut the door. My uncle finally turned the key in hit, though can't nobody remember the last time that door had been locked in her whole life of living in hit, and we all just stood there looking at the house a minute like hit was fixing to say something back, before we loaded up and went on down the holler, taillights strung out behind one another in the dark.
