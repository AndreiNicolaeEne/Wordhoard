I don't rightly know how to start this except to say I'm sorry it's took me this long. It's been the better part of a year since you pulled out of the yard with that truck loaded down plumb to the windows, and I've started this letter in my head a hundred times without ever once putting pen to it. Mama keeps asking did I write you yet and I keep saying I'm fixing to, which you know as well as anybody is a lie I tell myself near every week.

So. Here it is, finally, the whole of it, however long it runs.

The house is standing, is the first thing. That roof over the back room that's been dripping since before you left finally got seen to. Daddy had a man up from down the road, and they were up there two whole days a-hammering and a-cussing the weather, and it don't leak a drop now, not even in that hard rain that took the low bridge out for near a week. Daddy finally got that crack in the fireboard patched too, the one that's bothered Mama since before you were born, and you'd think from how pleased she is you'd never know it took him this long. Course no sooner was the roof fixed than the water pump commenced acting up, so now we're hauling water from the spring again like it's fifty years back. Daddy says he might could get to it before first frost, might could not, you know how he is about it, and he don't move around like he used to could, though he'd skin me for saying it. Mama put up forty-some quart of green beans off the garden this year and a good bit of tomatoes besides, and she set out a new bed of them big orange dahlias along the porch rail where the hydrangea died out. It's real pretty of an evening.

The big news, if you ain't heared it already through the grapevine, is the oldest got let go from the plant this past winter. They cut a whole shift loose without hardly no warning, and you should've seed Daddy's face when they come home and told us. Scared us all plumb to death for about a month, I won't lie to you. But it turned out for the better. Took on at the hospital over in the county seat, in the records office, and they like the work fine, say it's steadier than the plant ever was and the people's decent to work with. Had to move in closer to town for it, on account of the drive being too much on bad-road mornings, so the house up past us sits empty now, which is its own strange thing, driving by of an evening and not seeing a light on.

Mama: Well, hit's a good job. Steady.
Daddy: Steadier than that plant ever was, that's for sure.
Me: I still say hit was scary as anything for about a month there.
Mama: Everything's scary for about a month. Then it ain't.

That's about the size of it.

Don't think I forgot about the biscuit, either. I still laugh about it when I'm not even thinking of you.

The neighbors has shifted some since you been gone. The widow up the branch finally passed, back in the cold of the winter, she'd been ailing a long while and it wasn't no surprise, but the place is different without her setting on that porch watching the road like she done for I don't know how many years. A young couple bought it off her people, come up from somewhere down toward Knoxville, and they've took to fixing it up right nice, though the old barn out back is about as sigogglin as it ever was and I don't believe they've noticed yet what a job that's going to be. And the couple two houses down, you'uns used to wave at them of a morning, they had them a girl borned to them right when the dogwoods was blooming, and Mama's about wore the road out down there taking food and holding that baby every chance she gets.

Daddy's took to wearing his galluses again these days, says his stomach ain't what it used to be for a belt, and it makes him look about ninety though I'd never tell him that to his face.

Your aunt asks after you every single time I see her, first thing out of her mouth near about, before she even says hello proper. And the one that cuts hair down at the shop, she asks too, every time I go in, wants to know are you eating right, are you warm enough, same as if you was still twelve years old.

I'll tell you a thing here I ain't never said to your face and don't believe I ever will, so this letter's as good a place as any for it. I cried some, that morning you left. Not where anybody seen. I went round back by the woodpile and let it out there and then come back in like nothing. I ain't sorry you went, I want you to know that plain, you needed it and it was right for you. But hit hurt something fierce for a while there, hit truly did, and I never told a soul, and I'm only telling you now on account of you're too far off to make a fuss about it.

Winter treated us rough, I won't lie. We had an ice storm right in the deep of it that took the power out the better part of a week, and we was heating with the stove and eating whatever wouldn't spoil, and it felt right smart like the old days Grandma used to tell about. But spring come in kind and the garden's done good behind it, better than we had any right to expect after a winter like that, so I reckon it evens out the way it mostly does.

Now I got questions for you and I want real answers, not the two lines you generally send. Is it near as cold where you're at as they say, or was that just Daddy talking? Have you found you a church yet, or are you still sleeping in of a Sunday like I expect? And is that job keeping you as busy as you let on, or do you have you some time of an evening to rest, because I worry on that more than I let show.

I'll close this out before it gets to be a book instead of a letter. I'm sending you a jar of the chow-chow this time, I mean it, I ain't just saying it like I said it before and then didn't. It's setting in a poke by the door already so's I don't forget it again.

Write me back when you can. We think on you plenty up here, and the holler ain't the same without you hollering back down it.
