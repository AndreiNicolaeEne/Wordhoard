The mother set down on the porch step and drawed both of them in close, one on each side of her, before she said one word at all.

The mother: I got to tell you'uns something hard now, and I want it to come from me. Your papaw passed on this morning. He's died, hon. He won't be a-hurting no more.

The eldest: But what does that mean, though. Is he just gone? Where'd he go at?

The mother: His body quit on him. It just plumb wore out, same as an old truck engine finally gives up after it's run a right smart of years on it. He ain't a-breathing no more, ain't a-feeling nothing no more, no pain, no nothing, that part of him is finished and hit ain't never coming back. But what made him him, the way he loved on you'uns, the way he'd set right there and tell them same old stories over and over, that don't quit being true just because his body did.

The youngest: Is he cold?

The mother: He might be, sugar, but he ain't cold like you'd be cold. He ain't feeling it. He ain't feeling nothing a-tall no more, so don't you go fretting on that part.

She pulled them both in tight to her then, hugged on them good.

The mother: Come here to me, punkin.

The youngest: Will we get to see him again ever?

The mother: Not down here we won't, not the way we're used to seeing him, and I ain't gone lie to you about that. But I believe we will, someday, on the other side of things. That's what I hold to, anyhow.

Before she let them loose back to their playing she told them plain, kin was gone start coming up the holler tomorrow, the house was fixing to fill plumb up with people, and there'd be a viewing and then the burying a couple days on, so when it come they wouldn't be took by surprise on top of everything else.
