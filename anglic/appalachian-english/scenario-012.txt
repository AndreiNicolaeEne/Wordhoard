You remember them three or four I run with all that one summer, down at the holler past the ridge, the ones whose mamas never did know where they was half the daylight hours. We was down at the swimming hole ever day hit didn't rain, and some days hit did rain and we went anyway. There was a big old sycamore leaned out over the deep part, and a foot log further down that had gone sigogglin years back, tilted down into the water on one side so bad wasn't nobody using hit to cross no more, just to set on and dangle your feet.

The dare was climbing that sycamore to the highest limb a body could reach and jumping from there into the water. Wasn't the first time it'd been dared. There was a spring before where they'd dared me into something a right smart less than that, walking that sigogglin foot log all the way across without holding to nothing, and I'd backed out of hit halfway, climbed back off, and they never did let me forget hit. Called me chicken the whole rest of that year. Every time I wouldn't do a thing they'd bring hit back up, "you're the one backed off the log," like hit was wrote on me somewhere.

So that day at the tree, when they got to hollering up you'uns comin or not, and one of em said go on then, prove you ain't still chicken about the log, I clumb higher than I meant to. I want you to know that, Mama, I meant to stop two branches down from where I stopped. My arms was a-shaking already and I knowed it was too high, I could feel in my stomach it was too high, but they was down there a-hollering and a-carrying on, and I was scared worse of climbing back down in front of em than I was of the water.

I jumped and I misjudged hit. I don't know how else to say hit. They drug me out. All of em. I want you to know that too, they didn't run off and leave me, whatever else they done, they got a-hold of me and drug me up onto the bank and they was all of em crying and a-hollering for somebody to do something, and one of em had the sense to pull his shirt off and wrap it round my leg and press down on hit hard as he could. I laid there plumb white, they told me after, shaking, and the blood soaked through that shirt in no time.

We'd say I fell off my bike. Simple as that. Say I was riding too fast down the gravel and went over the handlebars and cut it on something in the ditch. Wasn't none of us going to say the word sycamore or the word jump to a soul.

And Mama, you believed hit. You took me and got it looked at and it got stitched up under that story, and I remember you fussing at me after about riding too close to the edge of the road, and I let you fuss. Ever time somebody's asked about it since, at the pool, at a doctor's office, that one time your own sister asked over Sunday dinner and I seed her looking right at it, I've told the very same lie, word for word near enough, like I'd worried it smooth as a creek rock. Fell off my bike. Cut it in the ditch. I could tell it in my sleep. I used to could tell it without hardly thinking on it at all, and here lately I can't tell it without thinking on the tree.

Me: Because I've got one of my own coming up on that age here directly, and I seen the look on his face here a few weeks back when some of his friends was daring him into something off the bridge, and I heard myself telling him don't you do it, that's how people get hurt, and I like to choked on my own words, knowing full well what I done at his age and never told a soul the truth of it. I can't raise him honest out of a story that ain't. And I got to thinking on you too, Mama, how you've kept that same worry all these years, thinking you had a boy who was reckless on a bicycle, when what you really had was a boy who couldn't stand to be called chicken one more time and went and near got hisself drowned over hit. I'm sorry them other young'uns and me made a pact right there bleeding on that bank and kept to it longer than any of us kept to near about anything else in our lives. I'm sorry every time you've told that bike story to somebody proud like it was just some ordinary childhood mishap, you was telling something I put in your mouth. Hit don't change the scar none. Hit's still there, same as always, same shape hit's always been. But I'd rather you know the real reason for it than have you keep on believing the false one just cause hit's the one you've always had.

Me: I ain't done yet crying about it, so you'll just have to put up with me a minute.

Mama: I got time. I always did have time for you, whichever story you was telling me.
