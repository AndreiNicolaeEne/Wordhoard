I come up the drive slow, on account of my knee still ain't right from the drive, and I seed the porch light on even though it weren't dark yet, like they'd left it burning all week just in case. The dog off the neighbor's place set up a-barking down the holler and I like to think that's what brung everybody to the door before I even knocked.

The door come open and for a second the mother just stood there a-looking at me like I was somebody selling something. Then it broke.

The mother: Lord God. Lord God have mercy, is that you?

Me: It's me.

The mother: LORD GOD IT'S HIM! Y'ALL GET IN HERE, HE'S HOME!

And then I don't rightly know how many arms was on me at once. The mother had ahold of my neck and wouldn't turn loose, just kept a-squeezing tighter ever time I went to pull back, like she was scared if she let go I'd up and disappear again for eight more year. I could feel her a-shaking.

The mother: Let me look at you. Let me just look at you a minute.

She held me back by the shoulders and her eyes was already wet and running over.

The mother: You have done got so thin. And look at that beard, Lord, you look just like your granddaddy done looked, don't he look just like Daddy did?

The aunt: He does, he's the spitting image, ain't he, he's done growed up on us.

The daddy come up behind her then, not saying much, just put his hand on the back of my neck the way he always done, and squeezed onct, hard, and that was about all he could get out for a minute.

The daddy: Well. Bout time.

Me: Yes sir.

The daddy: Bout time, boy.

That's when everbody commenced to talking at oncet, over top of one another, so it weren't hardly a conversation a-tall, more like a whole flock of guineas set loose in the house.

The eldest: Where's your bags at, you got more'n that?
The aunt: Did you eat? Lord he ain't ate a thing, look at him.
The mother: You still like your coffee the same way or did they ruin you off yonder?
The eldest: Is it true you was living clean up in the mountains out there too, or was that just what you told us?
The aunt: How long can you stay this time? Tell me you ain't fixing to run off again in a week.
The mother: We got to have us a dinner. I'm calling your grandmother right now, she's going to want everbody up here.
The eldest: Did you hear Cousin got married? Back in the spring. And her baby's near about walking now.
The aunt: And the Petersons' place sold, you remember that sigogglin old barn a-leaning off the side of the ridge, they tore the whole thing down.

I just stood there a-turning my head from one to the other of them, trying to catch aholt of any one thread of it, and give up and just laughed, and that set the mother off crying again, only now she was laughing too, which is about the only way I ever known her to cry, laughing right through it.

The mother: I ain't crying. I ain't. Don't you look at me.

Me: You're crying.

The mother: Well hush up about it then.

My bags was still a-setting right there by the door where I'd dropped em, and not one soul had so much as looked at em since. Didn't matter to nobody yet whether I had a clean shirt to my name, only that I was standing in the front room.

Back against the wall, half behind the daddy's chair, stood one of the young'uns, I reckon maybe seven or eight now, that I ain't hardly laid eyes on since she weren't nothing but a baby. She had her thumb up near her mouth like she used to do when she was little, just a-watching all this commotion with her eyes big as saucers, not coming no closer.

Me: Well now who's this. You ain't fixing to come say hey to me?

She just ducked back a little more behind the chair.

The aunt: That's your cousin, go on, she don't hardly know you, honey, it's alright.

The mother: She's plumb shy of strangers here lately, don't take it personal, she'll come around directly.

Me: Naw, that's alright. Take your time, girl. I ain't going nowheres.

I breathed in then and it hit me before I even thought to notice it a-coming, that smell of the house, wood smoke down in the walls of it and something a-frying back in the kitchen and under all that the same old must off the fireboard where Mamaw always kept them dried apples setting in a bowl. Eight year and it hadn't changed one bit, and I don't know why but that like to done me in worse than any of the hollering had.

Me: Y'all still keep them dried apples on the fireboard?

The mother: Where else would I keep em?

Me: Smells just the same in here. Whole house does.

The daddy: Ain't changed much. You have, though.

Me: Reckon I have.

The eldest: Look how his voice done changed even, don't he talk different?

The aunt: He does not, don't be starting that.

Me: I talk the same as I always did.

The eldest: You do NOT, you talk like one of them TV men now.

Me: Hush your mouth, I do not.

That got a laugh out of everbody, even the daddy, and it eased something loose in the room, like a knot coming untied.

I told em some, how the ridge behind the house looked just exactly the way it always had, same notch cut in the top of it against the sky, and how the road in was rougher than I recollected, washed out bad in that one low spot past the church same as it ever was, some things they never do get around to fixing. But the barn out back was gone crooked worse than it used to be, a-leaning further than I remembered it leaning, and the old truck weren't setting where it always set.

The mother: We might could get that barn straightened up if your daddy'd quit putting it off.

The daddy: Hit ain't hurting nothing where it's at.

The mother: It's fixing to fall plumb over is what it's fixing to do.

The daddy: Used to could prop it with that beam yonder. Reckon it'd still hold.

The aunt: Y'all hush about the barn, we got more important things. I'm fixing supper tomorrow, whole family, I don't care who's got to work, they can come after.

The mother: I'm already thinking what all to make. He always did love my beans and cornbread more'n anything I ever fixed.

Me: I have thought about your cornbread more'n I care to admit.

The mother: Well you're fixing to get all you can hold of it then.

The aunt: You remember what Mamaw used to call you? Little Bear. On account you was always a-getting into the honey when you was little, remember that?

The mother: Lord, Little Bear, I ain't thought of that in years.

The daddy, the only time I seen him near about smile the whole evening: Little Bear's home.

Didn't nobody explain it to the young'un standing back against the chair, and didn't need to, it weren't for her, it was just ourn, the kind of thing that don't mean nothing to nobody who weren't there for it the first time and means near about everything to the ones who was.

And then, all of a sudden, like somebody had turned a switch somewheres, the whole room just went quiet. Ever one of em just a-looking at me, not saying nothing, like they was making sure of something, like they had to see it a minute with their own eyes before they'd let theirselves believe it all the way down. I looked back at all of em looking at me and didn't nobody say a word for what felt like a right smart while, though I don't reckon it was but a few seconds by the clock.

Then the mother let out a breath she must've been a-holding the whole time, and reached over and took the bags up off the floor herself.

The mother: Well. Come on in the kitchen, then. Let's get you fed.

And just like that the hollering and the crying and the questions all settled down into something else, something slower, everbody finding a chair, the coffee going on, the young'un finally easing out from behind that chair to where she could get a good long look at me without me looking back too hard. First hour was over. Felt like I'd never left, and felt like eight year all at oncet, both of them things true together and neither one troubling the other any.
