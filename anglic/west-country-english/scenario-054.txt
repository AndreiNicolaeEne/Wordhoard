Right, so, you want the whole of it, do you. Sit down then, this idn't a short one.

We'd got to where we just never had the room no more. Four of us in a house built for two and a dog, and the youngest sleeping in what was basically a cupboard with a window in it. Every winter someone'd be sleeping on the sofa and by the spring us'd all be at each other's throats over the bathroom. So I said to the mother, I said, we do need another room out the back or we're going to end up killing each other, and that was that, decided over a cup of tea on a Tuesday like it was nothing, though it turned out to be a gert lot more than nothing.

Reckoned at the start it'd be me, my brother, and a mate of his who does a bit of building, and maybe three month if we were quick about it and the weather behaved. Three month. I do laugh now thinking on that. Took eleven, in the end, and there were six of us all told who put a hand to it one time or another, counting the fella who only came the once to sort the electrics and never came back, bless him, he'd done his bit.

First proper cock-up came inside the first fortnight, mind, before we'd hardly started. We'd set the footings out wrong, my brother and me, gone off the wrong line off the old wall, and didn't neither of us catch it till the block was three courses up and looking gert wonky against the fence. My mate stood back, had a look, and just went,

My mate: That idn't square, babber. Look at it.

Me: It's near enough.

My mate: It idn't near enough, it's off by near a foot down the far end. You'll be building yourself a house shaped like a wedge of cheese.

So down it come, the lot of it, and back to digging, and there went near two weeks we'll never get back, doing over what we'd already done once. Teasy as anything I was that week, proper wisht about the whole job, thinking we'd never get anywhere at this rate.

Then there's the day the extra hands turned up and nobody'd asked them. I come out one Saturday morning and there's my brother-in-law and his two lads stood in the garden with a mixer they'd borrowed off someone, just got in the van and come down because word had got round we were struggling. Didn't say nothing about it beforehand, just turned up.

Brother-in-law: Heard you was short-handed. We'm here now, where d'you want us.

That was a proper good day, that was. Got more done in that one Saturday than the whole week before it.

Weather had its say and all. Come the back end of it, must've been getting on for a week, near enough five days solid, rained that hard we couldn't do a thing. Everything under tarps, mud everywhere, the mother going mad about the state of the garden and the dog tracking it through the kitchen. Dimpsy of an evening you'd stand at the window just watching it come down sideways, nothing to do but wait it out and drink tea and get teasy with each other over nothing much.

Ran short of block partway through and all, right in the middle of the wall going up, and the yard couldn't get us more for the best part of a week either, some delivery gone wrong somewhere. So we made do, used what timber and bits we had knocking about from an old shed we'd took down backalong, propped and braced where we ought to have blocked proper, and it held, but it wasn't pretty for a spell.

Had a scare and all, mind, up the scaffold. My mate went over backwards off the top board, no more than seven, eight foot, but it's enough, and he come down on the pile of sand we'd got in for the mortar, which is about the only reason he walked away from it laughing instead of in an ambulance. Knocked the wind clean out of him and give us all a proper fright.

Me: You alright?

My mate: I'm mazed. Give us a minute.

He sat there a good ten minute just getting his breath, and after that we put a proper rail up and nobody argued about wearing the harness again.

Had words too, over the roof of it, how the pitch ought to go. My brother wanted it steep like the old part of the house, said it'd shed rain better and look right against the rest of it. My mate reckoned shallow, said it'd be cheaper on timber and quicker to do and who's going to see it from the road anyway.

My brother: You do it your way it'll look like a lean-to stuck on the back.

My mate: You do it your way we'll be up there another fortnight for nothing.

Went back and forth over that one a good few evenings, proper barny about it more than once, but we split the difference in the end and neither of them's happy about it to this day, which I reckon means we got it about right.

Worst of it though was the side wall. Got it half up, and the fella who'd come to check the electrics happened to mention, just in passing like, that the whole thing was sat about two inch out from where the plans had it, off the line of the drain below. Had it checked proper and he was right. Whole wall, every block of it, had to come down and go back up square. That was a low point, that was, standing there of an evening looking at a heap of blocks we'd already laid twice thinking about laying them a third time.

But then there's the day the frame finally stood, the roof timbers up and squared and true, and you could stand inside it for the first time and see the shape of the room you'd been imagining all them months. Small thing really, just timber and sky between it, but proper good feeling, that. First time in months anyone'd smiled about the whole job.

The eldest: It's actually going to be a room, then.

Me: Reckon it might.

Last stretch of it, the final push, we were out there four nights running working on past dark, lamps rigged off an extension lead, getting the plastering done and the last of the wiring in, everyone proper knackered and short with each other but nobody wanting to stop with it that close to done. Freezing some of them nights, breath showing, and still at it gone ten.

And then there's the day it stood finished, doors hung, floor down, and you walk round it and there idn't a thing left to do. Stood there a good while, just looking at it, not saying much, because after that long you don't quite believe it's over.

Worked it out after, roughly. Eleven month start to finish, six of us had a proper hand in it one time or another, not counting the fella who only came the once. Near enough three times what we'd reckoned on at the start, and I do laugh about that three month guess now.

Someone got the zider in that evening and we sat out the back of it, in the room that idn't a cupboard anymore, and it's a funny feeling now, mind, coming through that door of an ordinary Tuesday with the shopping, not thinking nothing of it, when I do remember the state of that patch of garden with the mud and the tarps and my mate flat on his back in the sand. Stands proper solid now. Proper job, if I do say so myself. See, that's the whole of it.
