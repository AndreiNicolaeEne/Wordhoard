Right, so you know Nan went into the home back in the spring, well the house had to go too in the end, sell it to pay for the care, weren't no way round it, the money just idn't there otherwise. So we all agreed we'd do the one day, get it cleared proper before the people come round to do the valuing, and it ended up being not just the mother and my auntie and me, but my uncle come down from Fishponds, and the cousins, and even my auntie's workmate turned up with a van, so it was a fair old crowd stood on the path that morning, gert cold it was too, mind, for the time of year.

We started in the kitchen, seeing as that's where you always start in a house like that, idn't it, everyone stood round not wanting to be the first to open a cupboard. My auntie was the one what found the daps, right at the back of the cupboard under the stairs, this manky old pair of blue and white ones gone grey with dust, and she went proper quiet a minute and then said,

Auntie: "These was mine. Primary school, these was. I remember scrumping apples off next door's tree in these, backalong, must be forty year gone."

And that set her right off, laughing about it and near enough crying at the same time, the way you do.

Then in the same drawer where the tea towels was, there was this little brass key, not much bigger than your thumbnail, and not one of us could work out where it was to. Didn't fit the shed, didn't fit the sideboard, didn't fit nothing we could find in the whole house. My cousin held it up going,

Cousin: "Where's this to then? Anyone?"

Nobody had a clue, so it went in a jar with the other odds and it's still a mystery now if I'm honest with you.

After that we moved through to the dining room, and that's where the row started, over the dresser, the big oak one Nan always kept the good plates in. My uncle reckoned it should go to whoever had the room for it, on account of it being that gert big, and my auntie was saying she'd always fancied it herself, and it was starting to get proper teasy between the two of them till the mother piped up.

The mother: "Nan told her she was having that dresser, years back, when she first got her own place. Said it to me herself. So that's settled, idn't it."

And that was that, no more said, my auntie got the dresser and my uncle didn't argue no further, though he was a bit wisht about it for a minute.

Next room along was the front room, and that's where the old armchair was, Nan's chair, the one she always sat in of an evening, all worn through on the arms and smelling proper of her tobacco still. The mother wanted it kept, said she'd have it re-covered, but my uncle was having none of it.

Uncle: "Her's had it, look at the state of her, gone in the springs and all. You'll be putting good money into a chair what's fit for the tip."

The mother: "I don't care, I'm having it. You don't have to sit in it."

Nobody won that one proper, if I'm honest, it just got left in the hall for the mother to sort out herself later, still going backwards and forwards about it under her breath.

Same room had the old wireless in the corner and all, and whether that was even worth keeping got argued over an' all, seeing as none of us had switched it on in years. My uncle wouldn't hear of it going though.

Uncle: "Her's a good bit of kit, that is, they don't make her like that no more. I'm having that whether anyone wants it or not."

Nobody minded enough to fight him on it, so he had it, wrapped up in a blanket in the back of his car before dinner even. He do have it going most evenings now, so he tells us.

It was up in the loft where we found what nobody expected, mind. My cousin's other half was up there passing boxes down, and behind the water tank, wedged in the insulation, was an old biscuit tin, and inside it, rolled up tight with an elastic band gone to powder, near three hundred pound in old notes. Nobody had the first idea it was there, not even the mother, and we all stood looking at each other in the kitchen doorway proper mazed.

The eldest: "She never said nothing to me about that. Not a word."

The mother: "Wisht, idn't it, thinking of her tucking that away and never spending it, see."

We put it towards the skip hire in the end, seemed only right.

The room that took the longest, though, by a country mile, was the little spare bedroom, the box room, on account of Nan never having thrown a thing away her whole life. Every birthday card anyone had ever sent her, every church newsletter, every Christmas decoration since about nineteen seventy, all of it boxed up and stacked to the ceiling. There was a whole tin of postcards an' all, off Weston, from back when her and Grandad used to take us of a Sunday, before it went thick with grockles come the summer. She always said she'd sort that room out dreckly, and dreckly never come, did it. We was in there the best part of three hour while the rest of the house got done twice over.

Round about one o'clock the mother called a halt to it all and we sat out the back on the wall, on account of it being the only dry patch left what wasn't covered in bin bags, and had pasties and a flask of tea between us, and my uncle produced a flagon of zider he'd found out the back of the shed that morning, proper cloudy old scrumpy stuff, and we all had a drop even though it was barely gone midday.

Uncle: "Her brother-in-law made this, backalong. Must be older than the cousin's kid."

The eldest: "Don't matter how old it is, it'll still put you on your ear."

We had a laugh over that.

After dinner we did the shed and the garden, my uncle stood back at the end of it looking at the empty shed going, "Now that's a proper job, that is." Right babber, he said, give us a hand shifting this lot round the front, and that's when it come to the piano, the great big upright one in the front room nobody had thought about till it come to actually moving it. Not one of us had the room for it, not one of us even played it proper, and getting a van big enough and blokes strong enough to shift it would have cost more than the thing was worth. In the end we rung round and a fella come and took it off for scrap the same week, and it was a shame in a way, watching it go out the door sideways, but tis how it had to be, there weren't no other way of it.

The tea set though, that went without a single word said against it. Everyone just looked at the youngest, seeing as she'd only just got her own place, and the mother said,

The mother: "Well you'm having that then, aren't you."

And the youngest just said "cheers my luvver" and that was the whole of it, no fuss at all.

By the time we got round to Nan's own room, the last one left, it didn't take but twenty minutes, seeing as most of it had gone to the home with her already. Stripped bed, bare walls, curtains down, and it stood there proper empty and echoing, the four of us just stood in the doorway a minute not saying nothing much.

What was left after that, bits of crockery and linen and odds and ends nobody had claimed, we just spread out on the kitchen table and split it up between us, everyone taking a bit here and there, "is the fruit bowl going to anyone, where's the rest of it to," no bother, no arguing left in anyone by then.

By the time we locked up it was gone dimpsy, that grey time before it's properly dark, and we all stood a minute on the path looking back at it.

The uncle: "Well. That's that then."

And we drove off and left it, empty, for good this time.
