The aunt: Now don't go giving me that look before I've even started, my luvver, just hear us out. Her workmate's sister was saying to me only backalong, he's ever so steady, that one, up in his daps and out the door six every morning, never a day sick off it neither.

The niece: Mm.

The aunt: Three things I'll give you, and you can weigh 'em up yourself after. One, his work's proper, proper job that is, always summat coming in, none of this here-today-gone-tomorrow carry-on. Two, his temperament, gert patient he is, don't go raising his voice over nothing, not like some. And three, mind, the family he's come from, decent sort, her mother and all, chapel people, none of that nonsense you get round some doors.

The niece: I idn't saying he's not decent, aunt.

The aunt: Well what is it then, babber? What's wrong with someone like that, go on, tell us straight, 'cause I do struggle to see it myself.

The niece: I'm just that busy at the minute, aren't I. Work's mazed this month and I idn't hardly had time to think, let alone go courting proper. Idn't the right time for it, that's all.

The aunt: Busy. Hm. Your cousin said the very same to me, near enough word for word, over the one from the farm. Teasy as anything about it for a fortnight, wouldn't hear a word said. And her's glad of it now, mind, tells anyone who'll sit still long enough how right her mother was to keep on.

The niece: I do hear you, aunt, and I idn't being funny with you, but I've a right to my own mind on it same as her did.

The aunt: Well. I'll let it lie for now, then. But it stands, see, whenever you come round to it. Don't leave it sat there gone dimpsy on you. Mind.
