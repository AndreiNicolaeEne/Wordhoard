By gone midnight the house had thinned right down to the five of them round the kitchen table, everyone else seen off home with a hug and a ring me if you need owt. The big teapot had gone stone cold twice over already and nobody had the heart to put the kettle back on for a third go. Somebody'd fetched a couple of bottles of zider up out the pantry, proper cold, and stood them on the table next the sandwiches, though nobody had touched a drop of it yet. Outside had gone properly dimpsy hours back and now it was just black at the window and the fridge humming away to itself.

It was the eldest what started it off, quiet, like she was only thinking out loud.

The eldest: Do you mind how she used to stand at that sink of hers, singing away to herself. Not a tune you could ever put a name to, just noise really. And if you asked her what she was singing she'd tell you she never was.

The youngest: That wadn't this house though, was it. That was back at the old place, the one with the yard.

The eldest: Was it now. Aye, you're right, that's the yard house, idn't it, not this one at all. I always do get they two houses muddled up in my head, I don't know why, lived in both of 'em long enough.

Then the workmate let a laugh out, sudden and short, right out of nowhere, and put her hand up over her mouth quick after like her'd done summat wrong to laugh at all tonight. Nobody minded it though. The mother even give her a little smile for it.

The mother: Go on, my luvver, laugh if you want to laugh. Her'd have laughed harder than any of us at that, that one.

The workmate wiped her eyes, still half laughing, half something else, and didn't say nothing for a minute.

Then it was the mother's turn.

The mother: I do keep expecting her to walk in through that door, you know, silly as that sounds. And I'll tell you what she used to say, every single time you went out it, whatever the weather, whatever hour of day. She'd stand right there in the doorway and say...

The mother, doing the voice: You mind how you go now, and you see you wrap up warm, tis proper parky out there.

And everyone half laughed again because the mother had done it dead exact, word for word, tis proper parky out there, right down to the little tut she always give after it.

The neighbour: I don't know as any of you knew this one. Her used to go down her old allotment of a Sunday morning, on her own, before any of us were even up. Didn't garden, didn't dig nothing, didn't plant nothing. Just sat there on that old bench of hers a good hour, on her own, whatever the weather was doing.

The youngest: She never did!

The room went quiet a minute after that, just the fridge humming and somewhere a clock going. Nobody quite knew what to do with a bit of her that none of them had had.

Then the workmate reached over and pushed the plate of sandwiches back round the table again, just for something to do with her hands.

The workmate: Go on, have another one of they, there's plenty left, somebody's got to eat 'em before they go curling up at the edges. Where's the opener to, anyway, I had it a minute back.

And that broke it, everyone reaching for a sandwich they didn't really want, glad of the excuse to move.

The mother: You still haven't brought that ladder back, you know. Three year now it's been sat in your shed.

The eldest: Mother, please, not tonight.

The mother: I'm not starting nothing. Just saying it's been three year.

The eldest: It's a ladder.

The mother: It's my ladder. She'd have been proper teasy about you dragging it on this long, I'll tell you that much.

Neither of them pushed it no further than that, and the neighbour filled the gap by pouring the zider out at last, four little glasses, her hand not all that steady with it.

The workmate: You want to hear the one about they gardening daps of hers. Must've had 'em fifteen year if she'd had 'em a day, held together with tape and a bit of string, gert hole worn clean through one toe. Every birthday somebody'd get her a lovely new pair, still in the box now I'd think, cause she wouldn't wear 'em. Said the old ones knew where the puddles was and a new pair wouldn't.

The eldest: That's exactly the sort of thing she'd say an' all.

The neighbour: Backalong, before any of you lot were even born, her told me a tale about scrumping apples down Days Lane with her brothers, off some gert grumpy old man's tree, getting chased off by his dog every single time and going straight back the week after. Never once give up on it till the tree come down.

They were all laughing proper now, none of it with any edge left in it, just the old fondness for a woman who'd have her own way or no way at all, and every one of them round that table knew, without a single one saying it out plain, that was the thing they'd carry of her longest, that gert stubborn streak in her, mazed as it made them all at the time and dear as it was to think on now.

Partway through the laughing the youngest had gone quiet, and when the mother looked over her was crying, not loud, not making any show of it at all, just sat there with the tears going down while everyone else kept talking gentle around her, nobody stopping to make a fuss of it. The workmate just reached over and rested a hand on her arm without a word. Her empty chair at the other end of the table looked a proper wisht sight, nobody having moved it up to fill the gap.

The neighbour: Her wouldn't want us stopping on her account, mind.

And they didn't, not really, the talk just softened round the edges of it for a bit till the youngest wiped her face on her sleeve and picked her glass back up herself.

It was gone one in the morning before anyone said the practical of it.

The mother: Right. Somebody's got to ring the vicar back in the morning, and somebody's got to go through her wardrobe, I can't do that on my own, I know that much.

The eldest: I'll do the wardrobe with you. The neighbour can see to the flowers, her knows what she liked.

The neighbour: Course I will. I know exactly what her liked, none of they gert stiff lilies, she couldn't abide them, never could.

The workmate: I'll ring round the ones from the factory, let 'em know when it is, once you've a date sorted proper.

Said plain as that, no ceremony to it at all, just who was doing what, same as anything else that needed doing.

It was the neighbour still awake last, near enough everyone else gone quiet or nodding off by then, and her stood up with what was left in her glass and lifted it, nothing prepared, just what come to her there and then.

The neighbour: Well. Here's to her, then. Gert stubborn, gert kind, and never wrong about a thing in her own head if you asked her. We'll miss you summat rotten, my luvver, and that's the truth of it, mind.

And they drank to that, the four of them, quiet in the kitchen with the fridge still humming and the window gone properly black, and nobody said another word for a good while after, and none of them needed to.
