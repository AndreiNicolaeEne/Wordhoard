Now then, my babber, you sit yerself down there by me and I'll tell it the way I had it off my own gran, and her had it off hers before that, so mind, some of it's worn a bit smooth in the telling, the way a step gets worn smooth where everyone do put their foot the same place, but it's true for all that.

We wadn't always Bristol, see. Backalong the family was out west, proper out, up past where the moor comes down close to the sea, small bit of a farm, sheep mostly and a strip or two of oats that never did come to much most years. Landlord sold the ground out from under 'em to money that had no want of farming it, only of the view, and the rents went up gert high all of a sudden, and there wadn't the work to pay it, not steady work anyhow, only what a man could pick up here and there. Two bad harvests on top of that and it was done, really, there wadn't nothing else for it.

So it was the old man went first, the great-great of you, and he went on his own, mind, which took some doing, I do think, more than we ever give him credit for. Her, his wife, stopped behind with the little ones because there wadn't the money to shift the whole lot at once, and somebody had to keep what bit of a home they had going while he found his feet. He walked a fair part of it, they do say, down off the moor to where the carrier's cart went, and rode in the back of that with the churns and whatnot near enough two days, and then a bit of a train from there, third class and no seat left by the time he got on, standing the whole way in with the smoke coming back through the carriages till his eyes did run. He come into Bristol of a dimpsy evening, they always did say that bit, coming in as the light was going and not a soul in the world to meet him and not knowing where he was to lay his head that night.

First bit was hard, proper hard. No work waiting, nothing fixed, and he lodged in with another family down by the river that had a room going spare, paid what he could out of what little he'd brought, and slept top to tail with their boys some nights he was that squeezed in. Weeks of nothing steady, just a day's carrying here, a day humping sacks there, whatever a man could get taken on for of a morning standing at the gates with the rest hoping to be picked. Teasy work, that, standing about in all weathers waiting to be looked at like cattle at market, and more days turned away than not.

Then he got taken on regular down the cooperage by the docks, making up barrels, and that were the making of him really, because once a man had a trade in his hands and a foreman who'd vouch for him, the work did keep coming. He stuck to it and it stuck to him, and it went on down the family after, his own boy after him and one more after that, coopering, making a proper job of every barrel that went out, because a barrel that idn't tight idn't worth making at all, and they were proud of that, mind.

Took the best part of two year, near enough, before he'd saved enough and got settled enough to send for the rest of 'em. Two year is a gert long time to be apart with little ones growing and not seeing it. Her used to say, the great-great of you, the one that made the crossing with the children...

The great-great-grandmother: Her did tell it herself, back when I were small enough to be on her knee for it. Her'd say, we come over and there was one of us didn't come the whole way. A young cousin, taken bad on the journey and gone before ever we saw the place. Her never would give a name to it when her told it, only "the little one," or "that poor soul," like the name itself was too much to carry as well as everything else, and I never did ask for it neither, some things you don't ask.

That's the only place it's ever spoke of, that, and only her ever spoke it, so it's stayed that way in the telling, no name to it, just the once mentioned and left be.

Her brought hardly nothing over, what could you carry with little ones on each hand and a journey like that in front of you, but her did bring one thing wrapped up in her shawl the whole way, a little wooden butter print, carved with a bird on the face of it, that had been her own mother's before her, out on the varm, for stamping the butter of a market day. It's up on the shelf in this house now, this very day, and it'll go to you dreckly, when the time's right for it, mind, and you'll keep it same as it's been kept.

When her did land, the family wadn't whole again till that day, and they say it was some homecoming, though it was only two rooms up over a shop they'd found by then, no lodging with another family no more, a place of their own at last, small as it was, and proper glad of it too. They'd got that settled within the first few year of him being over, once the cooperage work held steady, and it's the same bit of the city near enough that some of us are in still.

Children come along after that, right there, one and then another, and that's really what made it home, I do reckon, more than the walls of it. Once there was little ones born there and no other place they'd ever known, it wadn't the old country's family living in Bristol no more, it was a Bristol family that had come from somewhere else once, which idn't the same thing at all, whatever anybody tells you.

Now I do mind hearing all this myself, sat where you're sat now, my luvver, hearing it off my own gran on a Sunday with a drop of zider going round for the grown-ups and us little ones sent out the back to play, scrumping what apples we could reach off next door's tree same as her own lot did once, I don't doubt, and coming in mazed with cold and wisht for our tea. And her'd tell it the same every time, near enough word for word, the farm and the leaving and the old man going first and the two year apart and the little one that got no name, right through to the butter print on the shelf.

So there tis, my babber, that's how we come to be where we're to, and now you've had it off me the way I had it off her, and you'll pass it on same as it was passed to you, mind, and see you don't lose the little one out of it neither, name or no name, because that's part of it same as the rest, whether it's an easy part to tell or not.
