Up before it were even light, proper black out over the yard, on'y the security lamp round the neighbour's giving anything away. I'd set the alarm gone four but never needed it, laid there listening to the wind already going in they trees, wondering if it was going to hold or not. I don't mind admitting it, I do love that hour of the morning, quiet as anything, before everyone else is up and moving about.

Daps by the back door where I'd left 'em the night before so's I wasn't stood fumbling for shoes in the dark, kettle on first, always the kettle first. Mother was up before me anyroad, always is, stood at the stove with her cardigan over her nightie saying we'd want summat hot in us before we started, no good going out on empty.

Mother: You en't going up there on a cup of tea and nothing else, my luvver. Sit down two minutes.

I sat, but I wadn't really tasting it, mind already up the top with they trees, wondering how many'd come down in the night with the wind we'd had.

First proper job of the day, soon as there was light enough to see your own hand in front of you, was sorting out what needed doing and who was doing it, and that fell to Mother same as it always does, stood in the yard with her hands on her hips looking up the field like her was reading it off a page.

Mother: Right. Windfalls first, get they picked up before anyone goes treading on 'em and mashing 'em into the grass. Youngest can do that, low enough for her.

So the youngest got sent off with the little basket, the easy job, going along under the trees picking up what the wind had already done for us, bunging the bad ones with the wasps in over the hedge and keeping the good ones separate. Her didn't half moan about the wasps mind.

The youngest: There's one in here going for me, I swear it followed me from the last tree.

The eldest: It idn't following you, babber, you're stood in its way is all. Move on then.

The eldest got the hard end of it, same as always, up the ladder with the pole and the bag round his neck, working the top branches where the windfalls hadn't reached, shaking down what was ready and holding on with the other hand so as not to come off with it. Gert big lad, strong as anything, carried two full crates down to the trailer at once without so much as setting one down to rest. That was the heaviest part of the whole day and it fell to him same as it did backalong, same as it did the year before that, on'y complaint out of him being the ladder creaking.

We cleared the first row by mid morning and shifted everything along to the next bit, ladders, crates, baskets, all of us moving at once like we'd done it a hundred times, which I s'pose we had.

It was somewhere in that second row Gran started falling behind. Her wouldn't say nothing about it, just went slower and slower, hand going to her back every now and then when her thought no one was looking. No one said a word to her, no sense making her feel bad over it, the eldest just quietly finished off her tree while her was moving the ladder along, and I took to sorting what her'd already picked so it looked like her'd got through more than her had. Her knew what we was at, I reckon, but her never said nothing and neither did we.

Round about eleven Mother brought bread and cheese and a flask out, set it all down on the tailgate of the trailer, and we all stopped where we was and came over. Ham roll each, bit of fruit cake after, tea strong enough to stand a spoon in.

The uncle: Proper job, that. I could eat a horse and chase the jockey after it.

Mother: You'll have to make do with cheese then, there's no horse going spare round here.

Gran: Tis going to turn later, you mark my words. I can feel it in this shoulder of mine.

There was a walker come along the footpath at the bottom of the field about then, stopped a minute to look at us all at it, and the uncle give a wave and a grin.

The uncle: Least it en't grockle season proper yet, we'd never hear the last of it with a coachload stopped to watch.

We had a bit of an argument over the tea break too, over whether to keep shaking the trees down or go back to hand picking, the eldest reckoning shaking was quicker and Gran reckoning it bruised half of 'em and bruised ones don't keep and don't press as good neither.

Gran: You shake 'em down like that they're half mush by the time they hit the crate. Hand pick the good ones, let the rest go for scrumping.

The eldest: We'll be here till dimpsy at that rate.

Gran: Better dimpsy than a crate of bruises, my ansum.

Nobody won that one. We just went back to it doing a bit of both same as always, and the youngest scrumped one out the windfall basket when her thought nobody was looking, laughing round a mouthful of it.

By early afternoon the sky had gone that colour it goes before it turns, gert dark cloud coming up over from the west, wind picking up all of a sudden the way it does. Mother was straight out looking at it with her hand up shading her eyes.

Mother: That en't going to hold. We want the top field done first, never mind the hedge row, that can wait, get up there now afore it comes over.

So the whole lot of us shifted up top, leaving half a row not finished down below, everyone working quicker with one eye on the sky. It held off though in the end, just a spit of rain and nothing more, blew on through and left it bright again, so we pushed on glad of it, getting the top field near done while the going was good.

Then the barrow wheel come off. Just come clean off the axle halfway up the slope with a full load on it, apples going everywhere down the bank, half of 'em straight into the nettles.

The eldest: Oh, that's proper teasy, that is.

Mother: Don't stand there looking mazed at it, somebody go and pick they up before the wasps do.

We picked up what we could, left the wisht split ones for the birds, and the uncle got the barrow going again with a bit of string and a length of pipe wedged where the pin should've been.

The uncle: Where's that hammer to? I had it not two minutes back.

It'll hold long as nobody leans on it, he said, and nobody did after that.

Come late afternoon the light started going that gold colour it goes before dimpsy sets in proper, and Mother started passing the word to move quicker, no more standing about talking.

Mother: Come on now, we en't got all evening, get they last few trees done afore we lose the light altogether.

So we all converged on the last bit together, the whole lot of us in that one corner, the youngest handing up baskets, the eldest up the ladder, Gran and the uncle and me all picking below, everyone a bit in each other's way but getting it done all the same, and we cleared the last tree just as the light really was going, dimpsy proper settling in over the field, everything gone that blue-grey colour it goes.

Come the end of it we tallied up, near on thirty crates all told, gert lot more than we reckoned on when we started, enough for a proper press and zider besides if the weather holds for pressing day too, dreckly, whenever that ends up being.

Everyone just sort of sat down where they was after that, right there on the crates and the grass, nobody saying much, too wisht to talk, glad of it just being done. Mother was the one got up first, always is.

Mother: Right, before anyone gets comfortable, somebody's got to shut they hens in before it's proper dark, mind, they'll be all over the place else.

So the youngest got sent off one more time, dragging her feet something wisht about it, to shut the hens in for the night, while the rest of us sat there in the near dark not moving, listening to her grumbling all the way up the yard, and that was the day done, see, start to finish.
