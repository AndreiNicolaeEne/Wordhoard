It do come up the same way most times. Late of an evening, everyone gone quiet after their tea, and some young one asking why nobody round here walks the towpath past dimpsy on their own, not even now, not even grown. I've had that question put to me more times than I care to count and I always answer it the same, because tis the only way I know how, the way it were give to me.

Sit down then, babber, and I'll tell it you proper, start to finish, the way my own nan told it me.

You don't see her first. That's the thing folk get wrong straight off, thinking there's a shape to look out for, something you'd know if you clapped eyes on it. Tisn't like that at all. Her takes your ear before her ever takes a form, and by the time there's anything to look at, if there's anything to look at at all, you're already half gone from where you started. Some say a shadow thicker than the hedge round it, some say nothing, just the dark gone wrong somehow, gone thick. I don't rightly know and I've never asked to find out.

What her does, and this is the part that puts the cold in you properly, is call. Not a howl, not nothing animal, but a voice, and it idn't a stranger's voice neither. Her'll call in the voice of somebody you love. Your mother, your brother, whoever it is you'd come running for without a thought, and that's the whole cruelty of it, see, because her only wants you off the path and away from anybody as could pull you back. Her don't come calling in the daylight and her don't come calling with a moon up neither. Only after midnight, and only on they nights when there idn't a sliver of moon showing, black dark, the kind where you can't see your own hand held up.

Round here her's tied to the one bend down by the old lock, where the towpath dips in under the trees and the water goes quiet enough you can hear yourself breathe. Not the whole gorge, mind, not the whole river, just that one stretch, that one bend. I've heard tell there's spots like it dotted about everywhere, every village its own, but I don't speak for those, I only speak for ours.

I were no age at all when I first heard it, sat up past when I should've been sleeping, and it were my own grandad telling it, not my nan that time, older than I am now by a fair few years and his voice gone rough with it. He told it plain, flat, like you'd tell a body the shop shuts at five, no dressing on it at all, and that were worse somehow than if he'd made a game of it. There weren't a laugh in him telling it, not once, and looking back that's the thing sticks with me most, more than the story itself some nights.

Now, there's proof offered, always has been, and the first bit is the ground itself. Right at that bend there's a patch, not gert big, no bigger than a good-sized room, where nothing has ever taken. Grass won't hold, nothing planted there ever comes up, not so much as a nettle, and believe me plenty's tried, out of spite as much as curiosity. My own uncle put seed down there one spring just to prove it wrong and come back three months on to bare dirt same as before, like the seed never went in at all.

The second bit you don't ever get first-hand, and that's part of it too, part of how it stays true I reckon. Somebody always knows somebody who knows the one it happened to. A workmate's cousin, a friend of a friend's uncle, always one step off from whoever's telling it, and every account lines up close enough that you stop thinking it's coincidence after the third or fourth time you hear it told separate.

But the one that gets told whole, every time, start to finish, no shortening it, is what happened to my own uncle, backalong, must be forty year or more now. He'd been down the vield helping with the last of the harvest and walked home past midnight, no moon that night at all, and a drop of zider in him besides, though he always swore after that the zider had nothing to do with what he heard. Clear as anything, he heard his own name called from off the path, in his mother's voice, his own dead mother by then some years, calling him same as her used to call him in for his tea. And he went. Three steps, four, off the path and in toward the trees before something in him, he never could say what, made him stop dead and turn himself round and walk the other way without once looking back.

He'd tell it the very same words every time after, word for word, however many times he told it: I heard her plain as I hear you now, and I knowed it weren't her, I knowed it in my gut before I knowed it in my head, and that's the only reason I'm stood here telling it you at all.

The detail that quiets a room, even the ones rolling their eyes at the rest of it, is that he never could say what made him stop. Not a sound, not a sight, nothing he could point to, just a knowing that come from nowhere he could name, and that not-knowing is the bit that gets under people even now.

The detail that gives the doubters their laugh is the zider, of course, always is. A man's been at the drink half the night, walking home alone past midnight, hears his dead mother call his name, well, they'll say, there's your explanation, and I don't fault them for it. I'd say the same probably, if it weren't my own uncle telling it me with his hands not quite steady even years on.

I'll say honest, the story do change a bit depending whose mouth it's coming from. My nan told it softer, always give the thing a reason for what it done, near made you feel sorry for it by the end. My grandad told it hard, no comfort in it at all, just a warning plain and simple. I've never minded the difference, mind, I reckon that's just what happens to a thing passed hand to hand this long, it takes a bit of whoever's carrying it.

What I do know is this. Round here, you don't answer if you hear your name called from somewhere dark, not straight off, not never, not till you've laid your own two eyes on whoever's doing the calling. Don't matter whose voice it is, don't matter how sure you feel. You wait, you look, you make certain first. Been that way long as I remember and I don't reckon it's changing.

And I'll tell you honest too, since you asked it honest. I don't rightly believe it, not all the way, not in the daylight anyway. But I've walked home past that bend more evenings than I could count over the years, and I've heard my name called from off that path more than the once, and every single time, I've waited to see who it were before I've said a word back. Every time. Mind that, now, whatever else you take from it. See you don't forget it neither.
