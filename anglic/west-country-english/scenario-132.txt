Eleven days it went on for, no let up, not one. From the very first morning you could tell 'twadn't going to be one of they ordinary snaps, the air had a bite to it that got right in your chest soon as you stepped out the door. Had the stove going morning noon and night the whole stretch, never let her go out, feeding her logs and whatever scrap wood was down the shed just to keep the front room bearable at all.

Then the outside tap went. Solid through. Never done that before, not in all the years we've had it. Had to run the hose off the kitchen tap out the back door just to give the hens a drink, and even that had froze over again by dinnertime.

The eldest: I do mind the winter of forty-seven and all, and I never thought I'd see her beat. Well he's beat now.

Me: You sure of that, then?

The eldest: Proper job of a cold this is, babber. Worse'n anything backalong, I'm telling you.

Worst of it was the one night bang in the middle of the stretch, when the thermometer out the shed just give up telling you owt at all. Woke up teasy and half mazed with it even under three blankets, and by morning we'd lost the last of the leeks in the ground, frosted right through to mush, and one of the hens never saw the morning neither.

Eleven days her held like that, and then the eleventh night the wind swung round, and by dimpsy the next evening you could feel it going soft in the air, that first bit of a thaw creeping in under the cloud. Woke to water dripping steady off the shed roof, and I stood out there in me daps just listening to it a minute, proper glad of it, mind.
