Well, I'll tell 'ee, this were the year, no mistake about it. Clear as day early doors, the way the trees were hangin, gert heavy with it, comin in at near twice what we normally get off 'em, and everyone down the lane sayin so from May onward, see.

The plums went proper mad but 'twas the apples beat all, the whole orchard nothin but fruit top to bottom, more'n we'd zeed since donkeys years back, further than anyone could rightly put a number to. Had to get extra hands in and all, our own weren't enough for it, cousins and workmates and whoever'd stand still long enough, all of us out there half mazed with it, gettin it in some nights till gone dimpsy, afore it turned on the ground and went to waste.

More than we could ever use ourselves so a fair bit went down the store, more still into zider, and the rest got traded on, round to they what hadn't got none of their own that year.

Me: Where's that lot to, then, the good crates?

A workmate: Round the back, my luvver, mind you don't drop none.

Me: How's it stack up against what you remember, then?

The oldest grower: Best I've ever zeed, and I've zeed a fair few, I can tell 'ee. 'Tis the rain what done it, comin right when 'twere wanted, not too much to make a body teasy over it, and a mild stretch right through summer an all. Idn't always so kind to us, mind.

Me: Proper job, that.

The oldest grower: Ar. Proper job. Won't zee another like it in my time, mind.

And that's how it's stood since. Any poor harvest and folks do zay, aye, but 'tisn't like that year, and everyone knows which one they mean without another word said, backalong now but not forgot.
