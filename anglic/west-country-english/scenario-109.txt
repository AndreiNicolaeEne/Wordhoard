The door goes and there's a second, just a second, where the mother's looking right at me and her face idn't doing nothing at all, like her's looking at a rep come to read the meter. Then it goes.

The mother: Oh my days. Oh my days, no. No, it idn't. IS IT YOU?

Me: It's me, Mum. I'm home.

The mother: GET OUT OF IT. GET OUT OF IT NOW.

And then her's got hold of me and her idn't letting go, arms right round, face in my coat, and I can feel her shaking with it, and it goes on, it goes on that long the little one behind her starts up laughing at the state of it.

The mother: I don't believe you. I do not believe you, stood there. Let me look at you. Let me LOOK at you, don't you dare pull away yet.

Nan, from the front room: What's all the noise, what's, oh my good Lord.

Nan: (her's got her hand flat on her chest now) Well would you look at that. Would you just look. Her's doing service for a word better'n "look," her can't find it, so her just says it twice more, look, look.

Nan: You've gone and got a gert beard on you. I never. Proper job, mind, it do suit you, but I never in my life.

The eldest: (shoving past to get her arms round me an' all) You're not going anywhere, you idn't going anywhere ever again, I'm telling you now, we do talk about you every single week, don't we, don't we say it every week...

The mother: We do, we do say it...

The eldest:, and here you are big as brass stood in the hall like you only popped out for daps. Oh my LORD you've changed. You've changed that much I nearly shut the door on you, I swear down.

Uncle: (coming through from the kitchen with the tea towel still in his hand) Now then. Now then, look what the cat's dragged in from the airport.

Uncle: (proper gruff, but his voice do go a bit funny on it) Alright, babber.

Uncle: (and he's not managing it, not really, he's turned right round to put the tea towel down so nobody sees, but everybody sees) Give us a minute. Give us a, go on, get in the warm, don't stand there in the draught.

The youngest, hanging back by the stairs, half behind the eldest's leg: (whispering) Is that, is that really them?

The eldest: That's them alright. Say hello, go on.

The youngest: (doesn't move, just has a proper stare, gone all shy of the whole fuss, thumb near enough in the mouth)

Me: Alright, my luvver. You won't remember me.

The youngest: (shakes head, hides further)

The mother: He was only knee high last you saw him, don't you go taking it personal, he's just gone teasy with all the noise, he'll come round.

Nan: Give him a minute, he's mazed with the lot of us hollering.

The mother: (sniffing the air, sudden, like it's caught her off guard) Oh, can you smell that? That's my gravy on, that is, I had it on low all afternoon just in case, I said to your uncle, I said, they'll walk in starving, mark my words...

Uncle: You did say that.

The mother:, and there's the same old smell of this house under it an' all, idn't there, same old smell, been the same smell twenty year.

Me: It is. Soon as the door opened I got it. Same smell exactly. Gone straight through me, that has.

The bags are still sat there by the door where I dropped 'em, nobody's so much as looked at 'em twice.

The eldest: Right, sit down, sit down, there's that much to tell you I don't know where to start. Right, the cousin, you know the cousin, well her's married now, done it back in the spring, lovely do it was, out past the downs, and her's expecting an' all, due end of the year.

Nan: And the neighbour's boy, the one used to knock about with you scrumping down the lane behind the allotments, grown a foot, gone and joined the fire service, if you please, walks round like he owns the street now.

Uncle: And your mate from school, the tall one, moved out Weston way, got himself a little place near the seafront, packed it all in and went.

The mother: And your Aunt's not been so good, but her's alright now, don't you fret, her's just gone a bit wisht this last while, nothing serious.

Me: Blimey. That's, that's a lot to take in all at once. Everyone's gone and got on with it without me, idn't they.

The eldest: Well what did you expect, the world to stop turning?

Me: (looking round the hall, up the stairs, at the same wallpaper, the same coat hooks) It's mad, though. Half of it's exactly the same, the smell, them hooks, that crack in the ceiling you never did get seen to...

Uncle: Oi, don't you start on that crack...

Me:, and half of it's not. The corner shop's gone, idn't it, saw it walking up, all boarded. And you've had the front done, that's new. And you've all got so much bigger and older and I weren't even here for none of it.

Nan: That's what happens when you go swanning off eight year, my love. World keeps on.

The mother: So how long we got you for this time? Proper, now, none of your dodging.

Me: Bit longer than last time. A good few weeks, I reckon, if that's alright.

The mother: Alright? ALRIGHT? Right, that settles it, we're having everyone round. Sunday. All of 'em. I'm doing a proper spread, none of your bits on toast, a proper spread, and your Aunt can bring that trifle her does, and we'll get some zider in an' all, proper stuff, not that fizzy rubbish.

Uncle: Her's already planning the seating.

The mother: I am not.

Nan: Her is.

The eldest: (grinning, nudging me) 'Ere. D'you remember what Nan used to call you, when you was little and you'd come in filthy from being out all day?

Nan: (cackling before anyone's even said it) My gert mazed maid!

The whole room goes off at that, even though half of 'em weren't even born when it started, don't matter, it's ours, don't need explaining to nobody, it just gets said and everyone falls about.

And then, just for a second, right in the middle of all the laughing, everyone goes quiet at once, don't rightly know why, just goes still, and it's everyone stood there in that hall just looking at me, proper looking, like they're checking I'm real, checking I'm not going to go again, and I'm looking right back at all of them, and nobody says a thing.

Nan: (breaking it, soft) Well. There you are, then.

Me: Here I am.

The mother: (wiping her eyes with the back of her hand, laughing at herself for it) Right. Right, that's enough of that. Kettle's not put itself on. Sit down, the lot of you, give the poor soul some room to breathe, mind, and somebody get them bags in out the hall before we all fall over 'em.

And it settles, after that, the way it always does, everyone finding a chair, the little one creeping a bit closer now, the tea going round, and the questions still coming but slower now, easier, like the first hour's done its worst and we can all just sit in it a while.
