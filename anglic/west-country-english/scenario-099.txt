Me: Alright, my luvver! Bloody hell, I never thought I'd see you again, not in a month of Sundays.

The old classmate: I know, ay! Look at you an' all, you idn't changed a bit, babber.

Me: Give over, I'm gert older than I was, don't you lie to me. What you doing round here then?

The old classmate: Just picking up a few bits for the tea. I do come down the market most Saturdays now, it's cheaper than that big shop up the top.

Me: Same as ever then. How's your mum keeping, is her alright?

The old classmate: Oh her's grand, bit of trouble with her hip but her won't hear a word about slowing down. And yours?

Me: Can't complain. Working still, up the docks, same as before. You?

The old classmate: I'm over the other side now, driving, but tis alright, keeps the wolf from the door.

Me: Good, good. Proper glad to see you, I am, it's been years.

The old classmate: You know who I saw the other day, down the precinct? Only her. From school.

Me: Not her! God above, I ain't thought about her in years. Do she still look the same?

The old classmate: Older, obviously, but you'd know her anywhere. Put the fear of God in me, that woman.

Me: Don't. I still go cold thinking about her class.

The old classmate: That's cos she had one rule and one rule only, didn't she. You never, ever spoke out of turn. Not a word, not a whisper, till her said your name.

Me: And if you did?

The old classmate: You know what if you did. Lines. A hundred lines, every single time, whether it were "sorry Miss" under your breath or just dropping your pencil too loud.

Me: "I must not speak without permission." I can still see it in my own writing, a hundred times over, my hand proper aching by the end of it. Your head goes mazed doing the same line that many times.

The old classmate: A hundred, mind. Not ninety-nine. Her'd count them her own self.

Me: Her would an' all. And do you remember what her used to say, right at the start of every lesson? "I am not here to be liked, I am here to be obeyed." Every single time. Word for word, same as that.

The old classmate: Gave me the shivers just then, you saying it back like that.

Me: Do you remember when her got that girl up the front, the one who dropped her whole pencil case, everything went everywhere, and her just stood there while the whole class watched her pick it up? Nobody moved to help. Nobody dared.

The old classmate: I remember it proper. Wisht it was, watching that. We all just sat there like stone.

Me: I still remember freezing right up when her called on me. Couldn't get a word out even when I knew the answer. Heart going like billy-o.

The old classmate: Everyone did that. 'Cept for the one time, do you remember, when he talked back to her?

Me: Oh my god, yeah! I still can't hardly believe that happened, even now.

The old classmate: Nor can I, and I watched it with my own two eyes. Her'd got on at him for something daft, and he just turned round and said, calm as you like, "well that idn't fair, Miss, and you know it."

Me: And nothing happened to him!

The old classmate: Nothing! Not a line, not a word. Her just looked at him and moved on like nothing were said. We were all sat there waiting for the roof to come in and it never did.

Me: To this day I don't know how he got away with that.

The old classmate: Nor me. Braver than any of us put together, that boy.

Me: D'you remember her little habit though, with the chalk? Tapping it three times on the desk before her'd start talking.

The old classmate: Tap tap tap! Oh we used to take the mick somethin' rotten behind her back, didn't we. Whole class doing it under the desks when her weren't looking.

Me: And that face her'd pull if the chalk snapped. Proper teasy about it, every single time.

The old classmate: Do you remember the one time though, when her was kind? Proper caught me off guard, that did.

Me: Go on.

The old classmate: That lad, the quiet one, whose mum was poorly that year, remember? Her kept him back one dinnertime and I thought, here we go, he's for it now. But her just sat with him a minute and told him it were alright to be upset, and give him a bit of chocolate out her own drawer. Never saw that side of her again, not once.

Me: Never even heard about that till now. That's proper strange to hear.

The old classmate: I know. Made me think different of her after, even though I never told a soul at the time.

Me: What do you reckon happened to her anyway? Someone said her retired years back. Where's she living to now, d'you know?

The old classmate: That's what I heard an' all, but someone else told me her went off teaching abroad somewhere. Nobody really knows for certain, do they.

Me: No. Funny that, someone looms that big over your whole childhood and then just vanishes off and you never find out proper.

The old classmate: When you look back on it though, it idn't fear no more, is it. Not really.

Me: No. More like respect, if I'm honest. Her made us work, and her never let nobody mess about.

The old classmate: That's it exactly. Proper job of a teacher, say what you like about her methods.

Me: God, imagine trying that now though, a hundred lines. They'd have her up in front of the governors inside a week.

The old classmate: They would an' all! Can you imagine the letter home!

Me: Oh, my sides. I ain't laughed like that in ages, honest.

The old classmate: Mind you, I still get a little flutter of it now, thinking about her. Like her's gonna come round that corner and catch me talking out of turn.

Me: Same here if I'm honest. Old habits die hard.

The old classmate: Anyway, how's the family keeping? You still up that way?

Me: Yeah, still up there. Gert lot of building work going on round us mind, can't hardly hear yourself think some days.

The old classmate: Tell me about it, whole street's dug up over our way an' all. And the car her's playing up something chronic on top of it.

Me: You still down the allotment?

The old classmate: When I get a chance. Grew some proper good spuds this year. Wish I had time for a bit of scrumping like we used to, mind, save on buying the apples.

Me: Don't, I got caught once, remember, over the wall by the church, and her mum came out screaming blue murder.

The old classmate: I remember that! You legged it so fast you left one of your daps behind you.

Me: Don't remind me, my mum went spare over that.

The old classmate: Back-along we used to be down that lane till gone dimpsy, didn't we, scared to go home in case someone had told on us first.

Me: We did. Simpler times, mind, whatever her'd have said about it.

The old classmate: We should get a proper drink sometime, catch up properly. Bottle of zider down the pub, like old times.

Me: Go on then, my luvver. You got my number still?

The old classmate: I do. I'll give you a bell dreckly.

Me: Alright then. You take the kids down Weston at all these days?

The old classmate: Every summer, though it's packed out with grockles the whole season now, can't move on that beach for towels.

Me: Same down our way. Right, I best get on, but it's been lovely seeing you, mind.

The old classmate: You too, babber. Take care now, see.

Me: You an' all. See you.
