Righto, come in, mind the step, it's always been a bit dodgy, that one, never got fixed, just got walked around for about thirty years. This is it. This is the whole thing, so don't go expecting a big reveal round every corner, but.

See here at the front, this bit right inside the door, that's the first thing that hits you, always has, this sort of cool dark smell off the tiles, like the sun never quite got in there even on a stinker of a day. Heaps of coats piled on that hook, always one too many for the hook, and the school bags just dumped wherever they landed. You'd come in from footy or from the pool up the road and it'd be like walking into a fridge, honestly, best feeling going. The mother had this rule, shoes off at the door, and did anyone ever listen? Did they hell. There'd be this little battle of thongs and sneakers built up like a shrine to nobody in particular.

Through here's the main room, and this was where everything happened, fair dinkum, everything. The telly in the corner, always a bit dodgy on channel two, you'd have to give the aerial a wiggle. This was footy on the weekend with the old man losing his mind at the ref, this was Christmas, this was where you got growled at for jumping on the couch, this was where the whole family sat when someone was doing it tough and nobody said much, just sat there together, which said plenty really. The good rug in here, the one that wasn't for lying on, except everyone lay on it anyway.

Mother: Get off the good rug.

Me: It's comfy though.

Mother: I don't care if it's comfy, get off it.

Nobody ever got off it properly, we'd just shuffle to the edge and call it a compromise.

Kitchen's down the hall, and mate, the smell in there, onions frying off, that's the base smell of my whole childhood, onions and something on the stove going since about four in the arvo. One habit that always happened in that kitchen, without fail, was the mother on the phone with the cord stretched right across the doorway so you had to duck under it, having a good old whinge to her sister about something or other, the whole call punctuated with a wooden spoon waving around like a conductor's baton. You'd come in starving after school and she'd say she'll be right, dinner's not long, and it was always at least another forty minutes, but you learnt to just grab a bikkie and cop it sweet.

Bedrooms were up the back. Me and my brother shared, bunk beds, and let me tell you there is no justice system on earth as brutal as two kids negotiating over top bunk. My sister had her own room, smallest one in the house, always felt like she got dudded on space but she reckoned she didn't care because at least she didn't have someone's feet in her face all night.

Brother: You're on the floor if you keep kicking me.

Me: I'm not even near you.

Brother: You are, your foot's literally on my pillow.

That argument ran on a loop for years, never got resolved, just got aged out of when we both got too big for the bunks.

The back room, the little one off the laundry, that was Nan's, in everyone's mind, even after she wasn't living there full time anymore, even years after. Nobody else's stuff went in that room. Her chair, her little telly, the smell of Tiger Balm and this particular brand of mints she was mad keen on. You'd go and sit with her of an arvo and she'd tell you about someone down the street being a bit of a bogan behind their back, in the nicest possible way, mind you.

My own spot, the one that was properly mine, was under the house. Half the yard backed onto this raised bit, and under the floorboards there was a gap, dark, full of spiders probably, but I didn't care, I'd drag a milk crate down there and that was my whole kingdom. Nobody came looking for you under there. It was the one place in the house where you could just sit and reckon about whatever you wanted without someone telling you to come set the table.

Backyard was nothing flash, bit of grass the old man was always threatening to reseed and never did, the Hills hoist dead centre like it owned the place, and the esky permanently parked near the back step for whenever anyone had people over. Barbie went on out there most weekends in summer, snags going, mozzies going too, someone always getting bitten to bits and blaming someone else for not putting the coil on early enough.

Neighbours either side you could hear as clear as your own family, near enough. One side had a dog that barked at absolutely nothing, every single evening around six, like clockwork, you could set your watch by that dog. Other side was an older bloke, kept to himself mostly, but you'd hear him out the front of a Sunday morning giving his car a wash and having a bit of a whinge to himself about the rego going up again, or Centrelink mucking someone about, or the PBS not covering something it used to. Same sound every single day though, without fail, was the train line a few streets over, that low sort of rumble right on peak hour, six of a morning and again about five-thirty, and you just stopped hearing it after a while, it became like breathing.

Same sight every day, near enough, was the postie coming round about eleven, and later on it was the same bloke doing his lap with his dog, same time, same route, you could nearly time your brekkie by him walking past the window.

Street out the front was where the real stuff happened though. Cricket right up the middle of the road with a rubbish bin for stumps, someone's brother always fielding reluctantly near the gutter, and every car that came round the corner meant everyone yelling car and the whole game freezing till it went past. Bit of hopscotch chalk that never quite washed off the footpath no matter how much it rained. In summer it'd go till it was properly dark and someone's mum would be out the front yelling that dinner was on the table, come in now, and youse would ignore her for one more over.

Last time I remember properly being there, actual clear memory, was a barbie out the back, everyone crammed in, the esky nearly empty of ice, someone giving the old man heaps about his snag-flipping technique, and the mother laughing at something on the phone to her sister, cord all stretched out again same as ever. Just an ordinary arvo, nothing special happening, but I remember it dead clear, don't ask me why that one and not another.

It's still standing, the house, but it's not ours anymore, hasn't been for years now. Someone's done it up, put a nicer fence in, got rid of the Hills hoist which honestly broke my heart a bit when I drove past and saw the bare patch of grass where it used to be. Different mob living there now, different car in the drive, and you'd walk past and have no idea what it used to hold, none at all.

Anyway. That's the tour, mate. Probably more than you bargained for, but you did ask, so.
