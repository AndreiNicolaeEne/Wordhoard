I don't tell this one for a laugh, and I don't tell it to make out I copped something I didn't, because I didn't, not really. But every so often it comes up and I tell it straight, the way it actually went, no more and no less.

This was overland, crossing out of one country into the next on foot, the way you do when the road runs out and everyone gets down off the bus and walks the last stretch themselves, bags on backs, passports out, because that's just where the two countries meet and somebody's got to look at everyone before they're let go on. Makes sense when you think about it after, a border's a border, they're not going to wave a whole busload through without a look. But you don't think about it like that at the time. At the time it's just a long shed with a tin roof and a queue that's not moving.

And it wasn't moving. Heaps of people in front of us, a good half hour just standing there shuffling forward a foot at a time, the pack getting heavier on your shoulders the longer you stood, sun coming through the roof making the whole shed heaps hot.

The bloke behind us: How long youse been waiting?

Me: Dunno. Feels like a fair while. Reckon it's moving, but heaps slow.

Kept telling myself she'll be right the whole way up that queue, plenty of people go through here every day, you've got nothing on you, nothing to hide, it's fine. And I believed that, mostly. But there's a difference between believing something and your stomach believing it, and my stomach hadn't got the memo. Heaps more nervous by the time we were near the front than I let on to anyone I was travelling with.

Get up to the window finally and hand over the passport, the visa form, and the little arrival card you fill out on the bus with a pen that's not really working.

The officer: Purpose of visit.

Me: Holiday. Just travelling through.

That one's easy, you say it without thinking, plain as anything, because it's true and there's nothing behind it. But then comes the one that wants a bit more care.

The officer: How long you stay in my country.

That's the one you've got to get exactly right, because the visa in his hand already says a number of days on it, and if what comes out of your mouth doesn't match that number, that's when things go slow for you. So you stop half a second, do the sum in your head even though you already know the answer, and say it careful.

Me: Fourteen days.

He looks down at the visa, looks at the card, looks up at me. Doesn't say anything for a second. That little stretch of quiet while someone's going through your papers is a different kind of quiet to any other, I reckon. You notice every second of it, standing there trying to look like a person who's got nothing to be nervous about, which is a heaps harder thing to do than it sounds.

Then he asks where I'm staying that first night, and I go blank, properly blank, because I'd booked it off the phone the night before and hadn't written the name down anywhere, just had it saved in an app, and for a horrible second I couldn't remember it at all. Had to dig the phone out right there at the window, thumb it open in front of him, find the booking, and read the name off the screen. Felt about as smooth as it sounds, which isn't very. He just nodded and wrote it down, no dramas.

There was a bloke a few spots ahead of us in the queue, travelling on his own, who got held back a lot longer than anyone else. They took him off to the side of the window, still going through his passport page by page, and nobody in the queue said a word about it, not out loud anyway, but everyone was watching out the corner of their eye, the way you do, careful not to get caught watching either. He came good in the end, walked off with his bag over his shoulder same as anyone, but for a good ten minutes there it looked dodgy, and it put a different kind of quiet over the rest of us waiting behind him.

Kept my voice level the whole time I was up at that window, which took more out of me than I'd have admitted at the time. You keep your face doing the ordinary thing, keep the voice doing the ordinary thing, because you know that's half of what they're looking at, whether you're acting normal or acting like someone trying to act normal, and the two look different if a person's paying attention. He was paying attention.

Only nice bit of it came right near the end. He'd been dead serious the whole way through, no smile, nothing, and then he saw I'd written the date on the card the wrong way round, day and month back to front from how they write it there, and instead of making a thing of it he just leaned over, crossed it out himself, wrote it the right way round, and gave me this quick look, almost a laugh, like he does this ten times a day and it's nothing to him. Didn't say sorry for me, didn't need to, just fixed it and kept going. Small thing. Meant heaps at the time.

Stamp came down, passport slid back across the bench under the glass, and he just tipped his head, once, toward the door on the other side. Didn't say go. Didn't need to. That head-tip did the whole job.

Walked out through to the other side, and it's a funny thing, you'd reckon the relief hits you straight off, soon as you're through, but it doesn't, not for me anyway. Walked a good twenty minutes down that road before it actually let go, shoulders coming down off round my ears, hands not gripping the pack straps so tight, before I noticed I was breathing normal again rather than just breathing. That's when it caught up with me, the toll of it, not at the window where you'd think, but well down the road, safe, with the whole thing behind us and nothing left to hold together for.

So that's why I tell it careful when I tell it at all. Wasn't a drama, nobody did anything to me, papers were in order the whole time, I got through same as everyone does most days. But it wasn't nothing either, standing there with your heart going while a stranger decides in about ninety seconds whether your trip keeps going or not. Not going to sit here and make it out bigger than that, and not going to make it smaller either, pretend it was nothing when it wasn't nothing to me at the time. It was exactly what it was, fair dinkum. Half hour in a queue, a couple of questions, a bloke ahead of us who copped it worse, and twenty minutes of walking before my own body believed we were right.
