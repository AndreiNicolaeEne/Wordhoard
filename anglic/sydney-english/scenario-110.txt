Mother: Righto, sit down, sit down, there's tea in the pot. So. Come on then, what's it actually like up there? Proper picture, none of this "yeah good" you give us on the phone.

Me: Ah, it's heaps different, I reckon. Big. Like you forget how big till you're in it every day. Here you can get anywhere in fifteen, twenty minutes tops. Up there some days it's the better part of an hour just to get from one side to the other, and that's if the trains are running right, which, you know.

The eldest: An hour? To go where, though.

Me: Nah like literally across the city, one side to the other. And it's not even that far on the map, it's just everything. Traffic, roads all banked up, everyone doing the same trip at the same time. You leave heaps early or you cop it.

Father: Bloody hell. And the noise of it, I bet. You'd never sleep.

Me: First month, no. Sirens, blokes doing donuts somewhere, some mozzie in the room on top of it all, the whole racket. You get used to it but. It goes quiet in your head after a while, you just don't hear it the same.

Aunty: So where are yiz actually living then. Your mother wouldn't say properly, said you were sharing with someone.

Me: Yeah, sharing. Little unit, two bedrooms, I've got the smaller one. Everyone pictures like a big flash apartment but it's tiny, honestly, smaller than my room here was. And the rent, don't get me started. What you'd pay here for the whole house a fortnight, doesn't even cover my half up there.

The eldest: Fair dinkum?

Me: Fair dinkum. And that's not even inner anything, that's out a fair way. Groceries too, everything's dearer. A servo pie up there'll set you back what a full brekkie costs here.

Mother: And people. Are they friendly? I always picture everyone up there with their head down, not looking at anyone.

Me: That's the thing, it's not a straight yes or no. Like nobody's rude exactly, but nobody's talking to you either, not like here where the bloke at the servo asks after your nan. You get on a train, dead silent, everyone on their phone. But then something goes wrong, someone drops something, whole carriage helps out, no dramas. Or your neighbours, you might not know their names for a year, but you leave your bin out and it's back in by arvo, someone's done it without asking. So. Friendly's not really the word. More like everyone keeps to themselves till they don't.

Uncle: What about getting things done then, shopping and that. Where do you actually buy your stuff.

Me: Big market not far from us, Saturdays mostly, fruit and veg heaps cheaper than the supermarket if you go right at the end when they're flogging it off. Butcher there does a good sausage. Otherwise it's just Coles like anywhere, or the little IGA on the corner for milk and ciggies for my housemate, he's trying to quit, he's not trying very hard.

Father: And work. How's that all sorted up there, is it like here, ring around, know a bloke?

Me: Bit of both, honestly. Everything's online first, you put in for a job, sometimes fifty other people going for the same one. But once you're in somewhere, it's who you know same as anywhere. My workmate got me my start actually, said the boss owed her one. After that it's just turning up, doing your bit, taking your smoko same as anyone. Chuck the odd sickie when you need it, not that I'd say that to Centrelink.

Aunty: Don't tell your mother that bit.

Mother: I heard that bit.

Me: (laughing) It's a joke, mostly.

The eldest: Did you get lost much, first getting there? I would've been hopeless.

Me: Oh mate, first few months, shocking. Got lost coming out the wrong exit at the station, ended up the complete opposite way to where I meant, three, four times easy. One time I walked forty minutes the wrong direction before I twigged. You just don't know which way's which when everything looks the same, all the little streets running off at angles. Took me a good while before it clicked, before I stopped needing the phone every five minutes. Now I could get youse round half the place blindfolded. Well. Half.

Uncle: And the food. Better or worse?

Me: Bit of both again. You can get anything, any night, any hour near enough, and it's good, proper good, not just the same three things here. But it costs. And I do miss a decent barbie with a real esky full of drinks, everyone out the back, that's harder to do up there, most people haven't even got a backyard. So swings and roundabouts. Better in some ways, worse in others, I reckon.

Father: Right, so, straight up then. Better or worse than here, all up.

Me: Ah, come on, that's not really a fair question, is it.

Mother: It's a very fair question.

Me: It's just different, isn't it. Different to here, not better or worse exactly. Some days I reckon here, easy, no contest. Other days I couldn't imagine coming back. Depends what day you catch me on.

Father: That's no answer at all.

Me: (laughing) I know. She'll be right though, don't worry about it.

Aunty: So. Are you ever coming back for good then, or is this it now?

Me: Dunno, honestly. Not saying never. I'm keen to see how it all pans out up there for a bit yet, see where the work goes, whether the rent does me in first. But I'm not saying I'm there forever either. Might be back here with youse in a couple years, might not. We'll see how we go.

Mother: That's not an answer either.

Me: Nah. Reckon it's the only honest one but. More tea?

Father: Whinge all you like about the rent, you're not getting out of doing the dishes while you're back.

Me: Wouldn't dream of it. G'day to the dishes then, I suppose.

The eldest: No worries, I'll dry.
