Someone always leans over at the barbie eventually and goes, "G'day, where's your mob from, then, originally," and that's how this one gets told, every single time, same as Nan told it to us growing up, and her father-in-law's father-in-law before that, so here it is again, more or less how I got it off her.

Where Pop's people came from was hill country, dry as anything most years, little stone villages stuck up the side of a mountain, and there was never enough land to go round. Four boys in a family and one bit of dirt to split between them, and by the time it got down to that generation there was next to nothing left to work with. No rain some years, no work most years, and a bloke could break his back on that ground his whole life and still owe the priest and the landlord both come harvest time. That's the short of it, not much use dwelling on it, it wasn't a story with a lot in it besides hard yakka and nothing to show for it after.

So the one who went first, that was the great-grandfather, and he went alone. Not entirely on his own, there was a cousin from the same village went out with him, near enough the same age, the two of them thick as thieves their whole lives up till then, but no wife, no kids, nothing but what he could carry on him, because there wasn't the money going spare to send more than the one man and see how he got on.

The crossing was the hard bit, and nobody in the family's ever told it soft. Weeks and weeks on the water, down in steerage where you couldn't hardly breathe some nights for the heat and the smell of it, and everyone crook as a dog the first fortnight from the swell. The great-grandfather used to tell Pop it was the closest thing to hell a living man could climb up out the other end of, and he wasn't one for exaggerating, that bloke, not from what anyone says of him.

The cousin didn't make it. Fever went round somewhere out past the equator and took him, and they buried him off the side of the ship, which was the way of it then if you didn't make it, no bringing anyone home. And here's the thing about it, the great-grandfather never once said his name. Not to Pop, not to anyone that we ever heard. He'd talk about the crossing, he'd talk about the fever going round the lower deck, but that part he kept shut up in himself.

Great-grandfather: Lost me cousin out there. Good bloke. Never made land.

That's as far as it ever went, every time. You'd ask who, and he'd go quiet, and nobody pushed it twice after the first one tried and saw what it did to him.

He landed here with next to nothing, a bag, the clothes on him, and about enough money to last a week if he was dead careful with it. No English to speak of, no work lined up, nothing waiting. First stretch here was rough as guts, no steady work at all, just whatever turned up, a day carting, a day on somebody's building job, plenty of days doing nothing because nothing was going. He didn't have a place of his own for the longest time either, boarded in with another family from back home who'd come out a few years ahead of him, slept on a fold-out in the front room of their place, paid what he could when he had it and copped it sweet when he didn't.

Great-grandfather: Sorry to put youse out like this.
The one who took him in: No dramas, mate. You'd do the same for us.

That's what you did for your own back then, no worries, no dramas, you looked after whoever turned up off the boat from the same hills as you, because next year it might be your lot needing the fold-out.

Took him the better part of a year before the work sorted itself into something steady, got taken on with a building mob doing the labouring, carrying hod for the brickies, and he was keen on it straight off, keen to learn the trade proper rather than haul bricks for somebody else his whole life. Took a while but he got there, got taken on as a brickie in his own right eventually, and that was it, that was the trade, and it stayed in the family from then on. Pop's father did his time on the tools, Pop did his time on the tools, and even now you'll find one of us on a site somewhere of a Monday.

Soon as he'd saved enough he sent for the rest of them, the great-grandmother and the kids they already had, back in the old place waiting on word from him. Took just over two years all up before the family was together again in one place, which doesn't sound like much said quick, but you ask Nan and she'll tell you two years apart with kids growing up hardly knowing their own father's face was no small thing, fair dinkum.

The day the ship came in with the rest of them aboard, that's the bit Pop always told with a laugh in his voice, because his own father, the eldest of that first lot of kids, didn't know the great-grandfather standing there on the wharf, hadn't laid eyes on him since he was a little tacker.

The eldest: Who's that bloke, then.
Great-grandmother: That's your father, love.
The eldest: Doesn't look like him.

And he didn't, not after a year of building work in this sun, brown as a boot and thinner than when he'd left, a different man to look at than the one they'd waved off. But she'll be right, as they say, and it sorted itself out soon enough once they were all under the one roof and the kids worked out who he was by the way their mother went quiet and let him hold them.

The great-grandmother thanked the family that had put him up all that time, standing there on the wharf still with her bags in her hands.

Great-grandmother: Thank you for keeping an eye on him for us.
The one who took him in: No worries, love. That's what you do.

There's one thing that came out on that first boat with him and it's still in the family to this day, a little copper pot, dented in on one side, the one his mother used for making coffee back in the village, and he carried it the whole way out wrapped in a shirt so it wouldn't get knocked about below decks. It sits on Nan's stove now, and she still makes the coffee in it of a morning, same as her mother-in-law did before her, same as whoever it was before that again.

Within a few years of the family being whole again they'd found a little place of their own, nothing flash, weatherboard, needed heaps doing to it, mozzies shocking down that end of the place in summer, but it was theirs, first roof over their own heads since they'd left the hills. And that's the house the rest of the kids came along in, Pop among them, born right there. That's the bit Nan always reckons is the real turn of the whole story, not the boat, not the wharf, not even the pot, it's that the kids born after that were Australian from the first breath, no other place in their memory to miss, and that's what made it home rather than just somewhere they'd ended up.

These days when the young ones round here whinge about how dodgy it is trying to get anywhere with Centrelink, or sitting on hold about the PBS or the HCC over something simple, Nan just laughs and says they don't know they're born. Her father-in-law didn't have two bob and a fold-out bed in someone else's front room, and he built a trade and a house and a family out of it inside a decade, rolling his own ciggies out the back of an evening because that was the one thing he let himself have, never chucked a sickie in his life that anyone heard of, six days a week and Sunday brekkie was the only rest going. Whatever's hard now, she reckons, she'll be right, mate, it always is in the end, different to what he had it, but nothing you can't get through.

And that's the story, told the way I got it off her, sitting out the back of an arvo with the esky open and the thongs kicked off, same as she got it off him, same as I'll tell it again to whoever leans over the fence next barbie and asks where we're from, because heaps of families round this place have got one just like it, just with a different hill and a different boat under them, and it's worth keeping straight, the whole thing, cousin and pot and all, so none of it goes the way so much else did on that crossing, over the side and never spoken again.
