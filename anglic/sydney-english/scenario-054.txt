Right, so you weren't around for any of this, so let me take you through the whole saga, start to finish, cos it was a proper eleven months of it and I reckon I aged about five years doing it.

Basically we'd just outgrown the place. Two kids, then the eldest wanting her own room instead of bunking in with the little one, and the missus doing half her work from the kitchen table with her laptop balanced on the fruit bowl. We were tripping over each other. So we sat down one Sunday arvo with a cuppa and a bit of butcher's paper and worked out what we reckoned it'd take.

Me: I reckon two of us, maybe three if my brother-in-law's keen, four months tops. Slab, frame, roof, lock-up, then fit-out around us while we're still living in it.

The missus: Four months. Sure. You said the deck was going to take a fortnight and that took two months.

Me: This is different to the deck.

She wasn't wrong to give me that look, cos four months turned into eleven, and it wasn't two of us, it ended up six different people had a hand in it one way or another by the time we were done.

First stuff-up came early, and it was a shocker. We'd set the footings out, string lines up, all looking dead straight to the eye, and it wasn't till the chippy came round to have a squiz before we poured that he picked it. Whole thing was out of square by about forty mil over the run, which doesn't sound like heaps till you're trying to frame a room off it and nothing lines up. Two weeks of digging and formwork, gone, had to pull it all up and start again. I was filthy about it, properly whinging to anyone who'd listen, but the old fella down the road just said she'll be right, mate, better now than after the pour, and he wasn't wrong.

Then there was the Saturday the extra hands turned up and nobody asked them to. I'm out there on me own at seven in the morning trying to wrangle a stud into place by myself, swearing at it, and I hear a ute pull up. It's my mate and his brother, esky in the tray, tools in the back, no text, no phone call, nothing.

My mate: G'day. Reckoned you'd be under the pump by now. We're keen to help if you'll have us.

Me: Mate, you're a legend, I could kiss you.

My mate: Don't. Just get the kettle on and point us at something.

Put in a full day, the three of us, got more done in that one day than I had in the whole previous week on my own. Never did work out how he knew, reckon my missus rang his missus, but I never asked too hard in case the answer ruined it.

Weather had its say too. Best part of a week where it just would not stop, proper Sydney summer storm carry-on, the kind where the tarps go everywhere and you're out there at midnight in your thongs trying to peg down a corner that's flapping like a sail. Nothing to be done but sit it out. Six days, dead loss, watching the puddles form where the slab was meant to stay dry, drinking too much instant coffee and going a bit stir crazy.

Ran short of timber about halfway through as well, framing timber specifically, cos I'd underordered thinking I was being careful with the budget. Nearest supplier didn't have the lengths I needed for another eight days, and I wasn't about to sit around, so we made do with what was in the shed and what we could scab together off site, doubled up some shorter lengths where we could get away with it structurally, the chippy signed off on it, said it wasn't ideal but it'd hold.

The chippy: It's not textbook but she'll be right. Just don't go telling the building inspector I said that.

Had a scare up on the roof too, gave everyone a fright. I was up there fixing battens, lost my footing on the sarking, went down the pitch and off the edge, landed in the garden bed we hadn't dug out yet, thank god, cos six months later that spot was concrete. Copped nothing worse than a corked hip and a bruise the size of a dinner plate and a good telling off from the missus.

The missus: You are not going up there again without someone holding the ladder, I don't care how keen you are to finish.

Me: I'm fine, it's just a scare.

The missus: You're fine this time.

Had a decent blue with the brother-in-law over the verandah roof, whether to pitch it to match the main roof or run it flat off the wall plate. Went back and forth for a good hour, both of us dead set on our own way.

Brother-in-law: Mate, flat off the wall plate is half the work and it'll look fine.

Me: It'll look bodgy, that's what it'll look like. Do it properly or don't bother.

Brother-in-law: You're being a bogan about it, it's a verandah, not the Opera House.

We got there in the end, matched the pitch, and he'll admit now it looks better, though it took him a good few months to say it out loud.

Worst blow was the back wall. Had it framed, clad, all looking the goods, and then the sparky came through to run the wiring and found the whole thing was sitting proud of the slab edge by a fair bit, out of true from top to bottom. No fixing that one with packers and hope. Had to strip it right back to the frame and rebuild the whole wall from scratch. That was a low point, not gonna lie, standing there looking at a pile of stud offcuts thinking about the weeks we'd just un-done.

But the day the frame finally stood up straight, whole thing braced and square and looking like an actual room instead of a pile of timber, that was a proper good day. Nothing flash, just the lot of us standing back with a cold one out of the esky having a look at it.

Neighbour: That's coming up a treat, that is.

Me: Took its bloody time but yeah, not bad.

Last stretch, the final four nights, we were out there well past dark with the work lights strung off an extension cord, trying to get it over the line. Cold as anything, mozzies having a go at us under the lights, everyone knackered, but nobody wanted to be the one who called it a night with the finish line that close.

And then it just was finished. Stood there one evening, tools packed away, nothing left to do, and it felt properly strange, like the house had been holding its breath the whole eleven months and now it could let it out.

Tallied it up after. Eleven months start to finish, near enough three times what I'd reckoned at the start, and six different people had a hand in it along the way, counting the chippy and the sparky and the two of us and the neighbour who ended up roped in for a weekend and my brother-in-law who never left really. Six people, eleven months, one wall built twice, one fall that could've been a lot worse than it was.

Standing in it now though, of a night, kettle on, kids in their own rooms finally, it's hard to remember the whinging. You forget the two weeks lost at the start and the week the rain had us beat and the argument about the verandah. What you remember is that it's yours, properly yours, every stud and sheet of it, and it holds up fine.
