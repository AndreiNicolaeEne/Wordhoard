God, what a day. Sit down, I'll tell you the whole thing, because you would not believe it.

So you know the big old fig that hangs over the lane out the back, the one that's been there longer than any of the houses. Storm the other night took half of it down, right across the ROW, from her back gate almost to ours. Not a job you fix with a bloke and a bow saw of a Sunday arvo, this was proper heavy timber, whole limbs thick as a drum, and it had taken out a good stretch of the paling fence on the way down and was sitting there blocking every single gate up that lane. Nobody's car was getting out, nobody's bin was getting collected, nothing. Too big for one household, no way round it.

It was the one on the corner who saw it first, out with the dog at some ungodly hour, and she just started banging on doors. By half seven it was all through the street group chat too, up and down both sides, and I swear word travelled faster than the storm did. We were only expecting the usual suspects to turn out, five or six of us who always end up doing this sort of thing, and by nine there must've been twenty-odd people standing in that lane. Heaps more than anyone reckoned on. People who I don't think had said two words to each other in years, all standing round in their ugg boots and trackies with a cuppa, looking at this tree like it owed them money.

Somebody took charge pretty quick, sorting who did what, because otherwise it's just twenty people standing round having a whinge about the state of it. The young blokes ended up hauling the smaller branches down to the trailers, a couple of the women were on gutter and rubbish, and the bloke from the corner house, the one who does tree work for a crust, he took the chainsaw and basically didn't put it down all day. Fair dinkum, that was the heaviest yakka of the lot, cutting that trunk down into manageable lengths in that heat, and nobody envied him it, though there were a few offers of a cold one every time he stopped for a breather.

Course it wasn't all smooth. Round about eleven, dragging one of the bigger limbs clear with a rope off the back of the ute, it swung wide on us and took the head clean off next door's letterbox, the new one and all, brick post and everything, she'd only had it done a fortnight before. Bit of a moment there, everyone going quiet waiting to see how she'd take it, but she just laughed and said it was dodgy as anyway, some cousin's mate put it in cheap.

Then there was the blue over what to do with all the wood once it was down. Bloke with the chainsaw wanted it cut into rounds and split, said it'd season up nice for winter and someone could use it.

The one with the chainsaw: Nah, we're not chipping good hardwood like that, it's a waste, someone'll want it for the fire.

Her husband from up the other end: Mate, nobody round here's got a fireplace, we're all gas heating. Chip it and be done, or it's sitting in somebody's yard till Christmas.

The one with the chainsaw: It is not sitting in anyone's yard, I'll split it meself and take what's left.

Her husband: Yeah, righto, and who's carting six trailer loads to your place then.

Went back and forth like that for a good ten minutes, voices going up, other people chiming in from both sides of the argument, until the old girl who runs the corner shop just said, right, half gets chipped for the garden beds and half gets split, everyone happy, and that was that. She'll be right. Nobody argued with her twice.

Food kept turning up all day without anyone organising it, which is always the way. Somebody had an esky out on the footpath by ten with a slab in it and cordial for the kids, another house brought out a whole urn and a plate of Anzac bikkies, and come lunch the ones on the corner just fired up their barbie right there in the lane and did a couple of kilos of snags, onions, the works, everyone just wandering over with a piece of bread when they were ready. Someone's mum turned up mid arvo with a fruit cake like it was a wake, which got a few laughs.

The kids got put onto the kindling, gathering up all the little twigs and offcuts into a pile by the gate so nobody was tripping on it, and taking it dead serious, made a proper competition out of whose pile was biggest. And the old fella from the end house, must be well into his eighties, his wife tried to get him to sit in the shade with a cuppa and just watch, and he was having none of it. Grabbed a rake and did the whole gutter length of the lane himself, wouldn't be talked out of it, kept saying he wasn't going to sit there like an ornament while everyone else worked. Fair play to him, he outlasted a couple of the younger ones.

Best bit of the whole day though, when they finally cut into one of the hollow limbs near the top, this possum comes flying out of it in an absolute panic, straight across the lane, over the fence, and in through somebody's back door which some genius had left wide open. Screaming from the kitchen, someone's teenager legging it out the front door in his socks, the old fella nearly falling off his rake laughing, and then this poor possum eventually finding its own way back out a window and up into the next yard's tree like nothing happened. Every single person in that lane was in stitches, kids included, and I reckon it got brought up about six more times through the rest of the day.

We all reckoned we'd have that whole lane cleared and cleaned up by early arvo. Ended up finishing a good three hours later than that, must've been getting on for half five, six even, before the last trailer went off to the tip. But by the end of it the whole lane was clear, both loads of timber gone, a full trailer of mulch spread through people's garden beds, the fence patched enough to hold till the weekend, and even the letterbox back up, held together with duct tape and someone's bricklaying skills for now. When the last ute pulled out someone gave a shout and the whole lane just erupted, clapping and cheering like we'd won something.

And after, nobody wanted to just go back inside. People were still out there with a beer as it was getting dark, kids belting a tennis ball round the lane now it was clear, folks talking who I don't reckon had exchanged a word in the whole time I've lived there. Only lasted the one evening, I know that, by tomorrow everyone's back to their own front doors. But for a few hours there the whole street felt like it belonged to itself again.
