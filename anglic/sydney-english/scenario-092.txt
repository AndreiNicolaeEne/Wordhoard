By the time it got properly late the house had thinned right down, just the five of them left at the kitchen table, everyone else gone home with the last of the party pies wrapped in glad wrap under their arms. The kettle had been boiled that many times it wasn't even worth switching off at the wall. Someone's tea had gone cold twice and nobody had noticed enough to make a fresh one.

The neighbour: "I keep thinking about him out the front on a Saturday arvo, hosing down that driveway like it owed him money."

The mother: "Sunday. It was always a Sunday, that driveway carry-on."

The neighbour: "Was it? See, I always say Saturday, don't I. I don't know why, I've had it wrong in my head for years and nobody's ever pulled me up on it before."

The mother laughed properly then, the first real laugh of the night, and put her hand over her mouth like she hadn't meant it to come out that loud.

The mother: "No, you're right to say it, love. It's just I remember 'cause I'd be trying to get him to come in for brekkie and he wouldn't budge till that driveway was done, dirty or not."

The youngest let out a laugh at that too, sudden, a proper snort, and looked a bit embarrassed about it straight after, like he'd caught himself somewhere he shouldn't have been.

The youngest: "Sorry. Didn't mean to laugh like that."

The eldest: "No worries, mate, laugh away. He'd be rapt someone was still cracking up about him."

The mate: "He had that saying though, didn't he. Every time you rang him for anything, didn't matter what it was, car wouldn't start, footy team lost, whatever. Same four words."

The mother: "Go on then."

The mate, doing the voice, flat and unbothered: "She'll be right."

Everyone sort of half laughed and half went quiet at the same time, because it landed the way it was meant to, exactly the tone he used to say it in, no drama in it at all, like the words themselves had turned up in the room for a second.

The eldest: "He said that to me the week before, actually. About something nobody else even knew about."

The mother: "What, love?"

The eldest: "I'd rung him about the job falling through, the one I didn't get, and I was that upset about it, and he goes, nah, she'll be right, you weren't keen on that mob anyway. Said he never trusted the fella that interviewed me. Never told any of youse that."

The mother: "He never said a word to me about that."

The mate: "Startled" was about right for the look on the mother's face, mouth open a second before anything came out of it.

The mother: "Well. Fair dinkum. That's news to me."

It went quiet then for a moment, properly quiet, just the fridge humming and someone's chair creaking.

The youngest broke it by getting up and pushing the plate of sandwiches back into the middle of the table, and the esky lid banging as he fished out another round of drinks for whoever wanted one.

The youngest: "Anyone actually eating, or are we just moving food round the table for something to do?"

The eldest: "Bit of both, I reckon."

The mother: "Pass us one of them, love, go on."

The eldest and the youngest had a small go at each other then, over nothing much, whether the good glasses had ever come back from the eldest's place after Christmas two years back, an old back-and-forth that had clearly done a hundred laps before tonight, but neither of them pushed it, and it dropped again as quick as it came up, nobody wanting that particular fight in the room this week of all weeks.

The mate: "You want stubborn, though. Tell youse about the ute."

The mother: "Oh, don't start on the ute."

The mate: "Nah, nah, this is a good one. Dodgy old thing, wouldn't start half the time, mozzies could've carried it faster than the engine could. I must've told him fifty times, mate, get rid of it, get something with a bit of rego life left in it. He wouldn't have a bar of it."

The neighbour: "Wouldn't even let anyone else drive it."

The mate: "Wouldn't let anyone else drive it, wouldn't sell it, wouldn't scrap it, dug his heels in over a heap of rust that wasn't worth two hundred bucks. I offered to buy the thing off him just to put it out of its misery and he looked at me like I'd insulted his mother. Kept that ute longer than some people keep a marriage."

That got the real laugh going round the table, warm, no edge left in it at all, just fondness for a stubborn old bugger and his stubborn old ute.

Nobody said it outright, but it sat there in the room anyway after that, plain as anything, that if you asked any one of them the one thing to remember him by, they'd all land on the same word without even having to think about it.

The youngest had gone quiet during the ute story, and when the next round of talk started up, about the barbie he used to insist on doing even in the rain, a couple of tears were going down his face, quiet, no sound to it. Nobody stopped what they were saying. The eldest just reached over and put a hand on his arm for a second, kept talking the whole time, and the conversation kept moving gently round him rather than landing on him, which seemed to be what he wanted.

After a while it turned practical, the way it does.

The mother: "Right, someone's going to have to ring Centrelink Monday, let them know, and I've got to sort out the PBS scripts, cancel all that."

The eldest: "I'll do Centrelink. You want me to ring round the rest of the family too, or you doing that?"

The mother: "I'll do the family. You do Centrelink and the pension mob."

The neighbour: "I can do a run to the shops for whatever's needed for after, flowers and that, don't youse worry about it."

The mate: "I'll deal with the car, get it looked at, sort the rego out, whatever needs doing there."

The youngest, wiping his face with the back of his hand, half laughing through it: "I'll do the ute. Reckon it deserves someone who actually liked the bloody thing."

It was plain talk, no ceremony to any of it, just who was doing what, and somehow that settled everyone more than anything said before it had.

It was the mate left awake last, everyone else gone quiet or half asleep in their chairs, and he lifted what was left in his glass, not much in it, and didn't have anything worked out to say.

The mate: "To a good bloke who never once in his life admitted he was wrong about anything. She'll be right, mate. Always was."

And that was it, glasses knocking together soft across the table, and the house went quiet properly then, the kind of quiet that comes after everything that needed saying has finally been said.
