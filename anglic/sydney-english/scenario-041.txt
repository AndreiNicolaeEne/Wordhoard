Righto, I'll tell you the whole thing, start to finish, because youse have only heard the short version and the short version's a lie by omission if nothing else.

Two years I was there. Two years almost to the week. And honestly the first year and a bit was fine, more than fine, I used to tell people I was keen on the place, keen on the team, the whole deal. It's the back stretch that did me in, the last six months where every little thing started stacking up like washing you keep saying you'll get to.

The final straw was nothing, mate, that's the thing you've got to understand about final straws. It wasn't a blow-up, nobody yelled, nobody got sacked, nothing dramatic enough to make a decent story out of on its own. It was the roster. They changed the roster on a Friday arvo, sent it round in an email with no heads up, and I was down for a shift I'd already told them, weeks before, I couldn't do on account of a family thing. Small stuff. But it wasn't about that email. It was every email like it going back eighteen months, every time I'd raised something and got the same line back, we hear you, we'll look into it, and nothing ever moved. That Friday I just sat there staring at the screen and thought, right, that's it, I'm done, and it felt less like a decision and more like something that had already happened and I was just catching up to it.

So I spent the weekend rehearsing. Fair dinkum, in the shower, driving to the servo, wherever, running the lines in my head over and over till they sounded natural, which is the whole trick of it isn't it, making the rehearsed thing sound like it's not rehearsed. I wanted to be careful. Diplomatic. Not burn anything, because Sydney's small when it comes to industries, everyone knows someone, and I didn't want to be the one who stormed out and got talked about.

Monday morning I went and asked to see whoever was in charge.

Me: Have you got a minute later on, when you're free? Nothing urgent.

The boss: Yeah, no worries, come find me after the ten o'clock, we'll grab a room.

That "nothing urgent" was doing heaps of work in that sentence, let me tell you. Wasn't urgent to them. Was about as urgent as anything's ever been to me.

We sat down that arvo in one of them little glass meeting rooms, and I said my opening line, the one I'd polished all weekend.

Me: Thanks for making time. Look, I wanted to have a chat because I've decided to move on. It's not about any one thing in particular, it's just time for a change, I reckon.

Which was true and it wasn't, both at once. It partly was about one thing, or one long thread of things anyway, but I said "not about any one thing" because the real reason I held back, right there in that moment, was I didn't want it to sound like a complaint. I didn't want to be the person crying about a roster in an exit conversation, didn't want that to be the takeaway, didn't want a reference letter down the track with an asterisk on it in someone's memory. So I made it sound like ambition instead of exhaustion. Cleaner that way. Safer.

The boss took it pretty well, all things considered, a bit surprised though, eyebrows up, that thing people do where they lean back in the chair like the news needs more room.

The boss: Oh, right, okay. Didn't see that coming, if I'm honest. Is there anything we can do? We could look at a pay bump, or if it's the hours, we could talk about shifting your days round a bit.

And see, that's the bit that nearly got me, because two years ago that offer would've had me. But I said no, and I gave the polite reason, not the true one.

Me: Nah, it's not really about the money, it's just personal timing, I think I need a proper change of scene.

The true reason was I didn't trust that anything offered under pressure in that room would actually still be there in six weeks. I'd watched that happen to a workmate of mine already, given a whole new arrangement to stay, and within the month it had quietly drifted back to exactly how it was before, and nobody ever officially walked it back, it just sort of evaporated. I wasn't keen to be the second bloke that happened to.

We agreed on the standard, two weeks' notice, and I remember walking out of that room feeling dead calm, almost proud of how smooth it'd gone. And then about an hour later, sitting back at my desk, this doubt just landed on me out of nowhere. What if I'd done the wrong thing. What if the next place was worse, or dodgy in ways I couldn't see yet, and I'd just walked away from something that was, when you added it all up, still fine. Heaps of people would've killed for that job and here's me chucking it in over a roster email. I sat there half convinced I'd made a shocker of a call.

The fortnight went quick. Everyone was decent about it, in that surface way people are decent when someone's leaving, lots of "we'll miss you" and "keep in touch" that you both know is mostly air. Last day, they got a cake, someone said a few words, the usual. Goodbyes that were mostly hollow, going through the motions, shake the hand, good luck out there, don't be a stranger.

But there was one moment. Right at the end, packing the last of my stuff into a box, my workmate, the one who'd had the whole reshuffle thing happen to her, came over and just stood there a sec.

The workmate: You right?

Me: Yeah. Yeah, good. Bit weird, but good.

The workmate: For what it's worth, I don't blame you. I probably should've gone when youse first started talking about it.

And that was the closest either of us got to saying the real thing, that the place wears you down in ways that don't show up on any one day, that we'd both had enough a long time before either of us admitted it out loud. Neither of us said it plain. We just sort of let that sentence sit there and then I said something about keeping in touch and that was that.

It's been a while now since, and if you're asking how it's felt, mate, it's relief, no contest, heaps more relief than regret. I still get a little pang some days, wonder how things are going there, whether the roster's still a shambles, whether anyone else has cracked it and left. But it doesn't sit heavy. She'll be right, as it turns out, mostly was.

If I had that conversation to have again, I reckon I'd say less of the polished version and more of the true one. Not to burn the place down, nothing like that, just. I spent a whole weekend rehearsing how to be diplomatic about something that didn't owe anyone diplomacy, and looking back I don't know that it bought me much. They forgot the polite version by the next intake anyway. I'm the one who's had to carry the real one around ever since.
