Righto, this was my first full year on the plot down the community garden, so I reckon it's worth writing the whole thing down while I remember it, the good and the dodgy.

Started thinking about what to put in back in late winter really, sitting there of an evening going through the seed packets I'd been hoarding. Ended up keen on tomatoes, a few rows of beans up a trellis, some zucchini because you can't kill the things, a patch of corn just to see if it'd take, and a couple of pumpkins down the far end where I had the room. The woman on the plot behind mine reckoned I was heaps ambitious for a first year but I said nah, she'll be right, worst case something doesn't come up and I try again next spring.

Didn't put anything in the ground till a few weeks after the last of the cold had properly gone though, learned that off the old bloke who's had his plot since God was a boy, he said youse always get some galah putting seedlings in too early and then a late frost comes through and cooks the lot. So I waited, put the seedlings in early spring once it'd well and truly warmed up, and fair dinkum we got a run of just beautiful weather straight after, mild days, decent rain every few days without it being a flood, everything shot up like it was keen to be there. Best start I could've asked for.

First bit of trouble was the cabbage moth on the, well, I didn't even have cabbages but they went for the zucchini leaves and the pumpkin too, little caterpillars chewing lace out of everything. Tried the netting first, that helped a bit, and then a mate at the garden told me to do a garlic and chilli spray, boil it up and spritz the leaves of an arvo. Did that religiously for a fortnight. Only sort of worked to be honest, knocked the numbers back but never got rid of them proper, I was still picking caterpillars off by hand right through into summer. Bit of a whinge but you live with it.

Then came the dry stretch mid-season, no decent rain for what felt like six weeks straight, and the tank levels dropped right off. That was heaps of work, I was down there most days after work with the watering cans, and on the real stinkers I'd go twice, morning and arvo both, just to keep the tomatoes from wilting flat. Thongs off, boots on, hauling water up and down the rows for weeks on end. You do get sick of it but what else are you gonna do, let the whole thing die off after all that.

That's when the argument started with the bloke on the plot next to mine, over whose turn it was on the tap. There's meant to be a roster stuck up on the shed but half the time it goes walkabout, and during the dry spell everyone's cactus for water at the same time so tempers get a bit short.

The neighbour: You had it yesterday arvo, it's not your turn again till Thursday.

Me: Mate, my turn was rained out Tuesday, I never got a go, that's why I'm here now.

The neighbour: That's not how it works though, you don't get to carry it over.

Me: Well nobody told me that, and me tomatoes are looking pretty ordinary as it is.

Went back and forth a bit like that for a couple of weeks, both of us having a bit of a whinge to anyone who'd listen. In the end it was the committee fella who sorted it, said from now on the roster gets photocopied and everyone keeps their own copy pinned up on their own plot, and if it rains and you miss your slot you just slot into the next gap instead of losing it altogether. Fair enough really, and once that was settled the neighbour and I were sweet, he even gave us a hand rigging up a bit of shade cloth over my seedling trays not long after. No dramas in the end.

Best producer by an absolute mile was the tomatoes. Once they got going properly they did not stop, I was giving buckets of them away to anyone who'd take them, the whole garden probably sick of the sight of me turning up with another bagful. Clear win, no argument there.

Against that though the corn copped it from the birds. Cockatoos mostly, the big white ones, they'd work out exactly when a cob was ready before I even had, strip the husk right back and have a go, leave the rest hanging there half eaten and useless. Tried flash tape, tried an old CD on a string, tried covering the heads with paper bags which actually helped a bit but was a proper pain doing forty-odd cobs one at a time. Lost a decent chunk of the crop to them regardless, that's just how it goes with corn apparently, everyone down there says the same.

Then partway through summer we copped that big storm, proper wind and hail together, and it flattened a good three rows, the bean trellis went right over and took half the corn down with it, stalks all lying sideways in the mud. Turned up the next morning half expecting to just give it up as a loss but ended up spending the whole weekend out there, restaking everything, tying the beans back up, propping the corn that wasn't snapped clean through. Heaps of work for two days straight, back was killing me by Sunday arvo, but most of it came good again, only lost what had actually snapped off at the base.

Come the end of the season I sat down and had a proper tally of it all. Tomatoes were the standout like I said, absolute glut. Zucchini did well too once the moth settled down, more than I could ever eat myself. Beans were solid, corn was patchy thanks to the cockies and the storm both, and the pumpkins came good late, four decent ones off two vines which I was rapt with. Traded a couple of the pumpkins and a big bag of the tomato glut to the bloke two plots over for a load of his mulch and some seedling trays I needed for next year, worked out well for both of us, he's not fussed on tomatoes but goes through mulch like nobody's business.

Pulled the last of it out end of autumn, cleared the beds, dug through a heap of compost and put a cover crop of broad beans in over winter just to feed the soil back up while it rests. Plot's all put to bed now, tools cleaned and put away in the shed, netting rolled up for next time.

Looking back on the whole year of it, reckon it was a ripper first go even with the caterpillars and the cockies and the blue with the neighbour. Learn something every season down there apparently, and I reckon that's about right. She'll be right for spring, I'm already keen to have another crack at it.
