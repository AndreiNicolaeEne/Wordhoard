Alarm went off stupid early, still pitch black out, and the mother's already bashing round the kitchen with the kettle going and the radio mumbling away to itself. Brekkie was toast standing up, no one sitting down for it, cause once the sun's up on a harvest day you've lost the cool part of it and that's the bit you actually want.

The father come through pulling his boots on and just said, right, everyone's got a job, don't stand round like a shag on a rock. The youngest one, still half asleep, got put on the esky run, ferrying the water bottles and the cordial down to the end of the rows and lining them up in the shade of the ute so nobody's dying of thirst by nine. Easy job but it matters, cause you forget how quick you go from fine to gasping out there.

The eldest cousin, built like a brick dunny, got put straight onto the heavy end, hauling the crates. Every full crate off the ground and up onto the trailer, one after another, and nobody else was going to do that for six hours straight without their back giving out.

The father: right, you're on crates, don't let em stack up down the far end or we're carrying double later.

The eldest: yeah yeah, I got it, no dramas.

So off everyone went down the rows, bent over, hands going, and the first stretch was the good bit, still cool, everyone had their rhythm, nobody talking much, just the sound of stuff going into buckets and the odd magpie carrying on up in the gums.

Once that first section was done and dusted the whole lot of us shifted down the paddock to the next bit, and the jobs swapped round a bit too, the ones who'd been picking went onto sorting, chucking out the dodgy ones, the split or the mozzie-bitten, and a couple of the others took over the picking.

Somewhere in there my friend, who's not exactly built for this piece, started dropping back, whole rows behind, sweating buckets and not saying a word about it, too proud to whinge. Nobody made a big deal, the mother just drifted down that way with her own bucket and started working the same row from the other end, quiet, so it looked like they finished together, and nothing ever got said about it. That's just how it goes, you don't call it out, you just fill the gap.

Round about mid morning the smell of something good started drifting down from the house and the grandmother come out across the paddock with a big tray, snags and bread and a slab of fruit cake, and everyone downed tools quick smart and sat wherever, on crates, on the tailgate, in the dirt, didn't matter. Ten minutes, maybe fifteen, everyone talking at once.

The workmate reckoned the way the eldest was stacking crates was going to end in someone's back going out, said he was going too hard too early and he'd be stuffed by two.

The workmate: mate, you're gonna carrion out by lunch if you keep going like that, pace yourself.

The eldest: nah, she'll be right, I'm heaps fit, don't you worry about me.

The workmate: fair enough, your funeral.

Bit of a laugh over that, someone else chucked in that the eldest reckons that every year and every year he's the first one asking for a sit down, and the eldest copped it good, laughing along, no argument really, just the usual stir.

Bout half past eleven the sky started doing that thing where it goes that funny greyish colour off to the west and the wind picked up, and the father was straight up scanning it like he does, and called it, said forget the far paddock for now, we're moving everyone onto the low block, get that in before it hits, worry about the rest after if it clears.

So the whole lot of us shifted down, quick smart, and it held off longer than anyone reckoned, just a few spits of rain that didn't come to anything, and by half one it'd actually cleared right up and gone properly hot, that sticky after-rain heat, and everyone was flat out again, heads down, cause you'd lost time and you knew it.

Round about two the header, the little tractor rig they run down the rows for the heavier stuff, just cut out dead in the middle of a row, wouldn't turn over, nothing. The father under the bonnet swearing at it, the eldest trying to help, couple of false starts, and for about twenty minutes it looked like that whole section was going to sit there uncut. In the end they just left it where it died and everyone piled in by hand, doing that whole stretch the old way, bent over, filling buckets and hauling them up to the trailer themselves, and it was slower but it got done, nobody stood round waiting on a machine that wasn't playing ball.

The father: leave the bloody thing, we'll sort it tonight, just get this lot in by hand, come on.

By the time the light started going that late golden colour and dropping fast, the father come down the rows calling out to pick it up, said we've got maybe an hour of proper light left and he wanted the whole lot in before dark, not half in, all in, so everyone shifted up a gear, less talking, just going for it, buckets getting handed along a line instead of everyone carrying their own, quicker that way.

Last section, everyone ended up converging on it together without anyone really saying to, the youngest even got pulled off esky duty to help finish the last few rows, everyone shoulder to shoulder near enough, and it come together right as the light properly started dropping off, that last crate going up onto the trailer with everyone standing round it like it was something.

The father did the tally standing on the back of the ute, counting crates out loud, and reckoned it was the best day they'd had in a few years, more than double what got done the year before on the same stretch, and everyone cheered a bit, more from being knackered than anything.

Soon as the last crate went on, half the mob just dropped, right there in the dirt, backs against the tyres, not talking, just breathing, someone's arms shaking from holding buckets all day. The mother brought the esky over one last time and there wasn't a bottle of water left that wasn't warm as a cuppa, and nobody cared.

And right when everyone reckoned that was it, done, the father looked over at the eldest cousin, still sprawled out half dead, and said, oi, before you sit down proper, go chuck a tarp over them crates, don't need the dew getting into them overnight.

The eldest groaned like he'd been asked to run a marathon, but he got up and did it, dragging his feet the whole way, and only after that did anyone actually let themselves collapse properly, into the dirt, into the ute seats, wherever, done for the day, fair dinkum done.
