Okay so this the year I really committed to that garden plot, for real for real. Ion know why I ain't start years ago because mama'n'em been telling me for years I need something to do with my hands that ain't a phone. I signed up early in the year, put my name on the list at the community garden off the east side, paid the little fee, and then I just sat there the rest of that month planning like a fool. Graph paper and everything.

Early spring I'm going back and forth about what to plant. First I'm finna do all tomatoes because errbody know tomatoes is the flex of a garden, you tell somebody you grew tomatoes and they be like oh okay. But then I'm thinking about what we actually eat, what mama'n'em be asking for when I come through, and it's collards, it's peppers, it's some squash, some okra, because it's Atlanta and if you ain't growing okra what you even doing. So I ended up with a mix, collards and mustards on one end, tomatoes and peppers in the middle, squash and okra on the other end, and I threw some marigolds round the border because the woman two plots over swear up and down they keep the bugs off.

I put everything in the ground a few weeks after the last cold snap, like you supposed to, ain't want to rush it and lose a whole flat of seedlings to one late frost like happened to me a couple years back when I was just doing containers on the porch. And once it went in the ground, Lord, the weather did its thing. We had a good long run of that sun, not too hot yet, rain coming through every few days right on time like it knew what it was doing. Everything came up strong. The collards was out here looking like they was tryna take over the whole bed, tomatoes shot up the cage before I even got round to tryna tie them up good.

Then the pests found the collards, which, of course. It's always the collards. I'm out there one morning and it's holes all in the leaves, little green worms curled up on the underside just chilling like they pay rent. Cabbage worms, somebody told me, though half the garden got a different name for the same worm. I tried the soap spray first, the dish soap and water thing everybody swear by, and it helped some, thinned them out, but it ain't handle it all the way. Kept finding a few more every few days no matter how much I sprayed. Eventually I just started picking them off by hand in the morning before work, which ain't nobody got time for but you do what you got to do when it's your collards on the line.

Then partway through the season it just stopped raining altogether. Ain't rain for what felt like three, four weeks straight. The ground cracking, hard as concrete in spots. I'm out there every single evening after work hauling water in buckets and jugs because the hose situation at the garden is a whole thing I'll get to, and I'm talking about hauling water every day, not every other day, every day, because in that heat if you skip one day you come back and something done wilted flat to the ground.

That's when the whole situation with the water spot popped off. See it's one spigot that feed about six plots, and it's supposed to be a rotation, everybody get they twenty minutes or whatever and you move on. But it's this one plot-holder, stay right next to me, and he be hooking his hose up whenever he feel like it, don't matter whose turn it is. I come after work one day, hose already running on his side, ain't nobody in sight, been running who knows how long, and him nowhere around.

Me: Excuse me, I thought it was my turn on the water this evening.

The neighbor: Oh my bad, I was finna move it in a second.

Me: You said that yesterday too though.

The neighbor: Man it's a lot of people up in here trying to use one spigot, I ain't got time to be watching no clock.

Me: I ain't trying to argue with you every day about this, we just need to work something out.

That went on near about two weeks, him talmbout he forgot, talmbout he ain't hear the timer, little back and forth every time, me having to come check the spigot before I even bring my buckets out because I don't know is it gon be free or not. Finally the garden coordinator got involved after a couple other plot-holders started complaining too, not just me.

The coordinator: Look, what all we gon do is put a sign-up sheet right here by the spigot, everybody write they name and they time, thirty minutes each, and if you go over somebody gon be checking.

That fixed it mostly. The neighbor still tried to run over his time here and there but at least now it's something on paper, ain't just whoever get there first talking about it's they turn.

Now the tomatoes, the tomatoes was the story of the whole year, no debate. Once they got going ain't nothing stop them. I was pulling tomatoes off there every two, three days by then, more than I could even use myself, giving them to mama'n'em, giving them to whoever walking by the plot looking hopeful. Best producer by a landslide, hands down, no other crop even close.

Meanwhile the squash was catching it from the other direction. First it was the vine borers I believe, then once a few of them split or went soft on the vine the rot set in behind that, and it just went downhill fast. One week it's five, six good squash coming off there a day, next week it's mushy at the base and the whole vine looking sad. I pulled what I could save and just let the rest go, ain't no saving squash once the rot get up in it like that.

Then a storm came through partway into the season, wind was wild, I ain't never seen it lay a garden down like that. I came the next morning and it's like four rows just flat, stalks bent all the way over, the cages tipped, okra plants laying sideways in the mud. Took me a whole day staking everything back up, tying it, propping what I could, cutting loose what wasn't coming back. Lost a good bit of the peppers to that storm, they ain't handle getting knocked down too good, but the collards, believe it or not, they bounced right back up like it wasn't nothing. Collards built different, I'm telling you, raggedy as they was after, still hanging in there.

By the time it come round to tallying everything up at the end of the season I had way more than I thought I was gon end up with considering everything that happened, the worms, the dry spell, the water mess, the storm. Traded off a good chunk of the extra tomatoes and some peppers to the woman with the marigolds, she grow a whole lot of greens I don't mess with and some sweet potatoes I be wanting, so we just swapped, her greens and potatoes for my tomatoes and peppers, ain't no money change hands, just a fair trade between growers.

End of the season I cleared the beds out, pulled the dead vines, turned the soil over, threw some leaves and mulch down to let it rest through the winter. Sat there for a minute just looking at it after I was done, tired, dirty, satisfied though.

Looking back on the whole thing, it was a lot, more work than I thought going in, more drama with the water than I ever expected from a garden of all things, but it was worth every bit of it. Ion regret none of it. Already planning what all I'm finna put in come next spring.
