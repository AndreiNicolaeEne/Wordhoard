Girl, let me tell you about the day we just had, 'cause I ain't never gon forget it long as I live.

It started with that storm that blew through the other night, the one that had everybody phone going off with the warnings back to back. Come morning it wasn't just branches down, it was the whole big oak that been standing at the mouth of the street since before any of us was born, it come down right across the road, root and all, tore the sidewalk up with it. Wasn't nobody getting a car in or out. Blocked from one side clean to the other, hanging down into three different yards, tangled up in itself.

It was the deacon what called it first. He come out his door practically still in his house clothes, looked at that tree a good minute, then hollered down the street, "Don't nobody move this by theyself, we finna need everybody for this one today." I'm telling you, word ain't take fifteen minutes to travel from his end of the block to mine. Somebody knocking on my door, phone already going off in a group text, mama'n'em already out on the porch trying to see what all the commotion was about.

We was figuring maybe eight or nine people gon show, the regular ones who always come through. Wasn't even studying who all might turn out. Next thing you know it's twenty-five, thirty people standing in that street looking at this tree like it owe them money. Old head from three doors down. The mechanic and both his boys. Ladies who don't be outside till the mail run, out there in they house shoes. It's a lot of folks I ain't seen together since the last cookout, all crowded round this one tree.

Once everybody settled, the work split itself up, ain't nobody had to be told twice. Some on the branches with the loppers, some dragging brush to the curb, couple men went and got they trucks so we could haul the big pieces off. My nephew, he the one with the chainsaw, and I'm telling you he carried the heaviest part of that whole job on his back. Cutting through that trunk in all that heat, sweat just pouring off him, and he ain't stop till it was done. Everybody kept telling him come sit down, drink some water, and he wasn't tryna hear none of it. That boy stay showing off, but that day he earned every bit of it.

'Bout midday is when the little disaster happened. Trunk was cut about three quarters through and it shifted wrong, rolled a way wasn't nobody expecting, clipped the mailbox post and dinged the fender on a car parked too close. Everybody froze up a second, then everybody talking at once talmbout whose car is that, who car is that. Turned out it was the mechanic own car, so wasn't no stranger got to be mad about it, he just shook his head and kept working like it wasn't nothing.

That's when the argument flared up. The mechanic and the man from two doors down got into it over how the rest of that trunk ought to get cut, one saying cut it in short rounds so it easier to carry, the other saying cut it long so it don't take all day rolling pieces around. Voices was getting up there for a minute, both of them stubborn, neither one budging. Old head ain't said much the whole day up to that point, but he walked right between them two and said, real calm, "Don't neither one of y'all know more than this tree do. Cut it how it fall." And that was that. Wasn't nobody arguing after the oldest man on the block said his piece.

By then the food had started coming out. My mama brought a whole cooler of lemonade and cut up two watermelons on a card table she drug out the garage. Somebody's sister brought a tray of chicken she already had cooked for Sunday and just gave it up early. Man from the corner house fired his grill up right there on the sidewalk, hot dogs and all, talmbout everybody gotta eat if everybody working. Wasn't no plan to it, it just happened, one house then another, till there was a whole spread sitting in the middle of the street.

The children had they own little job too. They was gathering up the little twigs and leaves into piles, running the hose for whoever needed a drink, and one of them took it on theyself to keep count how many truckloads of wood we done hauled off, hollering the number out loud every time one filled up like it was a game show. Wasn't hard work but they was proud of it, you could see it on them.

And the oldest man on the block wasn't having none of just sitting on his porch watching everybody else. He got him a rake and went to raking sawdust and little pieces into a pile the whole day, and every time somebody told him he could sit that one out, he'd say he been raking longer than half of us been alive and he ain't finna stop now. He stay stubborn like that, and it was good having him out there, felt right, felt like it needed him in it too.

Now the one thing that had everybody laughing, and I mean everybody, same time, was when that trunk finally gave all the way and rolled off the curb, and my nephew, tired as he was, jumped back from it and his foot caught the hose and he went down flat in the mud that hose had been making all day. Man fell straight on his back, chainsaw thrown safe off to the side thank God, and before he even said a word half the street was already hollering laughing. He laid there a second looking up at the sky like he couldn't believe it, then he started laughing too, couldn't help it. Ion think that street laughed that hard together in I don't know how long. He still walk around talking about it to this day, and won't nobody let him forget it either.

We thought that whole job was gon be done by four o'clock. Wasn't done till almost seven, a good three hours past what anybody figured. Everybody hot, everybody tired, wasn't nobody complaining though. But when it was finished, it was finished. That tree that had blocked the whole street was gone, cut up and hauled off or split for firewood, stacked in three different piles for whoever want it. Four yards that had branches all in them was cleared out. Sidewalk swept clean. Fence that got knocked was patched up enough to hold till somebody do it right. When that last truck pulled off with the final load, somebody let out a cheer, and it caught, everybody cheering, clapping, tired as we all was.

That evening the street ain't feel like the same street. Everybody was out on they porch later than usual, still talking about it, kids still playing way past when they normally get called in, like nobody wanted the day to be over just yet. I know it's just a tree, and I know come next week everybody go back to being who they was, keeping to theyself mostly. But that evening, it felt different. It really did.
