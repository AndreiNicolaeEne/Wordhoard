Let me tell you how that whole day went, cause you wasn't there and it was a lot.

We finally had to go clear out grandmama house, the one on the east side she been in since before any of us was even born, since granddaddy bought it his own self back when he first come up here from down in the country. Wasn't nobody arguing about the why of it no more by that point. Grandmama gone to be with the Lord back in the winter, and the taxes on that house done got so high wasn't a one of us could keep paying on it just to let it sit there empty. Mama'n'em talked for a minute about renting it out, but ion know who finna manage a rent house from three, four states away, so they ended up putting it up for sale. Matter of fact it already sold. We just had that one Saturday to go in and take out what was ours before the new people come get they keys.

Wasn't just close family up in there that morning either. It's a lot of people when you count it all, cousins ain't nobody seen since the last funeral, my auntie's play sister who been knowing grandmama near forty years, the deacon from the church come through just to help carry boxes, all kind of folk asking who all coming and what all we finna do with this and that. Wasn't even ten minutes before somebody was hollering from one room to the next.

We start in the front room, cause that's what hit you first walking in, and that's where it got to everybody quick. My auntie the one who found the record player, that same little suitcase player been sitting under the side table since I don't know when. She pulled it out and just stood there a minute holding it.

The auntie: Y'all remember she used to play her Mahalia Jackson on this thing every Sunday morning fore church? Loud too. Whole street probably heard it.

She ain't even cry, she just stay laughing to herself, shaking her head, holding that thing like it's made out of glass. Wasn't nobody bout to rush her.

Then down behind the couch, tucked up where the cushion meet the frame, my little cousin found a little tin box, rusted all round the edge, and don't nobody know what it even is or why it was back there all this time. Wasn't no money in it, wasn't no jewelry, just one old key that don't fit nothing in that house, and a folded up piece of paper so old the writing done faded clean off. My cousin holding it up talmbout...

The youngest: What is this? Whose is this? It ain't got nobody name on it noplace.

Wasn't a soul could say. My uncle turned it over about six times like the answer finna just show up if he squint hard enough, then he set it on the counter with the rest of the stuff wasn't nobody trying to be the one to throw away.

Kitchen went by fast after that, cept for the fuss over grandmama table. Big old wood table, scratched up bad, one leg don't even match the rest cause granddaddy replaced it his own self one time with whatever he had laying round. My auntie wanted it, said she the one still doing Sunday dinner for everybody so it need to go where the family stay gathering at. My other auntie said she got more room in her dining room for it. Went back and forth on that a good while, don't nobody backing down, till the eldest stood up from where she was sitting on the arm of the couch and settled it plain.

The eldest: The table go where the Sunday dinner go. Period. Now hand me that tape so we can get up this rug.

And that was the end of that. Wasn't another word said about it the whole rest of the day.

Now the recliner, that was a whole different kind of argument. Granddaddy chair, sat in that same corner his whole self, the leather done cracked and peeling, one arm held on with electrical tape, and it still smell like his pipe after all these years. My uncle said it need to go straight to the curb, ain't nothing but a fire hazard sitting there falling apart like that. My auntie bout had a fit hearing that.

The auntie: You ain't putting Daddy chair out on nobody curb. That's disrespectful and you know it.

The uncle: It's a raggedy old chair. Ain't nobody sposed to keep a broke-down chair just for sentiment.

Went round on that longer than the table even, longer than anything else that whole day. Ended up they compromised and said whoever got a garage big enough gone keep it out the weather till they figure out what to do, which everybody already know mean that chair finna sit in a garage forever, but wasn't nobody willing to be the one to lose that fight all the way.

Hallway closet, that's where it got strange. Behind all the old coats and a whole box of them cardboard church fans, my auntie's play sister pushed back a loose piece of the wall panel and found a whole shoebox been hid there, letters, old photographs, and a little roll of bills wrapped in a rubber band gone brittle as glass. Wasn't nobody in that family knew that box was even there. My mama came running soon as she heard the hollering.

The play sister: Y'all know she had all this hid back in here?

The mother: I ain't had no idea in this world. Don't nobody hide money in a wall unless it's a reason for it.

Turned out to be letters from way back when granddaddy first come up for work, fore he even sent for grandmama, and them two been writing back and forth for near a year fore she ever got up here. We all sat right there on the hallway floor reading a couple out loud, and that was bout the only quiet minute the whole day had in it.

Back bedroom, grandmama room, that's the one that ate up near the whole afternoon by itself. Wasn't nobody wanting to rush through a woman's personal things, and it's a whole lot of stuff packed in that one room, sixty some years of somebody's whole life, dresser drawers, the closet, boxes shoved under the bed she been had back there since before half of us was grown. Took us near three hours on that room alone while every other room in that house ain't took more than thirty, forty minutes apiece.

Round about one everybody stopped and ate. Deacon had brought a whole spread, fried chicken, greens, some cornbread my other auntie made that morning fore she even come, and we all sat out on the porch and round the yard on whatever we could find, paper plate in everybody lap. Wasn't much talking for a minute, just everybody hot and tired and glad to be off they feet.

After we ate we tackled the big stuff. That old upright piano in the dining room, don't nobody play no more, don't nobody got room in they house for it neither, and moving a piano ain't never no easy thing regardless. Called round to everybody first, my cousin, my other auntie, even the deacon, and wasn't a single person could take it or wanted the trouble of it. Ended up having to pay some men to come haul it off, and it was kind of sad watching it go out that door sideways, but ain't nobody had no better answer for where else it was gone go.

Grandmama Bible though, that one ain't take no discussion at all. Sitting right there on her nightstand the whole time, all marked up in her own hand in the margins, and soon as somebody found it everybody just looked over at the eldest and she nodded and that was it, settled without a word. Everybody already knew that Bible belonged with her.

By the time we finally got that back bedroom standing empty, walls bare, carpet still holding the marks where the furniture used to sit, it was already getting on toward evening good. Whatever was left over that ain't nobody claimed proper, some dishes, a few loose pictures wasn't nobody sure who wanted, we just laid it all out cross the kitchen counter and let everybody take what they was drawn to. Wasn't no fighting over none of it by that point, everybody too worn down for it.

Last thing we did, we walked back through every room one more time, just checking, making sure it was clear for real. My mama stood in that front room a good minute by herself fore she turned the last light off. Then she locked that door same as she done a thousand times fore, except this the last time it's ever gone open for none of us again.
