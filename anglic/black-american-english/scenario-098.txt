Alright, lemme tell it the way mama'n'em used to tell it, sitting out on the porch when it get too hot to be inside the house.

We ain't always been from here. Before Atlanta it was a little bitty place out past Macon, further down than that even, red dirt that get up under your fingernail and don't never come all the way out. My people was farming somebody else's land down there, sharecropping is what they called it, though wasn't no sharing to it, it was just they crop and everybody else profit off it. The old folks say the boll weevil come through one year and ate up near about everything that was growing, and on top of that the man they was renting from start talmbout the family owe him for seed and mule and this and that, when everybody know wasn't no owing to it, that was just how he did colored folks down there, keep you owing so you can't leave. That's the reason plain as it is. Wasn't one thing, it was that stacked up on everything else that already wasn't right, and it got to where staying wasn't safe noway, not after what happened to a man two farms over that don't nobody talk about too much to this day, just that he was there and then he wasn't.

So it was the one, my great-great-uncle, the oldest boy, he went on first. Alone. Didn't tell nobody but his mother he was finna go, wasn't tryna have the man that owned the land catch wind of it and come looking for him. He was young, wasn't but seventeen, eighteen, and he done made up his mind before anybody could talk him out of it. Went up on the rails, hopping what he could, walking what he couldn't, sleeping in whatever field or church would have a colored man passing through and asking nothing back. Ion know the whole route he took, wasn't never told to me exact, just that it took him near on three weeks to get where he was going, and it wasn't three weeks of nothing easy either. Cold some of them nights, and he ain't have but the coat on his back and a little bit of cornbread his mother wrapped up for him before he left.

He get on up to Atlanta and it's a lot of people in there already, more than he ever seen in his whole life, and don't nobody know him and he don't know a soul either. First few weeks he stayed wherever a church would let him lay his head, and then he fall in with a family from back home that had come up a season before him, and they let him stay on in the back room they had, ain't ask him for nothing but to help out where he could. That's how it went then, you stayed with whoever had a little room to spare, wasn't no shame in it, everybody done it one time or another, mama'n'em same as anybody.

But work wasn't there just cause you showed up for it. He be down at the corner every morning before light with the rest of the men waiting, and some days a wagon come by need three men for the day and some days it don't come by at all. Day's work here, day's work there, hauling this, digging that, whatever a man with a truck was willing to pay for. He stayed on like that near a year before he catch on steady with a man laying brick, and that's the trade he took up and never did put down after. Bricklaying. He done laid brick the rest of his natural life, and half the men in this family learned it off watching him do it.

Once he had put enough back, once he had a room to call his own and a little saved on top of it, he sent word down home for the rest to come on up. Wasn't quick money in them days, it took him better part of two years, going on two and a half, before he could send for his mother and his brothers and sisters, my great-great-grandmother among them, she was the baby of that bunch. And when they finally did make the trip up, it wasn't the whole family that made it whole. My grandmother, when she tell it, she a talker most times but she get quiet right there. She'd say...

The grandmother: It was a cousin of ours ain't make it up here with the rest. Took sick on the road and wasn't nobody had what it take to help her right then. That's all I'm gon say on it, and don't ask me for the name cause I ain't got it, ain't been spoke in this family since, out of respect.

That's as far as anybody ever tell that part. Don't nobody in this family, far back as I can ask, know who all was even traveling with her when it happened, just that it happened, and that's carried quiet ever since, no name attached to it in my whole life hearing this told.

The one thing that did come up whole was a quilt my great-great-grandmother had folded down in the bottom of a sack, pieced together out of flour sack and old dress scraps off that same farm, and that quilt been in this family longer than Atlanta been home to us, mama been say that. It's folded up right now, today, in a cedar chest at my auntie house. Don't nobody use it for the bed no more, too old and thin for that, but don't nobody get rid of it either. It's the one thing come up from down there we can still put our hand on.

They was all together again finally, better part of two and a half years after he first went up by himself, and once they was whole they found a place, little shotgun house on the east side, rented same as everybody at first, but within a few years they had bought it outright, paying a little at a time the same way he laid his brick a little at a time. That's the house my grandmother was born in. That's the house her brothers and sisters after her was born in too. And that right there, far as this family concerned, is what turned Atlanta from just a place he had run to into home, wasn't the working, wasn't even the buying of the house, it was when the babies start coming and this the only place they ever known. Once it's where the children born, it ain't a stopping place no more, it's just where you from.

My grandmother told it to us on that same porch same as I'm telling it now, and she stay laughing halfway through it even on the hard parts, cause that's how she was, she could find the light in the worst of it and still hold the weight of it too. I'm the one heard it as a child sitting up under her feet not understanding half of what all it meant yet, just knowing it mattered by how she'd go quiet on that one part and how she'd hold on to that quilt like it was something living still breathing in her hands. Now it's on me to tell it, and I tell it the way it was given to me, I don't change what I don't know and I don't make up what I ain't sure of. I just pass it on the way mama'n'em passed it to me, cause that's the only way it stay worth anything at all.
