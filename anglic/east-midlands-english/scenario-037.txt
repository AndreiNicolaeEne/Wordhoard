The father: Ey up, come on, up you get, the lot of you, we in't got all day to sit here scraitin about who's tired.

Nobody were scraitin, mind, we were just half asleep an all wobbly getting us boots on the wrong feet an that. Mam had the kettle going and cobs cut and wrapped for us snap, and a great flask of tea that'd have to do us till dinner. Dad give the first job out before we'd even got outside proper.

The father: Right, you lot want to get them gates open and the trailer hooked up before owt else, no messing.

The youngest: I got it, I got it, dint even need help.

The eldest: Aye go on then, don't get mardy if it dunt go first time.

It went first time as it goes, and off we went down the jitty at the side of the yard, out to the top field where it were all stood waiting, heavy with dew still on it. The uncle: Reet, me and him'll tek the top end, you lot start on the flat bit and work up to us.

So that's how it went, the two of them grafting away up the slope while the rest of us worked the flat, and it were slow at first, everybody finding their rhythm, nobody talking much, just the sound of it, boots and baskets and the odd cough.

Once we'd cleared the first section down the flat, Dad come round and swapped us all about.

The father: Right, you two swap over, you've been bent over that low row too long, you'll do yer back in, go and tek turn on the carrying, let them two have a go down here.

That's how the day goes really, in't it, nobody stopping still doing one job, you get moved on to whatever wants doing, keep the blood going so nobody seizes up.

By mid morning our youth's cousin, who'd only come to help out for the day and dunt do this sort of graft normal like, were falling well behind, proper struggling, red in the face and not saying nowt about it, just going slower and slower and not looking up.

The mother: Ey, come here a minute, tek a breather, have a swig of this.

She never made a show of it, just quiet like, went and worked alongside her a bit, took half her row without saying owt about why, just chatting about nowt in particular so it dint feel like charity. Nobody said a word about her being behind, you just don't, that's not the way of it.

Round about eleven the snap come out, Mam and our nan brought it down the field on the old barrow, cobs with cheese and ham in, and a big tin of biscuits, and we all sat down on whatever were dry enough to sit on and had us tea out the flask, going cold by then but nobody cared.

The uncle: Ee, I'm clemmed, I could eat a scabby horse.

The eldest: You've had three cobs already, give over.

The uncle: I'm a growing lad.

The father: You're forty six.

That set everyone off laughing, and there were a bit of argy bargy an all about the best way to do the next stretch, our uncle reckoning you want to go along the rows one way and Dad reckoning the other, neither giving in, both of them stood there with their hands on their hips like two old hens, till Mam told them to gerroff arguing and get on, dinner in't gunna eat itself for us.

We'd only been back at it an hour or so when the sky started doing summat we dint like the look of, gone that funny colour, and the wind dropping right off, dead still and heavy.

The eldest: Ey up, look at that, it's black over Bill's mother's.

The father: Aye, we'll want to get a shift on then, forget the far bit for now, we'll do the low field first while it's dry, that's the one we can't afford to lose.

So the whole plan changed there and then, no arguing about it this time, everybody just moved down to the low field cos that were the one that'd flood worst if it come down hard, and we went at it a fair bit quicker than before, everyone feeling it in the air, that sort of hurry that comes over you when you know weather's coming.

It held off though, thank god, just a spot or two and then it cleared right off and the sun come out proper hot for the afternoon, so we pushed on through, glad of it, even though we were all nesh about the cold snap earlier and now moaning we were baking.

The youngest: I'm boiling, can I have me coat off.

The mother: Aye go on, sun's got its hat on now int it.

The uncle: Aw for gods sake, that's twenty minutes work gone in two seconds that has.

The father: Never mind moaning about it, get it picked back up, wor's done is done, somebody find us some bailer twine and we'll rig that gate shut for now.

So that's what we did, tied the thing up with bailer twine and an old belt off somebody, and got back to loading, bit more careful this time, and nobody dwelled on it, just got on.

By late afternoon the light were already starting to turn that gold colour that tells you it in't gunna last much longer, and Dad come round again, quicker this time, calling out to everyone.

The father: Right, we want to get a shift on now, we've not got the light we think we have, let's get this last bit in before we lose it altogether.

And that were it then, everybody converged on the last section together, no more splitting up into jobs, just all of us in a line working across it, youngest and all, cos when it's the last bit you all finish it together, that's just how it's done. Nobody spoke much, just heads down, working till the light properly started to go purple over the hedge.

When it were done, proper done, Dad walked the length of the trailers counting it up out loud.

The father: That's four full an a half today, best we've had this year that is, by a fair way.

Everybody just sort of dropped where they stood after that, backs against the trailer wheels, nobody able to say much, just glad of it being over, aching in places you dint even know you had.

The mother: Right, before anybody sits down proper, somebody's got to shut them gates up top or we'll have half the county's sheep in the yard by morning.

The youngest: Ah, do I have to.

The mother: Go on, quick, then you can rest same as everyone else.

And off he went, dragging his feet a bit, muttering summat under his breath, and that were the actual end of it, once them gates were shut, the lot of us just sat there in the yard as the dark come down proper, too tired to even talk, just glad it were done and it were a good day's work, whatever state we were all in for it.
