Ey up, me duck. Sit yersen down a minute, I'm proper done in, but I've got to tell you about today because I've not seen owt like it round here in years.

You know that big old ash behind the yards, the one that leans right over the jitty? It had gone black over Bill's mother's about six the night before, our dad said that meant summat were coming and by God he were right. That tree come down in the wind, roots and all, right across the jitty, and took the fence panels out between ours and next door's, and cracked the roof clean off next door's shed into the bargain. I got up for work and couldn't even get out the back gate, there were that much of it blocking the way, branches everywhere, mud where the roots had pulled up, the lot. And that were never a job for one house on its own, not that size, not with the wall gone an' all.

It were the workmate from two doors up who got it going, really. She were out first, seen the state of it before anyone else were even up, and she went straight up and down knocking on doors, shouting, "ey up, get your coats on, we've a tree down and it's took the fence out an' all, we need hands." Word went up that street faster than owt. Half an hour, and it weren't just her and me and the ones either side neither. We'd reckoned on maybe five or six of us sorting it before work, but by eight o'clock there must have been getting on for twenty, twenty-two happen, stood there in their coats over their pyjamas, well over a dozen more than anyone had thought would turn out.

So we split it up between us, more or less as it fell. Some on the saws, cutting the branches down to summat you could actually shift. Some stacking the wood as it come off, into piles by the wall. A couple of the lads digging round the root ball to see how deep it had pulled. Somebody had to sort the fence, get a prop up so the yards weren't all open to one another. And it were the youth from the end house, big lad, works on the building, who took the heaviest of it, the main trunk itself. He tret that trunk like it were nowt, on it with the bigger saw most of the morning, hauling the rounds off once they were cut, and nobody else fancied a go, it were proper heavy going, but he never once said owt about it, just got on.

Then about eleven we had a right disaster. They'd got the trunk cut through in a couple of places and were trying to roll one of the big rounds out the way, and it went the wrong way, took the prop from under the fence panel they'd only just stood up, and that went down again, straight into the greenhouse two doors up. Glass everywhere. Nobody hurt, thank God, but there were a right minute where everyone just stood looking at it not knowing what to say.

And that's when the argument started, over how they should have propped it in the first place. The one whose greenhouse it were, he were proper narked.

The neighbour: You wanted doing it your way and look what's happened, glass all over my yard now.

The workmate: We had to shift it somehow, you can't just leave a great trunk sat there all day, and dunt he know that.

The eldest: Will the pair of you stop your row and let's get it sorted, we've not got all day to be stood here arguing the toss over a fence panel.

And that were that, more or less, though he stopped a good hour mardy about it before he come round.

By dinnertime it were half a work party, half a street party if I'm honest. My mother and the one from the corner house had brought cobs out, ham and cheese, must have made forty of them between them, and a big flask of tea passed round, and somebody's dad brought his snap out an' all and shared it round rather than take it to work, said this were more important. Later on someone fetched a crate of bottles out and it turned into that sort of afternoon. The kids had their own job, gathering up the little bits, the twigs and the kindling, into bags for firewood, and they loved it, running about with their arms full, getting under everyone's feet more than helping if I'm honest. One of the little ones went scraitin when a branch caught her leg, but she were right as owt after a cob and a go on the flask lid.

And the eldest on the street, must be gone eighty now, she weren't having any of sitting in her chair watching. She come out wrapped up like it were the middle of winter, said she weren't nesh, mind, just careful, and she'd not be told to sit it out, she'd sort the wood into proper piles by size so nobody were tripping over it. She stood there directing where every log went for the best part of the afternoon, and woe betide whoever put summat in the wrong pile.

Best laugh of the day, though, were when the youth, the one who'd been on the trunk all morning, went to heave the last big round up onto the barrow and his foot went straight from under him in the mud. Down he went, flat on his back, welly stuck upright in the mud on its own like it had grown there. "Gerroff," he says, laid there, not even trying to get up yet, "somebody gerroff laughing and give us a hand." He weren't hurt, just covered head to foot, but the whole street were creased up, even the eldest, and he had to sit there a minute laughing at himself before anyone could get him up.

We'd reckoned we'd have the jitty clear by dinnertime, tea at the latest. It were gone seven before the last of it were finished, three hour past what anyone had said, worra day it turned into. But by the end we'd got that jitty cleared right through, the fence back up proper this time, not just propped, the wood all stacked and split into three great piles for whoever wanted it, and the yard swept back to how it should look, near enough. When the last round went on the barrow there were a cheer went up right down the row, and even the ones who'd not lifted a finger all day come out their doors to see what the noise were.

It were a funny sort of evening after that. Chairs out in the yards, kids still playing past when they should've been in, nobody wanting to go in just yet. I don't know how long it'll last, int it always the way, probably back to normal by the weekend, everyone keeping to their own doors again. But tonight the whole street felt different, felt like it were ours again for a bit, and I were knackered mesen, but that's not summat that happens every day, is it.
