By gone midnight the house had thinned right down to just the five of us, sat round the kitchen table while everyone else had cleared off home. The front room still had all the chairs pushed back to the walls where they'd been stood talking, and there were a whole plate of cobs gone curly under the clingfilm that nobody had touched in an hour. Kettle had been on and off that many times it were barely worth switching off between.

The aunt were the one to start it off, quiet like, not really looking up from her tea.

The aunt: Ey up. Do you remember him stood at that cooker of a Sunday, faffing about with the gravy like it were rocket science. Wouldn't let nobody near it, not even to stir it.

The mother: It were out the packet half the time and all, love.

The aunt: Never.

The mother: It were. I've caught him mesen, more than once. Don't you look at me like that.

The aunt laughed then, proper laughed, shook her head and said she'd always got that wrong, thought he'd made it from scratch every single week of his life, all them years. The eldest let a laugh out sudden, more of a bark than owt, and clapped a hand over her own mouth like she hadn't meant it to come out that loud, but nobody minded. Not tonight.

Then the youngest come out with summat about the mornings, how he'd shout up them stairs the same three words every day of the week, and did it exact, right there at the table, pitched it just how he used to.

The youngest: Youth! Kettle's on!

And you could hear him say it, plain as owt, like he were still stood at the bottom of the stairs in his socks.

The eldest: He never once said it different in all them years. Not once.

There were a bit of a pause then, the good kind, everyone sat with it a minute. Then Me came out with summat none of the others had heard before, not properly, and it landed different to the rest.

Me: Did anyone know he used to walk down the jitty behind the chippy of a Tuesday night and feed them cats that lived round the bins there? Proper feeding them, tinned stuff, not scraps. Been doing it years. I only found out cos I followed him one time, thought summat were up.

The mother: Never.

Me: Straight up.

The mother: All them years and he never said a word to me about it.

That got a proper "never!" out of the aunt an all, and the room went quiet a minute after, everyone just sat with that one, like they were turning it over.

It were the mother broke it in the end, up out her chair reaching for the tray.

The mother: Right, come on, get one of these down you, they'll only go harder. Anyone want owt in their tea while I'm up.

And that were enough, chairs scraping, the plate going round, somebody passing the sugar, just an excuse to move about a bit.

Setting back down, the aunt and the eldest got into it a bit then, about summat from years back, nothing to do with any of this, an old barney about who were meant to have had the good dresser out the front room when their nan went, and whether it were ever actually promised to anyone or whether the eldest just took it. Voices went up a notch for a second.

The eldest: You know full well what were said.

The aunt: I know what I remember being said, which int the same thing.

But neither of them pushed it, both sort of let it drop of their own accord, cos it dint feel right, not tonight, and the aunt went back to her tea and the eldest reached for another cob like nothing had been said at all.

Then the youngest started on about the time he wouldn't gerroff about the heating.

The youngest: Do you remember when the boiler packed in that winter and he wouldn't have the new one the bloke recommended, said it were a rip-off, said the old one just needed a part. Weeks he had us in coats indoors. Right mardy about it an all if anyone brought it up.

The mother: Nesh as owt normally, couldn't stand the cold himself, and there he were sat in three jumpers rather than admit he were wrong about it.

The eldest: And it cost more in the end getting the part than the new one would've been.

The youngest: He never let on that it did, either.

Everyone laughed proper at that one, warm laughing, no edge in it at all now, just fondness, and somewhere in the laughing it seemed to settle itself without anybody actually saying it out loud, that that were just him all over, stubborn as they come, dead set on being right even when he weren't, and that were the thing, more than any other thing, that they'd always have him down as.

It were partway through the next bit, somebody talking about his snap tin, how he'd have the same two cob sandwiches wrapped up for work every day of his working life and moan if the bread were the wrong sort, when the mother went quiet and it turned out she'd started scraitin, soft, not making a show of it, just sat there with her tea going cold and the tears coming down while everyone carried on talking gentle around her, nobody stopping to make a fuss of it, just the aunt's hand finding her arm on the table without either of them looking at the other.

Talk went on soft like that a good while longer, and then it turned practical, the way it does in the end. Who were ringing the undertaker in the morning, who had the number for the flowers, who were sorting his clothes out and when, said plain, no ceremony to it, just what needed doing said out loud so it were said.

The eldest: I'll do the flowers first thing, and I'll ring our lot and tell them what time.

The youngest: I'll sort his wardrobe out, if that's alright with everyone. Rather it were me.

The mother: Ta, love.

By the time that were all settled it were gone two, and it were only Me left proper awake, the others heavy-eyed in their chairs, and there dint seem a right way to end it so it just come out plain, glass of whatever were left in the bottle held up a bit.

Me: Go on then. To him. Gravy out the packet and all.

And that were it, glasses touched quiet, nobody saying much more after, just sat there a while longer in the warm of the kitchen before anyone made a move for bed.
