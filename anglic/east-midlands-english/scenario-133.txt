Ey up. Sit thissen down a minute and I'll tell thee about this plot o' mine, whole year of it start to finish, cos by heck it's been one thing after another.

Started off thinking on what to put in back end o' winter, doing a bit of a plan on the back of an envelope, spuds down one end, beans up t'other. This year I says to mesen, right, I'm avin' a proper go at broad beans and onions and a row of leeks an' all, seeing as t'ground had had a good rest off it. Dint put nowt in till a good few weeks after the last cold snap, mind, cos you put owt in too early round here and the frost'll have it off thee, no messing. Told the youth up top plot the same when he were rushing to get his spuds down. Ey up, I says, me duck, you've gorra wait till the soil's warmed through, don't be so daft as to rush it, there's no medal for being first.

Well once it all went in we had a right good spell o' weather, warm and soft, rain of a night and sun of a day, and everything come up lovely. Spuds through in no time, beans fair shot up the canes, and I thought, this could be a good un this year and no mistake.

O' course summat allus comes along to spoil it. First thing to show its face were greenfly, right thick on t'broad beans, tops gone black wi' em. I tret it wi' a spray o' soapy water like me old dad allus swore by, and it knocked 'em back a bit, kept the worst of it off, but it dint sort it proper, they kept coming back every week or so and I were forever out there of a morning pinching the tops off wi' me fingers an' all, mucky job that.

Then we had a right dry stretch, weeks of it, not a drop out the sky, ground gone hard as owt underfoot. I were down there every night after work hauling watering cans up from the tap by the gate, up and down that jitty at the side wi' two full cans at a time, arms fair dropping off me, just to keep the stuff alive, never mind growing owt more off it. Dint dare miss a night neither or you'd come back and find summat wilted flat, and I'm that nesh first thing of a morning I hated going down before work an' all, but there weren't no choice in it.

And that's what started the bother wi' next door's plot. Tap's shared between four or five of us and there's meant to be a rota pinned up in the shed, but it were that dry everyone wanted it same time, and one evening I gets down there and the fella on the plot next to mine's got the hose running full pelt into his water butt, and I'd had me name down for that very slot.

Me: Ey up, that were meant to be my turn on the tap tonight, y'know.

The neighbour: Were it? I've been stood here ten minute, no one said nowt to me.

Me: It's on the rota, plain as owt, look, it's pinned up right there.

The neighbour: I dint see no rota. Alright, keep thi hair on, I'll gerroff it in a minute, just letting the butt fill first.

Worra to-do that were, I'll not lie, both on us stood there mardy as a couple o' kids over a garden tap, going on about whose turn it dunt matter to now. In the end the site secretary sorted it proper, put a proper timed rota up wi' names and half-hour slots wrote clear, and after that it dint cause no more grief between us, we just give one another an ey up of a morning same as ever, like nowt had happened. Water does that to folk though, brings out the worst in the mildest of us, int it.

Best thing I grew, no arguing wi' it, were the runner beans. Once they got going they dint stop, pod after pod, giving loads away to anyone who'd have 'em off me, workmates, the woman on the corner, our lot were sick the sight on 'em by August and I still had carrier bags full to give away. Proper win that were, no bother wi' it at all bar the picking, and picking's no hardship when it's coming that easy.

Sweetcorn were the opposite tale though. Just as the cobs were filling out nice, birds had the lot, pigeons mostly, stripped clean, husks pulled back and pecked to nowt. Could've sat down and scraitin over it, honest, after all that watering an' all. Put netting over what were left but it were shutting the gate after the horse had bolted by then, lost more than half the row to 'em and there weren't no getting it back.

Middle o' summer we had that storm come through. One o' them evenings it'd gone black over Bill's mother's all afternoon and you just knew summat were coming, and by heck it did, wind and hail both together, flattened three whole rows of the beans and knocked the sweetcorn what were left clean over an' all. Spent best part of a weekend out there staking it all back up, tying canes, propping stems back straight, mucky and back-breaking work wi' the ground all sodden underfoot, but most on it come back, tougher than it looked bless it.

Come the end though, when I got it all tallied up, it were a right good year on balance. Traded a load o' me runner beans and a few leeks to the fella wi' the fruit trees for a bag o' his apples and some plums, cos I've nowt that grows fruit on my plot and he'd more veg off his own than he knew what to do wi'. Dug the rest of it over end o' the season, cleared the old stalks off, put manure on and covered it over ready for winter, packed a cob and a flask in me snap bag the last day just to make an afternoon of it out there on me own, and stood looking at it all bare when I'd done and thought, well. Bit of everything this year, pests and drought and a right barney over a tap and a storm an' all, but more good than bad by a long chalk when you weigh it up proper. I'd call it a good un, all told, and I'll be back at it come spring doing it all again, daft as that sounds to some.
