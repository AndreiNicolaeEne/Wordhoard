Ey up, come in then, mind the step, it's higher than it looks and everyone what ever come here went arse over tit on it at least once. This were the front door, right, and you'd never use it unless you were a stranger or the meter man, cos family and that come round the side, always. But say you did knock here, you'd stand on that step and you could hear the telly through the glass and someone shouting who is it, and there were always a good two minute wait while whoever were nearest undone the chain, cos the chain were always on, even at four in the afternoon. Arriving here always felt like being let in on summat, like the house had been sat there holding its breath for you and now it could get on.

Straight through is the front room, though we never called it that, we called it the other room, cos the real living happened through yonder in the back. This one had the good settee in it, the one with the plastic still half on one arm where nobody could be bothered to peel the rest off, and the good telly wasn't even the good telly, it were just the only telly, but this room got used Christmas, and if the vicar come, and any time summat serious were being said. You'd know it were serious cos you'd get sat in here on your own with a squash and told to stay put while the grown-ups talked next door.

The main room, though, the back room, that's where it all went off. Telly on all day whether anyone were watching it or not, just for the company of it. That's where the arguments happened and the making up happened an' all, where the youth would sprawl on the floor doing homework he swore blind he'd already done, where the mother sat with the ironing board up permanent, cos there were always summat wants ironing in this house, always. That room smelled of fabric conditioner and whatever were on the go for tea. You'd get told ey up, budge up then when you come in, budge up meaning move your backside off the good end of the settee.

Kitchen's through here, mind the dog. This kitchen always smelled of onions frying, no matter what time of day, no matter what were actually for tea, there'd be onions going in a pan cos that's how you started everything. The one habit that always happened in here, the mother stood at that counter of a morning cutting cobs, buttering away, wrapping snap up in foil for whoever were off to work, and there were always a row about whether there were enough cheese to go round. Kettle never went cold. If you come in nesh off the street she'd have you stood by that oven before your coat were even off, warm your hands duck, gerroff moaning and warm 'em.

Upstairs then, careful, the third one creaks summat rotten and always has, been creaking as long as anyone remembers and nobody's ever fixed it, it's just the third stair, everyone steps over it without thinking now. Two bedrooms up here for however many of us there were. The eldest had her own room, cos she were the eldest and that were the law of it, no arguing. Rest of us top and tailed in the other, two to a bed some years, elbows in your back all night, and you'd hear whoever were awake muttering gerroff when a foot strayed too far. Nobody thought owt of it, that's just how a house worked round here.

That little room at the top, the boxroom, that were the nan's, in everyone's head, even after. Even when it were being used for the ironing pile or the hoover, you'd still say I'm just popping into me nan's room, it kept the name for years. Smelled different to the rest of the house, a bit of lavender, bit of mothball, and you weren't to go rooting through her drawers, though everyone did once, looking for owt interesting, and got a right telling off for it.

Under the stairs were mine, mesen, my own little spot, dark as owt but I loved it, had me a torch and a couple of comics stuffed down there and nobody bothered looking for me under there cos who'd think to. You could hear everyone through the wall, the whole house going on above and around you, and you'd feel like you'd disappeared a bit, in a good way.

Out the back were the yard, not a garden really, more a yard with a bit of grass fighting for its life in one corner. Washing line strung corner to corner, and you'd duck under sheets to get to the outside loo, which weren't used for owt but bikes by the time I remember it. That's where the youth kept his bike, propped against the wall, always summat wrong with the chain on it, always being fettled and never quite fettled right.

The neighbours were close enough you could hear their kettle whistle through the wall, near enough that when their lad were being mardy about summat you'd hear the whole performance without trying. One lot had a jitty running down the side of their place, a proper twitchel, dark and narrow, and that's where all the kids cut through to get to the shops quicker, though we'd been told a hundred times not to, cos of who might be down there, though nowt ever happened, it were just the shortest way and that's all that mattered when you were being sent for milk.

Same time every evening you'd hear the same sound, the ice cream van doing its round further down, that same tune half strangled, and everyone's heads would go up like meerkats. And same time every morning, near enough, you'd see the same fella walk his dog past the window, same slow shuffle, same dog stopping at the same lamppost, you could set a clock by it.

The thing that were always broken, always being moaned about, were the back gate. Hinge gone, had to lift it to shut it, and every single visitor got told mind that gate, lift it don't push it, and every single visitor pushed it anyway.

Summer the yard were where you lived, doors open, everyone in and out, that washing line never empty. Winter the whole house shrunk down to the back room round the fire, and if it come on to rain hard someone'd look out the window and say it's black over Bill's mother's, best get that washing in, and there'd be a mad scramble for pegs.

Street itself were ours of a summer evening, jumpers for goalposts, and you'd hear someone's mother stood on the step hollering a name down the street when tea were ready, and you'd have to leave the game mid-goal, moaning the whole way in.

Last clear memory I've got of this place is stood right here in this kitchen, everyone crammed in for someone's leaving do or summat, laughing at nowt in particular, the kettle going again, someone saying budge up, budge up, room for one more.

It's not ours no more, love. Someone else's now, done the kitchen out different, took that back gate off altogether by the sound of it. House still stands though, still there on the street, still creaking on that same third stair for all I know. That's it though, duck, that's the lot, that's where I'm from.
