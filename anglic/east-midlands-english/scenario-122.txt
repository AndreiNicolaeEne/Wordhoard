The young one: Ey up. Go on then, tell us the one about what calls out. Nan says you're the one as knows it proper.

Me: Aye, well, sit yersen down then, me duck, and dunt be interruptin', cos I only tell it the once through and then it's told for the night.

It allus comes up the same road, this one. Somebody new to the place, or somebody's lad grown enough to be out past dark on his own two feet for the first time, and they ask why folk still say gerroff that stretch of towpath past the lock once it's gone dark, dunt walk it, whatever y'do. And I'll tell 'em same as I'm tellin' thee now, cos it's not summat you mek up to frighten bairns with, whatever your mardy uncle reckons.

It's not a thing you see, not first off, not ever really proper. It's felt afore it's owt else, a coldness comin' up off the water that's got no business bein' that cold on a mild night, and it's heard afore it's ever seen, if it's seen at all. Voice first, allus voice first. That's the whole shape of it, if shape's even the right word for it, summat that puts a voice on afore it puts owt else on.

And what it does, what it's said to do, is call out to thee in a voice thee knows. Not a stranger's voice, mind, not owt daft like that. Your own mother's voice, or your dad's, or whoever it is you'd drop everythin' and run to if they called. It calls thee off the path, out into the dark and away from the road and away from anybody as could get to thee in time. That's the whole of its business, drawin' folk off where nobody can reach 'em.

And it only comes after midnight, never afore, and only on them nights when there's no moon at all, black as the inside of your pocket. Black over Bill's mother's is one thing, that's just a storm comin', and everybody knows to fetch the washin' in for that. A moonless night down by the lock is another thing entire, and them as knows, knows.

The spot's just the one stretch, mind, I'm not sayin' it's owt bigger than that. Just where the towpath dips down under the trees past the old lock, where it goes narrow and the water goes quiet like it's listenin' and all. That's the bend. Nowhere else round here, and I'll not have it said different. It's not the whole valley's, it's ours, that stretch and no more.

I first heard it off me own nan, when I were nowt but a bairn mesen, sat by the fire of a night when I ought to've been abed. She'd have her cob and her tea afore she'd start on it, same as ever, and then she'd tell it to me plain as owt, no smile on her at all, flat as readin' out the weather, tret it dead serious the whole way through. I've heard others tell it since with a laugh in it, a little chuckle at the end like they're lettin' thee off the hook, but that laugh never once reached their eyes, not on a single one of 'em, and I noticed that even as a bairn and I've never forgot it.

First bit of proof folk allus bring up is the ground itself. There's a patch right at that bend, not big, size of a good rug, and nowt has ever grown on it. Not grass, not weed, not nettle even, and nettle'll grow through owt. Folk have tried plantin' there just to prove a point and it comes to nowt every time, dies back to bare dirt within the season, int it summat.

Second bit is the sort you allus get told secondhand, somebody heard it off somebody who heard it off somebody else, the way them things travel. A workmate of mine told us about it one dinner time over us snap, said her cousin's fella were walkin' home that way one black night and heard his own name, clear as owt, called from right off the path, and he legged it the whole rest of the way without lookin' back once. I never met the fella mesen. That's how it allus comes, at one remove, but it comes so regular and from that many separate mouths that you'd have to be daft not to wonder what's behind it.

But the one that gets told in full every time, word for word, is what happened to a relative of mine, years back now, long afore I were born even. Walkin' that stretch alone, late, moonless night same as ever, and heard their own name called, clear as a bell, from off the path in the trees. And they'd gone three, happen four steps toward it, off the path and into the dark headin' toward the voice, afore summat made 'em stop dead and turn back. Nobody in the family's ever agreed on what that summat were, whether it were a noise or a feelin' or just plain luck, but they stopped, and they turned, and they walked the rest of the way home fast and dint look back, not once, not scraitin' but shakin' summat rotten by the time they got in.

And to this day the family tells it the same road every time, word for word near enough: "It were me own name, clear as you're standin' there, and it were her voice and all, my own mother's voice, and she'd been dead three year." That's the line, allus that line, near enough exact, and the family says it the same whichever of 'em's tellin' it.

That's the bit that shuts the doubters up for a second, that. The dead mother's voice, callin' by name, that precise, that particular. Even them as reckon it's all nowt but an old tale, they go quiet on that one detail a beat afore they carry on.

And then straight after, the very same breath near enough, is the bit that gives the doubters their openin' to laugh it off again, cos it were pitch dark and cold and my relative admitted afterward they couldn't rightly swear whether they'd gone three steps or four, or whether it were even off the path proper or just close to it. That little bit of not-quite-sure is all the doubters need, and they'll tek it every time, and I dunt blame 'em for it neither.

Story changes a bit dependin' on who's tellin' it and all, I'll not pretend different. Some tell it softer, mekk it sound near gentle, like it's just lonesome rather than owt cruel, summat that only wants company. Others tell it harder, mekk out it's after thee proper, meanin' thee real harm if it gets thee off that path and away from anybody. I've heard it both roads off different mouths in me own family, and I've never once minded the difference, cos it's the shape of it that stays the same underneath, whoever's doin' the tellin'.

And the one thing we still do here, quiet like, nob'dy mekkin' a show of it, is we dunt ever answer if we hear us own name called from somewhere dark, not till we've had a proper look with us own eyes and seen who it actually is stood there callin'. Might be nowt. Might just be somebody's mardy dog wants lettin' in, or a workmate messin' about on the way back through the jitty, or the wind doin' summat daft through the trees. But we look first, allus, every time, and we gerroff inside quick after.

And I'll tell thee straight, cos I've never lied to thee about owt else: I dunt fully believe it mesen, not in the cold light of day, not really. I'm nesh as owt and daft with it, and I've still walked that stretch a good few times of a night in me time. But I've never broke that rule once, not once, and I'll tell thee summat else and all, me duck. I dunt plan on startin' now.
