Ey up. Sit thi sen down, I'll tell thi the whole of it, start to finish, cos nob'dy ever gets the full tale, they just see the end of it and think it were nowt.

Right so. We'd outgrown the place, simple as that. Four of us in what were built for two, and the little un sleeping in what were basically a cupboard wi a window in it. Yer can only shuffle round each other in a kitchen so long before summat's gorra give. So me and the wife sat down at the table one Sunday wi a brew each and worked it out proper, reckoned it'd tek two of us and maybe one more now and then, four month, tops, done by the summer. Eight month later I were still laughing at that number.

Ended up six hands went through that job one way or another over the eleven month it took. Me, the wife, my mate from work, her brother, the lad from two doors down who were between jobs and just wanted summat to do wi his hands, and his dad and all near the end, who knew more about brickwork than the rest of us put together.

First wrong turn come early doors, which I always think's the worst kind, cos yer no rhythm yet to catch it. Set the footings out by eye, thought I'd got the line off the fence dead right, and it were only when the lad next door come round wi his level and stood there going...

The one from next door: What's that doing, then. That in't square, that.

, that we clocked it were out by a good few inch across the run. Two weeks' work, all that digging and shuttering, back out again and done proper wi string lines and pegs this time. I could've cried, honest, standing there of an evening looking at a hole where a wall should've been.

Then there were the day the extra hands turned up wi'out being asked. I hadn't rung nob'dy, hadn't said owt to anyone, just gone out one Saturday to mek a start on me own, and by dinnertime there were four blokes stood in the garden. Turned out the wife had mentioned it to her brother down the pub, who'd mentioned it to two of his mates, and they'd just turned up in their work boots wi a flask each like it were nowt.

Her brother: Well tha can't do a roof on thi own, can tha. Gerroff wi thi, we're stopping.

Weren't asked for it but I'll not forget it. That were the day we got more done than any other single day of the whole job.

Weather had its say an' all. Middle of it, best part of a week, nowt but rain sideways and black over Bill's mother's every morning we looked out the window, so black yer knew it were no use even getting the ladders out. Sat in doing nowt but drink tea and look at the tarps flapping on what we had up. Mardy as owt by day four, all of us, snapping over nothing, cos there's nowt worse than being ready to graft and the sky just wain't have it.

Ran short on timber about halfway an' all, right when we needed it most. Yard were out, next lot weren't due a week and a half, so we mekked do wi what were kicking about, bits left from an old job of my mate's, a few lengths the lad's dad had in his shed doing nowt, one length we planed down off summat that were nearly firewood. It weren't what I'd have chose but it went in and it's held, and if yer don't know to look for it yer'd never clock it in't the same batch as the rest.

Had a scare an' all, up on the scaffold, lost me footing reaching for a bag of nails I'd left too far over. Went down, not far, maybe ten foot, landed on the mixed-up rubble and old boards we'd not cleared yet, which I reckon's what saved us any real damage. Winded summat rotten, couldn't get a breath in for what felt like an age, and the wife come running out the back door white as owt.

The wife: Don't thi dare move, just breathe, breathe for me, come on.

Nowt broke though. Bruised black down one side for a fortnight and couldn't lift me arm proper for a week, but nowt worse than that, and I'll tell thi, I were more nesh about heights after that than I'd ever been before it, even in August wi the sun out I'd be shivering up that ladder.

Had a right barney over the chimney breast an' all, me and the lad's dad, cos he reckoned it wanted doing one way, ties in every course, and I reckoned another, said it'd hold fine wi'out all that faffing. Went back and forth over it a good hour, both of us mardy, neither giving. In the end he just looked at me straight.

The lad's dad: I've built more of these than tha's had hot dinners, love, so tha can do it thi own road if tha wants, but tha'll be doing it twice.

He were right an' all, course he were. Did it his way in the end and it's stood solid since.

Worst of it though were the wall out the back. We'd got it half up, thought it were sound, and it were only when he come to check it proper, string, level, the lot, that we found the whole thing were leaning, ever so slight, but enough that in ten year it'd have been a proper problem. Had to tek the lot down, every block, and start again from the footing up. That were a low day, that were, standing looking at a pile of blocks that used to be a wall and knowing we'd to do it all again wi nowt to show for the last fortnight's graft.

But then come the day the frame stood up proper, all of it, roof timbers on and squared and true, and we all just stood there in the garden looking at it, not saying much, just, there it were. Little thing really, in't it, a frame wi no walls yet, but it felt like summat. First time the whole job felt real instead of just a mess of holes and mud.

Last stretch, final four nights, we were out there past dark wi a lamp rigged off an extension lead, cold enough some nights to see yer breath, finishing off skirting and the last of the plastering, cos we'd set us minds on it being done and none of us wanted it dragging into another month. Worked past when any sensible person'd have packed in, but there's summat about being that close that yer just carry on.

And then it were done. Just like that, one evening, last bit of paint on, and we stood in the doorway of what used to be a hole in the ground eleven month back and it were a room, a proper room, wi light coming in right and everything square where it should be.

Tallied it up after, eleven month near enough on the nose from that first Sunday round the table, six of us all told who'd had a hand in it at one point or another, though two of them only the once. Weren't the four month I'd reckoned that first day, not by a stretch, but I don't think I'd have believed anyone who told me the real number back then anyroad.

Stand in it now most days and still can't quite credit it were us that done it. Yer forget the wall we tore down, the two weeks lost at the start, the rain, the fright off the scaffold. Yer just stand there of a morning wi yer brew and it's warm and it's square and it's yourn, done wi yer own hands and five other pairs an' all, and that's summat, that is. That's really summat.
