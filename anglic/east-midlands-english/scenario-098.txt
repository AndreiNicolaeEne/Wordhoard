Ey up. You've asked us this before, but I'll tell it again, same road me nan told it me, and her mother told it her, back and back till you get to the one it happened to first hand.

We weren't from round here to begin with, not this family. There were a bit of ground back over in Ireland, rented, nowt more than a smallholding, and the fella who owned it never once set foot on it his whole life, just took the rent same as ever whatever the year had been like. And some of them years were bad, crop failing two, three times running, nowt in the ground worth digging up and nowt to sell at market neither, and the rent still wanted regardless. Too many mouths, not enough spuds, that's the shape of it. Not a story about a country really, just a story about a family that had run clean out of road where they were.

It were the great-great-grandad went first, on his tod, youngest of the lot and hardly out of being a youth himself. Nobody sent him exactly, he sent himself, cause he'd heard there were work this side, in the mills or down the pits or wherever a strong back'd get took on. He went with what he stood up in and not a lot else besides.

The crossing were the hard bit, and whoever tells it never makes light of that part. Weeks packed in below, folk being sick, water going bad, the boat rolling summat rotten day and night. He dint talk of it much himself by all accounts, and he dunt need to, cause the one bit that always got said were about the cousin. Never a name to it, mind, that's kept back out the whole tale even now. Whoever's telling it, that's as far as they'll go: there were a cousin went on that crossing and never got to see the other side of it. You'd ask more and you'd get looked at like you'd said summat you shouldn't, and that were the end of asking, every time.

He landed with next to nowt in his pocket. A woman at the dock felt sorry for the state of him and give him a cob off her own, and he always said that were the first bit of kindness he had off anybody this side of the water. First while there were no work steady at all, just whatever a man could pick up day to day, carting, digging, holding a horse for somebody, owt going. More than one gaffer telled him to gerroff before he found one that'd take him on for even an afternoon. "Worra you stood there for," one of them says to him, "get in or gerroff, I've not got all day," and he learned quick which yards were worth the asking and which weren't.

He'd no roof of his own neither, not for a good while. He lodged in with another family off the same bit of ground back home who'd gone over before him, crammed in with them down a jitty where half the houses backed straight onto the next street's yard. Slept where there were room, ate what were going, give over what he earned toward his keep. Not his own place, borrowed off them entire, and that family tret him decent, like their own near enough, and he spoke well of them the whole rest of his life for it.

It were a fair while before he got took on proper. A word put in for him in the end, and he got set on down the pit. Steady wage after that, same hour every day, and once he'd summat put by he sent word back that he'd gorra place now and enough saved for the crossing.

Two year it were, near enough, afore the rest of them come over. His wife and the bairns waiting all that while on word from him, not knowing one month to the next whether he'd send for them or whether summat had happened to him instead and they'd never hear at all. Two year's a long stretch to be apart on nowt but a letter now and again saying he were still living. But he kept his word and they come, and that were the family whole again, first time in two year, and by all accounts nobody said much when they landed, they just stood there holding onto one another.

What the great-great-grandmother carried with her weren't much, you couldn't carry much on a crossing like that, but she'd a little tin box off her own mother, nowt grand, just tin with a bit of pattern worked round the lid, and it come the whole way wrapped up in a shawl so it wouldn't knock about. It's still in the family now, sat on a shelf, and whoever's got it don't let the kids play with it, though half of them have had a go when nobody were looking, mesen included, I'll not lie.

Once they were all together they got a house of their own, one of them back to backs, shared wall both sides, no more room in it than you could swing a cat in, but it were theirs to rent proper, not borrowed off nobody, and that come within a few year of him first landing. And down the pit he stayed the whole rest of his working life, snap tin under his arm every morning same as the next man, and his lads went down after him when they come of age, and their lads after that, right up till there were no pits left to go down.

The bairns come along in that house, one after another, and that's the bit everyone tells you were what made it home in the end. Not the landing, not even the house itself, but the first one born there, cause once there were one of the family come into the world in that place and no other, it stopped being where they'd ended up and started being where they were from.

That's how I got it mesen, sat at me nan's table of a winter evening, black over Bill's mother's outside and the rain coming sideways, one of the little ones scraitin cause they'd been sent up and dint want to go.

Nan: "Sit thee down a minute, me duck, I'll tell thee how we come to be here, seeing as you're that mardy about going up anyroad."

Me: "Go on then, Nan."

Nan: "Ey up, don't rush me, I'm getting to it. And gerroff that chair arm, you'll have it over."

And she'd tell it slow, same shape every time, though the words moved about a bit telling to telling. She'd get to the cousin and go quiet a second, same as her own mother must've gone quiet telling her, and none of us ever pushed past that quiet, we knew by then not to.

Me: "Weren't you nesh, coming over on a boat like that?"

Nan: "You'd be nesh an' all, love, standing on deck in nowt but what you'd left the house in. Now stop asking and let us finish."

I'm telling it thee now the same road it were told me, near enough word for word where I can manage it, cause that's the only bit of that old ground we've got left, the telling of it. House is stood, tin box is on the shelf, and every one of us that come after were born right here and nowhere else, and that, when you get down to it, is the whole of what makes anywhere home, int it. Not where you started. Where the bairns come.
