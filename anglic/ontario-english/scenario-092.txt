By the time it was down to the five of them the trays in the front room were more crumbs than squares, and somebody had gone around and shut off the big light so it was just the one over the stove and the little one over the sink. The wife had her sleeves pushed up and a tea gone cold in front of her she wasn't going to touch. The eldest was across from her still in the good shirt, tie long since balled up in a pocket somewhere. The brother had his coat half on like he was leaving any minute and never quite did. The friend had gone out to the car around eleven and come back in with a two-four, "just in case," and nobody had said no to that. The younger one sat closest to the stove with her knees up on the chair, the way she used to sit when she was small.

The friend: He used to stand right there, right in that doorway, hollering that it wasn't a barn door, somebody shut it, hydro's not free.

The wife: Every single night. Forty years of that.

The friend: Every night, I'm telling you.

The eldest: He said it different though. It wasn't hydro's not free first, it was were you born in a barn, and then hydro's not free right after it, like a one-two.

The friend: No, no, it was hydro's not free first, and then the barn thing.

The eldest: I don't even know why I'm arguing it, you're probably right. I always get that one backwards, eh.

The younger one let a laugh out sudden, a short bark of one she clearly hadn't meant to let go, and clapped her hand over her mouth after, and nobody minded it at all, the brother even smiled a bit into his cup.

The wife: You know he'd walk into the counter, same counter, every week of his life, and say sorry to it. Sorry there. Like it had done something to him.

The brother, doing the voice low and a little gruff, exactly the way it used to come out: Sorry there.

That got a laugh too, softer than the last one, the kind that has a bit of a catch behind it.

The brother: You guys know he used to write poems?

The wife: What?

The brother: I found a notebook out in the garage, must be two, three years back now, tucked behind the paint cans. "For no one in particular" wrote right on the front of it.

The eldest: You're kidding me.

The wife: He never once said a word to me about that.

The eldest: No. That's, no.

Nobody said anything for a second after that, just sat with it, the fridge humming in the quiet, and then the younger one got up without a word and brought the plate of butter tarts back round the table again, an excuse more than anything, and everybody took one they didn't really want.

The brother: You still owe me forty bucks from that euchre game at the cottage, you know that, eh.

The friend: I do not owe you forty bucks, that's a myth you've kept alive for like six years.

The brother: It's not a myth, I've got a good memory.

The friend: Your memory's the myth, buddy.

Nobody pushed it past that, it wasn't the night for it, and the brother just waved a hand like forget it and let it go.

The friend: Remember when his knee went, and everybody was on him for two solid years to get it looked at, and he just kept giving'r anyway, wouldn't even take the good parking spot at the plant, walked the whole lot on it.

The wife: Two years. The doctor finally saw him and said how long's it been like this and he goes, oh, a while.

The eldest: A while.

The friend: Stubborn as anything, that man. Wouldn't call anybody for the eavestrough either, remember, insisted on the ladder himself at seventy-one years old, and your mother standing at the bottom of it hollering at him the whole time.

The wife, and there was something soft under it now, not quite a laugh: I told him, I said if you fall off that ladder don't come crying to me, and he just kept right on going up it.

There wasn't an edge left on any of it now, it was all just fondness, the kind a story gets once enough time's gone soft around it.

Nobody said it right out, but there was a feeling settling over the table anyway, that whatever else got said tonight, that was going to be the one thing, that he never once missed a thing, not a birthday, not a stag and doe three towns over, not a march break drive up north when nobody else wanted to make the trip, he was just there, always, no fuss made about it, he'd just show up and put the kettle on.

Partway through the eavestrough story the wife's eyes had gone and she was crying quiet into her cold tea, no sound to it hardly, just her shoulders going, and nobody stopped what they were saying or made a thing of it, the eldest just reached over and put a hand flat on her back and kept right on talking to the brother like nothing, and that was somehow the kindest thing anybody could've done, letting it just sit there alongside everything else being said.

After a while it turned practical, the way it does.

The eldest: Okay, so, I'll call the cousins in the morning, the ones out west, they're on their own time anyway so it doesn't matter what hour.

The brother: I'll take care of the flowers, get them sent back or whatever you do with them after.

The friend: I can go up to the cottage this week, open her up, check the pipes didn't freeze anything, get the eavestrough looked at for real this time.

The wife: Somebody's going to have to sort the garage eventually. Not this week. Just, eventually.

The younger one: I'll do the garage. I want to, actually.

The eldest: Okay. For sure. We'll figure the rest out as it comes.

No ceremony to any of it, just names against jobs, the kind of talk that gets a family through the part right after, and then it went quiet again, comfortable this time, everybody just sitting in the one warm patch of the kitchen with the dark pressed up against the windows.

It was the friend still up when the rest were starting to nod, the two-four down to a couple cans and somebody's mickey making one last round from the glovebox out to the counter. He stood up, a little unsteady, held his can up not like he'd planned it at all.

The friend: To the guy who yelled at the door for forty years and never once actually meant it. Beauty of a man. That's it, that's the whole toast, I didn't think that far ahead.

Nobody laughed this time, but everybody lifted something, a mug, a can, the wife's cold tea, and held it there a second before they drank, and that was as much ceremony as the night was ever going to get, and it was enough.
