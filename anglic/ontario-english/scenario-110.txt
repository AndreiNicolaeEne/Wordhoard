The eldest deals the cards round the table, everybody home for the cousin's stag and doe, and the one who moved down to the city back for the weekend of it, tie still half done from the church part, sleeves rolled up now.

The eldest: So what's it actually like, then, eh. Real talk, not the version you'd give your boss.

Me: Big. Like you cannot picture how big till you're in it. You know how it's ten minutes tops from your place to anywhere in this whole town.

The eldest: Sure.

Me: Down there it's a good forty five minutes some days, hour even, just to get across. And that's if the 401's behaving itself, which it mostly isn't.

The uncle: An hour. To cross a town. What's a fella even do with an hour.

Me: Sit in it, mostly. Or take the subway, which is its own hour, just underground and you can't see why you're stopped.

Mother: And the noise of it, love. How do you even sleep.

Me: You don't, first while. Sirens all night, buddy next door's got some unit humming right through my wall. Now I don't hear any of it. Come back here where it's dead quiet and I lie there like something's wrong.

A cousin: Where do you even live down there anyway, like an apartment or what.

Me: One bedroom, up on the ninth floor. Everybody pictures it one of two ways, either some big fancy thing or a shoebox, and it's neither, it's just small. Costs about what your whole mortgage payment does though, for the one room.

Dad: Get out.

Me: Swear to God. And that's before hydro, which is its own separate nightmare on top.

The uncle: These are friendly people down there, or no.

Me: That's not really a yes or no kind of question. Nobody's rude, exactly. Everybody's sorry, sorry if they bump your elbow, sorry to a chair practically. But it's not like here where you know a fella's whole life story inside a week. Took me the better part of a year to learn my neighbour's name. Still don't know the guy two doors down, just give him a nod in the hall, that's the whole relationship there is.

The eldest: So worse then.

Me: Different, not worse. There's a lady down the hall brings me soup if I'm sick, so.

Dad: Where do you do your groceries even, is there a proper store.

Me: There's a market, few of them actually, one guy's got nothing but cheese, whole stall of it, another one's selling produce right off the back of a truck. Kind of like if you took the farmers market and put a roof on it and ran it every day of the week. Prices'll take the wind right out of you though, mind you.

A cousin: And work, how's a person find work down there even.

Me: Mostly who you know, same as anywhere, if I'm honest. Sent out I don't know how many resumes, got nothing back, then a buddy from a course put a word in and that was that. Once you're in somewhere people move around a fair bit, so you keep an ear out, keep in with people.

The eldest: You get lost much, when you first landed.

Me: Oh, for sure. First few months I was lost more than I wasn't. Took the wrong line twice, ended up somewhere I'd no business being. Third time a fella had to walk me two blocks just to point me the right way, I was that turned around. Now I could near enough get you there blindfolded. Takes a while though, learning what street runs into what, what's one way, where the construction is this month.

Mother: And the food, is it any good down there.

Me: Better some ways. You can get anything, any hour, any country's food you're in the mood for. Worse some ways too. Nobody down there's got a proper chip stand, and don't get me started on the butter tarts, everybody down there thinks they invented them and half of them go and put raisins in like that's a normal thing to do to a person.

The uncle: Milk still comes in a jug down there, or.

Me: Jug, or the carton. No bags. First time I asked somebody down there for a bag of milk they looked at me like I'd grown a second head right there in the store.

Dad: You still eating the KD like a growing boy, down there on your own.

Me: Every second night, don't tell my mother. Sorry, Mother.

Mother: I heard that.

The uncle: So is it better then, down there, or worse. Straight up, none of this different business.

Me: (laughing) That's not really a straight up question, is it.

The uncle: Chirping me now, are ya.

Me: Little bit. No, it's just, it isn't better or worse, it's just its own thing. Different kind of tired, different kind of good day. I miss going up north on the long weekends something fierce, I'll tell you that. Down there the closest thing to a cottage is a park with a lake you're not meant to swim in.

The eldest: Bring your toque, this time of year, if you're going up.

Me: Always do. Sorry, someone's gonna sneak past ya there, I gotta grab a coffee before this next hand, I'm not made of the stuff you people run on up here at six in the morning.

Dad: Beauty, that's the good stuff there, help yourself.

Mother: You gonna come back for good, though. Someday. Give it up down there and just come home.

Me: Maybe. I think about it plenty. Don't know yet, honest to God. Ask me again next visit.

Mother: I will and all.

The eldest: Deal me in already, we haven't got all night, some of us are working the euchre for real here.

Me: No worries, no worries, I'm in, deal away.

And the cards go round again, and somebody out on the porch is already into the two four somebody's uncle brought up for the weekend, and it is, for a while, exactly the same as it always was, which is its own kind of answer to the question nobody quite asked straight out and nobody quite got a straight answer to either.
