Chile, mek I tell you bout dis day, cause I ain't hardly got de strength left fa tell it proper, but I gwine try.

Dat ditch wha run long de front of all we yard, e bin a headache long before de storm ever come, but after e blow through e get bad fa true. Limb fall in dere, mud wash down off de field, and de water ain't had nowhere fa go but back up under everybody floor. Three, four house already standin in water by de time anybody say a word bout it out loud. Dat kind of job too big fa one household by e self, enty. Buckra own dat road, but buckra ain't never come fix nothing on it, so wasn't nobody gwine fix it but we self.

Was de one whose yard flood de worst first call it out. E walk de road early, bout day clean, knockin on do after do, hollerin,

The neighbor whose yard flood worst: "Oona come on out here now, we got to dig dis ditch out fo de whole road wash away. Bring you shovel, bring you rake, bring whatsoever you got."

And chile, word gone up and down dat street fasta than you could blink. Ain't need no phone call. De chirren run it porch to porch, and by de time de sun good up, everybody done yeddy bout it.

We ain't spect but eight, nine people fa show. Look like de whole settlement turn out instead, well over a dozen more than anybody count on, standin dere wid shovel and hoe and wheelbarrow, some still eatin dey breakfast right out dey hand. De work spread cross everybody dere. Some da dig from de top end, some da haul de mud out in de wheelbarrow, de young ones da cut de limb up small so e easy fa carry off. But de heaviest lift of de whole day fall to one man, big fella, e da pull dat old busted culvert pipe out de ground near bout by e self, mud up to e knee, sweat runnin off him like rain. Nobody ax him fa do it. E just tek it on, and wasn't nobody bout fa argue him out of it neither.

Partway through de mornin we run into trouble. De bank of de ditch cave in right where dey dig deepest, and it near bout bury de wheelbarrow and near bout tek a man leg wid it. Everybody holler and come runnin, and by de grace ain't nobody hurt, just mud everywhere and one shirt soaked through and a wheelbarrow bent up on de side. That set off a whole 'nother thing, cause den come de argument.

The one wid de wheelbarrow: "I tell oona dat bank too soft fa dig straight down like dat, we suppose to slope it!"

The one dat dig it: "Ain't nobody ax you how fa dig a ditch. Dis how my daddy do it and e never once cave in on him."

Voice gettin loud, one cuttin e eye at de other, and it look like de whole day bout to turn sour right dere in de mud. But de elder, de oldest one on dat street, ain't say much all mornin, just been settin close by wid a hoe cross e lap. E get up slow, walk over, and say,

The elder: "Both of oona right and both of oona wrong. Slope de top so e don't cave, keep de bottom narrow so de water run fast. Now finish talkin and finish diggin."

And chile, that was de end of it. Ain't nobody say another word bout it, dey just pick de shovel back up.

All day long de food and de drink kept comin out. One house bring a big pot of red rice, another bring sweet tea cold enough to hurt you teeth, and de tittuh from down de road bring benne cake wrap up in wax paper, settin it out on a plank table under de tree fa whosoever hungry. Ain't nobody go without dat day, and ain't nobody ax fa be paid neither, just sweet-mout and welcome all around.

De chirren had dey own little job. Dey ain't allow near de deep part, but dey da carry de small stick and de leaf off to de side, keepin de path clear fa de wheelbarrow, and dey tek it serious, like it de biggest job in de whole yard. De oldest one on dat street ain't sit dat day out neither, even though everybody tell him fa rest e self in de shade. E insist e got a job too, and end up de one settin de string line straight down de middle of de ditch so de digging stay even. Wasn't nobody bout fa tell him no.

Bout midday, somebody dig down and turn up a old cootuh, layin low in de mud like e live dere all e life. One minute wasn't nothing dere, den I shum poke e head up right out de mud, slow like e ain't got a care in de world. Somebody holler like e see a snake and jump clean back, near bout drop e shovel on e own foot backin away from a cootuh slower than de mud e crawl out of. When everybody see how slow dat thing really move, de whole street bust out laughin at de same time, laughin so hard some had fa set down right dere in de dirt. Even de elder wid de string line was laughin, and e ain't laugh easy fa nobody.

We spect fa be done by supper time. Job ain't finish till a good three hour after dat, sun already gone down, and we out dere workin by lantern and by whatsoever light left in de sky. But by de time e done, dat ditch run clear from de crossroad clean down to de landing, deep and sloped just like de elder say, and every bit of dat fallen limb hauled off de road. When de last shovel of dirt get thrown and de water start runnin free down dat ditch fa de first time all week, somebody let a cheer go up, and chile, it spread down dat street just as fast as de call did dat mornin.

Dat evenin de whole street feel different. Folk who ain't hardly speak fa month was standin round together in de dark, tired, mud dry on dey leg, still laughin bout dat cootuh. Wasn't gwine last forever, I know dat. But fa dat one evenin, dat street feel like one house, one family, wid one job done together, and can't nobody tek dat from us, enty.
