By the middle of the night the house done thin down to just we five, the closest ones, everybody else done gone on home already. The dish towel still cover the food on the table, the sweet tea done gone flat in the pitcher, and the only light left burning good is the one over the stove. Nobody studying sleep yet.

The niece the one break the quiet first, e voice low like e just thinking out loud to sheself.

The niece: Oona member how e used to leave e coffee cup right out there on the porch rail every single morning? Wouldn't never bring it in till evening. Say e forget, but e ain forget nothing in e whole life.

The eldest: Enty e was ice tea e leave out there, not no coffee. E ain drink coffee since e pressure gone bad on em, doctor tell em stop.

The niece: (laughing at sheself) Lord, you right, tittuh, I always do get that part wrong. Ice tea, sweet like cane syrup, gnat done drown in it by noon and e still gon drink it down anyway, say the gnat ain hurt nobody.

The neighbor let out a laugh e clearly ain mean to, sharp in that quiet room, and e clap e own hand over e mouth quick like e shame of it. But nobody mind it none. The widower just look down at e hand and shake e head little bit, smiling with e eye wet.

The neighbor: I sorry, I sorry. I just shum in my head, standing right there with that cup.

A little quiet fall, then the eldest pick another one up, the kind everybody in that room done hear a hundred time, so much so half of em start saying it before e even finish.

The eldest: Oona know what e used to say every time somebody come to em complaining. E'd look at you dead in you eye and say, "De tide don't wait on nobody, e wait on God alone." Say it just so, slow, like e was preaching it.

The widower: "De tide don't wait on nobody, e wait on God alone." (e say it exact, the same weight on the same word, and for a second it near sound like the voice itself in the room)

Nobody say nothing right after that. The niece wipe under e eye quick with the back of e hand.

Then the neighbor speak up again, careful, like e been holding this one back a while.

The neighbor: I gon tell oona something oona might not even know. Every winter when the shrimping been bad and my family been short, e used to slip a little money under my door at day clean, before anybody up to see em do it. Never say a word about it. I only find out was em cause I catch em one morning, walking back cross the yard in e housecoat.

The eldest: Enty! E never tell me that.

The niece: (soft) E never tell nobody nothing e ain want told.

The room go quiet again for a minute, just the icebox humming and somebody's chair creaking.

The widower break it, reaching for the plate under the towel.

The widower: Oona eat something more. Ain no sense letting this gumbo set here going to waste. Pass that plate, tittuh, and them benne cake too.

Food go round the table again, plate to plate, an excuse for hands to have something to do.

Somewhere in the passing of it the eldest and the neighbor fall into an old back and forth, quiet but with an edge still left on it from a long time ago.

The eldest: You still ain never paid me back for that net you borrow off me, ten year gone now.

The neighbor: Ten year, and you still counting it. I bring you a new one, better one, and you know it.

The eldest: Mm. (e cut e eye at the neighbor, just a flick, and let it go)

The neighbor: (letting it go too, waving e hand) Not tonight. We ain doing that tonight.

And they let it drop, easy as it come up, both of them looking back toward the widower like tonight belong to somebody else.

It the niece that bring the stubborn story, and e face already softening into it before e even start.

The niece: Oona remember when e hand start shaking on em, and every last one of we been telling em, let we help you finish that basket, let we do the coiling for you. E wouldn't hear it. Sat right there three whole day, sweetgrass and palmetto everywhere, doing every stitch e own self, slow slow, and when e finish e hold it up and say, "See there. Ain need nobody hand but mine." Hardheaded like a cootuh what won't let go a stick.

The widower: (laughing for real now, wiping e eye with the same hand) That was em all over. Couldn't tell em nothing when e mind set. Buckra doctor sheself couldn't tell em nothing.

Nobody say it right out, but it settle over the room anyway, easy as the quiet after, that that was the one thing e always be remembered by, hardhead and soft heart both together, stubborn as anything living and generous past what e ever let on.

Partway through all that the niece start crying, quiet, not making no show of it, just tears running while e still holding e cup. Nobody stop talking on account of it. The eldest just reach over and rest e hand on the niece shoulder a second, keep right on talking, and the talk move on gentle around em like water going round a stone.

The eldest: We gon need somebody fa see about the preacher first thing, and somebody fa call on the burial society bout the paper.

The widower: I'll see to the preacher. Tittuh, you and the neighbor handle what need handling with the food for after, oona good at that.

The neighbor: We'll manage it. Ain no trouble.

The eldest: And the insurance paper in the top drawer, the one with e name on it. Somebody need fa find that fore day clean.

The widower: I got it already. E showed me where long time back, say e ain want nobody hunting for it after.

Said plain, no fuss to it, the way such things get said when there ain no other way left to say them.

It the niece still awake last, everybody else gone quiet or nodding where they sit, when e lift what left in e cup, just tea gone warm now, and e don't make no speech of it, just say it soft into the room.

The niece: Well. Rest good now, you hear. Ain nobody gon leave that cup out on the rail no more, but we gon remember it just the same.

And the room hold that a minute, quiet, before somebody yawn, and the night just go on from there toward morning.
