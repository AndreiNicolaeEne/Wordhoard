My friend, leh me tell you bout a twenty dollar that never once change hand, and how it come that a cootuh end up gettin kiss in front the whole settlement down to the landing.

E start small, like most foolishness start. Me and my sister husband bin settin on the porch of an evenin, checkerboard tween us, just passin the time, no thought of nothin serious. We ain bet nothin at first, just playin fa true, movin the men back and forth. But the crowd bin standin round like dey always do when two man git to playin like dey mean it. My auntie shum from the yard and come stand by the step. My woman workmate come too, and the tittuh from cross the crick, and dey all start fa talk.

My woman workmate: Oona ain playin fa nothin, enty? Somebody oughta put something on it.

My sister husband: I ain scared to put something on it.

Me: E talkin big for a man who ain win a game off me since Christmas.

Everybody laugh at that, and the crowd start fa push it higher, one sayin put a dollar, another sayin put five, till somebody call out twenty and everybody settle on it quick, like it bin the number all along. Twenty ain enough to sting nobody bad, just enough to make a man play e best and mean it. We shake on it fore the first man even move.

Well, that game gone on a good while. My sister husband bin ahead a spell, then I catch up and pass e, then e catch back past me. The crowd da holla every time a man git jump, sayin oooh and ahh like dey watchin something fa true, not just checker. Cut-eye pass tween me and my sister husband more than once when a man git took wrong, but that all part the game, ain no harm in it.

Then it turn. E turn hard, right when I think I bin holdin my own. My sister husband see a way through I ain see comin, and e start fa clean me out, one man after e next, and ain nothin I could do bout it no more. The bet bin lost fore the last man even git took off the board, everybody could shum it comin, and when e finally happen, when my last man gone, I groan out loud and say I done lost, I lost fa true, ain no use fa pretend otherwise.

Everybody laugh and clap my sister husband cross e back, and I bin ready fa reach in my pocket fa the twenty. But my auntie hold up e hand.

My auntie: Uh uh. We ain want oona money. Oona hush and yeddy me now, cause we gon tell you zackly what it got to be, every piece of it, right now, so you can't come back after and say you ain know.

And that when dey lay it out, right there on the porch, everybody addin a piece till it bin a whole thing. E got to go down to the landing past the ole buckra field at day clean, fore anybody stirrin but dem that come fa watch. E got to git down on e knee in the pluff mud like e courtin proper. E got to bring a cootuh, a live one out the crick, and sing to e like e a bride, three verse, not two, three. E got to bring a piece of benne cake and offer it to the cootuh proper, like a gift befittin a bride. And last, e got to kiss the cootuh on e mouth, in front everybody, fore the sun git all the way up over the marsh.

I try fa talk my way out. I say leh me just pay the twenty and be done, no need fa all that foolishness. My sister husband ain have it.

My sister husband: Enty we shake on twenty? Twenty bin the number till you lost. Now the number done change. Ain no bargainin now, ain no way out of it.

Everybody laugh so hard at that I know I bin caught fa true. Dey set the day for the Sunday comin, after church let out, down to the landing, and word bin gone round the whole settlement fore the week even out. By Sunday mornin, wasn't a soul missin. My auntie dey, my sister husband dey, my woman workmate dey, the tittuh dey, even people I ain hardly know come stand on the bank in dey good clothes still from church, waitin.

I come down to the landing with a bucket, cootuh already caught the evenin before and settin quiet inside. Day bin just breakin, sun da come up slow over the marsh grass. I git down on my knee right in the mud, and I feel it come up cold and slick through my trouser, and everybody already laughin fore I even open my mouth. I lift the cootuh out and hold e up high, and I start fa sing, best I could remember an ole courtin song my mama used to hum, bout a girl sweet as benne candy, and I change the words round fa fit, singin to a cootuh like e the finest gal on the whole island. Three verse, like dey say, and by the second verse half the crowd bin singin along with me, clappin time, somebody bangin a bucket for a drum.

Then I bring out the piece of benne cake I bin savin wrapped in a cloth, and I hold it up to the cootuh mouth proper, and everybody hollerin now, sweet-moutin the cootuh right along with me, tellin e how pretty e is, how lucky e is fa git such a fine man courtin e of a Sunday mornin.

And then come the kiss. I lean in close, eyes shut, tryin fa keep a straight face and do the thing proper since I bin caught and ain no way out of it, and right when my mouth bout to touch, that cootuh pull e head clean back in e shell, quick, like e ain want no part of this foolishness neither. My mouth land right on cold empty shell, and I lose my balance in the same breath and go down flat in the pluff mud, cootuh and all, right there in front the whole settlement.

The laugh that come up off that bank, my friend, I ain never yeddy nothin like it fore nor since. My sister husband bin laughin so hard e had to set down right on the ground. My auntie bin wipin e eye with e church handkerchief. Even the tittuh, who ain say three word all mornin, bin doubled over holdin e side. That the moment, right there, that cootuh pullin e head in right when I bin ready fa the kiss and me endin up face down in the mud instead. That the moment still git brought up.

Cause e do still git brought up. Every time we all git together since, somebody bring it up again, and it done grown bigger every tellin. Now dey say the cootuh wink at me fore e pull e head in. Now dey say I sing five verse, not three. Now dey say I ain just fall in the mud, I roll clean into the crick after, cootuh and benne cake and all. Ain none of that happen fa true, but ain nobody fa correct it no more neither, cause the story done stop bein bout what happen and gone to bein bout what git told. Twenty dollar bin the wager, but e ain what nobody remember. Enty that always how e go.
