The eldest: Come on now, tell we the whole of it, start to finish. We done yeddy piece of it here and piece of it dey but never the whole thing set out proper, and the young ones need fa know.

Me: A'right, a'right, oona hush up then and let me talk, cause my husband here, e ain't gon say one word all night, e just gon set there and smile like e always do when I get to telling this.

It ain't no grand thing how we meet, oona. Ain't no dance, ain't no cousin fixing we up. I bin sorting shrimp at the shed of an evening when the boat come in, same as I do most day dat time of my life, hand full of ice and my apron done wet through. E daddy boat pull up to the dock and e come up behind e daddy carrying two basket, one on each arm, and e set em down next to me without say nothing bout who e is or nothing. I look up and dey a little cootuh done get caught up in the net wit all de shrimp, flipping e leg round trying fa get out, and 'fore I could even open my mouth fa say good evening, e look down in dat basket dead serious and say, "Enty a cootuh in dey wit de shrimp?" Like dat the only thing on e mind in de whole world. Ain't sweet-mout me one bit, ain't ask my name, just worried over dat cootuh. I laugh so hard my eye water, and e ain't crack a smile, just look at me like I the strange one for laughing. Dat right dey the thing I ain't never forget, how e come to me first time worried more bout a turtle than bout talking sweet to a girl e ain't never met.

Now I gon tell oona true, I be the hesitant one. E family from cross the creek, and e mama and my mama ain't speak good from way back, some old business bout land dat ain't even mine fa carry, but I bin carrying it anyhow, cause dat how it go. I ain't want no trouble knock on my mama door on account of no boy. So I cut-eye at him plenty dat first month, wouldn't hardly give him de time. But e come back to de shed near every evening after dat, not fa buy nothing, just fa carry my basket out to de truck, and e do dat quiet, patient, never once ask me for nothing back. Near a month e do dat before e ever open e mouth fa ask me one thing for himself. My mama shum from de porch one evening and e say to me, "A man who carry your basket a month and ain't never ask for nothing, e ain't studying trouble, e studying you." Dat wore it off me right dey.

Courting bin slow after dat, cause life ain't make it easy. E working construction cross on de mainland dat whole season, and I bin on de island wit de shed, and de last ferry don't run late, so most day we ain't even in de same place fa see one another proper. First time we ever spent time together dat wasn't just chance meeting at de dock bin a fish fry down to de church ground, and even dat near ain't happen cause e boat man near miss de crossing. But e catch dat last ferry every single evening after dat, tired as e bin from work, just fa get a hour wit me on my porch 'fore e got to turn round and go back for morning shift. We'd set out dey talking till day clean sometime, and e daddy would fuss bout it come sunup, but e ain't never miss a night e could help it.

Somewhere in dat stretch e whittle me a little cootuh out of a piece of driftwood e find down by de landing, and I bin carry dat thing in my pocket ever since, e still setting on my windowsill right now this minute, oona done shum it a hundred time, ain't no need fa me fa point it out. Dat de joke round dis whole family now, anytime somebody move slow or worry over de wrong thing at de wrong time, somebody gon call em a cootuh, and everybody know where dat come from.

I know it bin turning serious 'fore I even say it out loud to myself. I catch myself one evening saving de last piece of fish off my own plate for him without even thinking on it, just done it like e bin mine already. E find out how far gone I bin a different way though. My tittuh from down de road hear me talking to de neighbor one day and I done call him "my husband" plain out my mouth, months 'fore e ever ask me nothing, and my tittuh ain't waste no time carrying dat straight to him. E ain't say nothing to me bout it for a week, just walk round wit a look on e face, and when I ask him what ail him e finally tell me what e yeddy.

De asking itself ain't no big to-do. E ask me right dey on de dock one morning at day clean, 'fore e daddy boat gone out, still smelling of bait and diesel, crab trap still in e hand from setting em out. E ain't get down on no knee proper, just stood dey and say, "Oona gon marry me or not?" like e half-scared and half already knowing de answer. I say yeah 'fore e even finish getting de words out good.

Bout a year pass 'tween dat morning and de wedding. My mama bin glad from de start, but e mama took her time coming round, dat old business setting heavy 'tween em still. What broke it bin my mama carry a whole tin of benne cake down to e mama house one Sunday, ain't say nothing bout de past, just set it on de table and set down wit her. E mama sent a tin back de next week. Dat de whole peace treaty right dey, and neither one of dem old woman ever call it dat, but we all know what it bin.

Wedding day, what stay wit me ain't nothing dat show up in a photograph. It de smell. Tide bin low dat morning, marsh mud smelling strong de way it do, and every time I catch dat same smell even now, right dat second I back standing dey again in my mama yard waiting fa walk out.

We done weather a hard stretch since, same as any two people do, a bad storm season dat took near everything off de place one year and put we back a long while. Ain't gon dwell on it here at de table tonight, but what carry we through it bin de same thing dat bin carrying we from de start, church family round we and my husband here steady as e always bin, never one to make a big noise bout nothing, just do de next thing dat need doing.

And dat de part ain't never change, oona, from dat very first evening at de shed clean up to right dis minute. E still worry more bout every creature dat cross e path than e ever worry bout sweet-mout talk fa me, still slow and steady and quiet in e own way, still gon carry my basket 'fore I even ask him. So oona raise oona glass now, all oona, and drink to a cootuh dat wander into a shrimp net one evening and bring we two together, and to every year since.
