Me: It come up most evenings, when the work done and the porch cool down and somebody young done move here fresh, or done come back after long time off, and they yeddy the old people say don't walk down by the bend after dark, and they laugh the little laugh you laughing right now, and they ax why. That's how it always start. Somebody new want to know how come grown people scared of a piece of dirt road. That's the tale we been telling oona youngun long as I can remember, and long before that too.

I gwine tell you the way it come to me, and you gon just yeddy me now, all the way through, cause that's how the telling go. Ain't nobody break in on this one. Not never.

You don't see it first. That's the thing folks get wrong when they hear about it secondhand. Ain't no shape standing in the road, no white sheet, none of that mess they put in the picture show. What come first is a voice. You feel the air get thick and still like before rain, and then, before you see one blessed thing, you yeddy a voice. And it ain't no stranger voice either. It the voice of somebody you love. Your own mama, your own tittuh, whoever it is you'd walk into fire for, calling your name soft and sweet-mout, like they need you, like they hurt somewhere in the dark and only you can come find them.

That's the whole trick of it. E ain't there to hurt you with claw or tooth. E there fa walk you off the path, away from the road, away from any house with a light on, away from anybody could hear you holler. E want you alone. That's all e ever want.

E only come after midnight. Never before. And e only come on the nights with no moon a-tall, when the water and the sky and the trees all the same one black, and you can't tell where the road end and the field begin. Full moon night, new folks be walking that bend all night long and never yeddy nothing but crickets. Dark of the moon, though, that's e's night, that's when e da walk.

The spot it tied to, right here, is the bend where the county road dip down low by the black creek water, past where the gate to the old rice field used to stand back when the buckra still had it worked. Ain't tied to nowhere bigger than that. I done hear people try to make it into a whole big to-do, say it walk every dark road on this island, and that ain't so. It's that one bend. That one stretch of low ground by that one water. Other places got their own, and they ain't the same story, whatever people want to make of it.

I bin small, no higher than this table, first time I yeddy it, and it come from my own grandmother, who everybody round here call the eldest cause she was the oldest one still living on the place. She told it to me plain the first time, sitting right on the step shelling peas, like she was telling me how to plant benne or when to cut okra. Just fact. But when she got to the part about the voice calling your name, I seen something cross her face, and she laughed, just a little laugh, and it ain't reach her eyes a-tall.

The eldest: Chile, e can call yo name sweeter than yo own mama ever call it, enty? That's how e get you. Don't you never answer nothing call you from off in the dark till you done shum with yo own two eye who it is.

Me: That right there is the whole rule, and I'm gon come back to it.

First proof anybody give you, if you ax, is the ground itself. Right at that bend, off to the side where the old field start, there's a patch, ain't but the size of a small room, where nothing done ever grown. Not grass, not weed, not benne, not collard, nothing. Folks done try. Somebody plant there every few years, just to see, and it come up dead or don't come up a-tall. Been that way long as anybody living can remember, and long before that too, if you believe the old people, which I do.

Second proof, and this the kind that always come secondhand, somebody heard it from somebody who heard it from somebody else, is about a cootuh. Story go one of the field hands, years and years back, was walking home in the evening, still daylight, mind you, nothing spooky about the hour, and e come up on a cootuh crossing the road right at that bend. Cootuh get right to the edge of that bare patch and stop dead. Turn right back around. Wouldn't cross it. Went the long way round through the ditch instead. Now I ain't seen that myself, and the man who told me ain't seen it himself either, e got it from e uncle, but that's how it always get told, and it always the same cootuh, same spot, same turning back.

Now here the one that get told all the way through every single time, no matter who telling it. My own tittuh, my sister older than me, years back when she was a young woman still, was walking home from her aunt house late, later than she meant to be, dark of the moon and past midnight both, and she yeddy her name. Plain as day. In our own mother voice. Calling from off in the trees, off the road, toward the water.

And she started toward it. Three, four step off the road, toward that voice, before something stopped her, she never could say what, just something, and she stood still, and she turned back around and looked, and there wasn't nothing and nobody there but dark.

She tell it the same way every time, all these years, and I done yeddy her tell it more times than I can count. You push her too hard on it, ax too many question, and she'll cut-eye you quick and go quiet on the whole thing.

My tittuh: I know my own mother voice. Wasn't nobody playing no trick on me out there, wasn't no owl, wasn't my mind neither. She called me by name, plain and clear, and I took them steps before I even thought about it, cause that's what you do when your mother call you. And then something in me just stop, like a hand on my shoulder, and I turned, and wasn't nothing there. And when I got home not ten minutes later, my mother was sound asleep in her bed the whole time, ain't been nowhere near that road that night.

Me: That last part, that's the piece that make even the ones who don't believe nothing go quiet for a second. Her mother, home, asleep, the whole time, and more than one other person in that house can say the same. That's hard to laugh off.

But here the piece that gives the doubters their opening right back. It was dark of the moon, yes, but it was foggy too that night off the water, thick fog, and my tittuh will tell you herself she'd been up long hours and tired down to the bone. So the ones who want to laugh, they say, tired woman, thick fog, easy enough to think you hear what you scared of hearing. I don't argue with them too hard on that point. I just let them have it and go on.

Truth told, the story change a little depending on whose mouth it coming out of. Some tell it softer, say maybe it ain't looking to hurt nobody, just lonesome, just wanting company bad enough to lie for it. Others tell it harder, say plenty people done walk off that road and never come back a-tall, and ain't none of that ever proved either way. I done yeddy it both ways my whole life and I never minded the difference none. A story belong to whoever telling it that day.

What I mind is this. Round here, we still don't answer. Somebody holler yo name from off in the dark, off the road, off where you can't see who it coming from, you don't say one word back till you done shum with yo own eye who it is calling you. Don't matter if it sound just like yo own mama, yo own tittuh, whoever raised you. You wait. You look. You make sure.

And I'm gon tell you plain, I don't believe it, not all the way, not in the daylight part of my mind. But I ain't never broke that rule my whole life, not one time, day clean or dark of night, and I don't reckon I ever will.
