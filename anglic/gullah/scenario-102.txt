Come on in, watch ya step, dat second board been loose since fo eva. You see how de porch stand off from de road like dat? Dat's how you know we home fus ting, cuz e always somebody settin out dey of a evenin, rockin, watchin de road. Wen we bin small we'd run up dem step and de wood would talk under we foot, e know we comin fo we mama even call out.

You walk in and de front room open right up, dat's where everyting happen dat matter. Comp'ny set dey, preacher set dey when e come by, and Sunday dinner git served off de good table wat stay covered wid a cloth de rest a de week so de dust don't settle on it none. Dat room smell like cedar and ole paper, on account de Bible and de photograph dem keep on de shelf. Nobody run in dat room. Oona could hear dat in how quiet everybody git wen dey cross de door.

Now de kitchen, dat back dey, dat's a nother world altogether. E always smell like woodsmoke and rice, and unnerneat dat, benne, de grandmother parch benne seed in a black skillet mos every mornin, e say e sweeten de blood. Dat's de one habit dat never change: soon as day clean, fore de rooster half done crow, you smell dat benne startin to pop, and you know de day open. De grandmother stand dey wid her back to de door, stirrin, and she don't want nobody underfoot till she done. She'd cut-eye you quick if you come grabbin fo taste befo it ready.

We sleep tree to a room, some night four. Me and my brother had de one bed by de window, and my tittuh she share de other room wid de littlest one, cuz dat one still cry in de night and need somebody close. De grandmother had her own corner, a little room off de kitchen dat wasn't much bigger den de bed itself, but dat room belong to she and nobody else, not even to visit. Oona could go knock, but you wait fo she say come. Dat room hold all she keepin, de quilt piece, de Bible, de picture of people we never bin meet.

My own corner was under de house, back where de crawl space open up wide enough fo a child to fit but no grown to follow. I keep tings dey, a jar of marble, a broke pocketknife, a cootuh shell I find down by de creek. Wasn't nobody know bout dat place but me, and dat's how I like it.

Outside de door de yard run right down to de dirt road, swept clean every mornin wid a brush broom, no grass allowed to grow dey, dat was de style den. De chicken had dey run off to de side, and de fig tree stand by de fence, and in summer de whole yard smell like ripe fig fo true.

De neighbor dem close enough you could yeddy dey radio of an evenin, or smell dey supper cookin fore you smell yours. De one next door, she and de grandmother swap news over de fence mos every day, and enty everybody know everybody bidness up and down dat road?

Every mornin bout de same time you'd yeddy de shrimp truck comin, dat bell e ring, and de women come out dey door wid dey pot to buy fresh. And every evenin, same time again, you'd see de old man from down de road walk by wid e cane, headin to set on somebody porch, same time, same pace, like clockwork, every single day e pass.

De screen door never did hang right. E squeak and e stick and somebody always sayin dey gwine fix it, and it never got fix, not de whole time I bin dey. Dat door tell you who comin and who gone fo you even look up.

Wen de summer come, de heat lay heavy and de whole house open up, do and window bof, and you sleep to de sound of de bug outside. Wen winter come, de wind come off de water and fine e way tru every crack, and we all crowd close to de stove, and de grandmother tell story bout de old time, bout de buckra dat used to own de land, bout spirit dat walk de road at night if you ain't careful.

Right out dey in de road, dat's where all de children gather, ain't no yard big enough fo all we game. We play till day dark, hidin and seekin, jumpin rope, and de old folk set on dey porch and watch, and sometime somebody granny call out fa we to come, cuz e time, and don't nobody sass, oona just go.

De last time I clear remember bein dey, e was a evenin like any other, de grandmother on de porch shellin pea, e sweet-mout de baby to stop e frettin, and de light goin down orange over de marsh, and somebody down de road singin low, and de whole place jus settle, quiet, like it always done at dat hour. I ain't know den dat was gwine be de last one I keep whole in my mind.

De house still stand, but e ain't de same. De porch done fall in on one side, de fig tree gone, and stranger own de land now, buckra from off, come down here and build big and don't know nothin bout what bin dey befo dem. You could walk right by and never know a family bin raise up dey, generation on generation, never know a soul bin call dat place home.

But dat's how it bin, and dat's how I show you, room by room, same as if you bin standin right dey wid me in de door. Oona see it now? Dat's de place I come from.
