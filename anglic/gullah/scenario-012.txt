Mother, I got something I been holding on my chest a mighty long time, and I done decide today the day I put it down. You gwine yeddy me out, please, cause once I start I ain gwine stop till the whole a it done come out.

You member that scar on my leg, the long one run crooked from the knee down? Twenty-some year now you done ask me bout it, and every time I answer you the same lie, the one we all say back then. I tell you I bin fall off my bike by the ditch behind the church, and you believe me cause I never bin a chile to lie to you before that day. Well it never happen so. I ain never fall off no bike. I gwine tell you the true thing now, all of it, and then I gwine ask you fa forgive me for carrying it this long.

That summer me and them chirren I run with, oona know the ones, we bin down to the creek near bout every day the sun stand up hot. It had that big tree leaning out over the deep part, the one everybody father say don't climb, the one stood on what used to be buckra land before anybody could remember different. We done climb it plenty time before, but only so high, just enough to jump off the low limb and make a big splash and come up laughing. That day was different.

The eldest one look at me and say, "You scary."

Me: "I ain scary."

The eldest one: "You bin scary the last time. You back out. Oona all remember that."

And my tittuh, the one always right there beside me in everything, she cut her eye at me, just one long cut-eye, ain say a word, but I know what it mean plain as day. It mean, don't shame us again.

One of the boys: "E ain gon do it. E talk big but e ain gon do it."

I climbed. I told myself I was only gon go to where I went before, but every branch I got to, I could yeddy them down below, and pride is a hot thing in a chile chest, so I kept on. I went on past where my hands bin shaking already, past where the branch bin thin enough to bow under me, way higher than I ever meant to go, higher than any of us bin go before. I looked down and the water looked small and far and dark, and my tittuh was down there with her hand up over her mouth, and the eldest one had gone quiet for the first time all day.

The eldest one: "Come on down, then, if you scary now."

That word done it. I ain had no more sense left in me after that word. I let go.

I misjudge how far out the tree lean, and I misjudge how deep that water really was where I aimed, and when I hit I hit wrong, feet not straight, and the water knock every bit of wind clean out my body. I went under and couldn't tell up from down for a second, and then something under the water, something I ain never see coming, some old root or a piece of rusted something or maybe an old cootuh shell washed down in there, I don't know to this day, it opened my leg clean along the side. I ain even feel it cut, Mother, I just come up with the water going red round me and my leg burning like fire.

They pulled me out. My tittuh had a-hold one arm and the boy who bin talking so much had a-hold the other, and they was crying near bout as hard as I was, all of us shaking and soaked, and the blood ain stopping.

The eldest one, and the eldest one was scared now too, scared different from before: "Oona can't tell. Oona can't tell nobody what we bin do."

My tittuh: "We got to say something. Look how e bleeding."

The eldest one: "Say e fall off the bike. Say e fall off the bike by the ditch. Everybody know that ditch got rock in it. Say that."

And right there, soaked to the bone, all of us still shaking, we shook on it together, every one of us, like grown folk shaking hand on a deal. We agreed it right there in the mud before we even got our breath all the way back. I walked home holding my leg together with my own hand, and by the time I got in the yard I had the story down pat, like I bin practicing it my whole life instead of just them few minutes walking.

I told you I fell off the bike, and you ain doubt me one bit. You cleaned it out yourself, you remember, you had me lay cross your lap right there at the kitchen table and you poured that stinging stuff on it while I bit down on a rag, and the whole time you was so gentle with me, so worried, asking was the bike broke too, was I dizzy, did I hit my head. And I laid there and let you believe every word, cause the true telling would a brought punishment I couldn't stand to think on, and worse than punishment, it would a brought shame, cause a chile that back down once and then go and near bout kill theyself trying to prove different, that ain a good story to tell a mother who already bin worried bout that tree.

So the false story got told, and it got treated, and it healed over into this scar, and every person since then who ever ask me bout it, I done sweet-mout them with the same bike tale, smooth as anything, never a stumble in it. Enty that's a terrible thing, to get so good at a lie you could tell it in your sleep.

But Mother, I done carried the weight of it longer than I ever carried the cut itself. Every year that go by it get heavier, not lighter, cause it ain just about a tree and a jump no more, it about you trusting me and me letting that trust stand on a foundation that wasn't there. I done think about my tittuh too, how she done carry the same lie all this time, how the eldest one done carry it, how we all four of us made ourself a rope right there in that mud and we been holding it together ever since without a one of us letting go first.

What push me to tell it now, after all this time, is I got my own chirren coming up round water and trees same as I did, and I can't keep telling them to always come to me with the truth of a thing while I got this sitting under my own skin, a lie I been wearing like a mark of honor when really it ain nothing but stubbornness and a bad decision made worse by pride.

So here the truth, plain, no more folding it up small: I took a dare I bin too proud to walk away from, I climbed higher than any sense I had, I jumped from too high into water I ain judged right, and I cut my leg open on something under that water none of us ever seen, and then I lied to you about how it happened, and I kept that lie going more than twenty year.

I'm sorry, Mother. I'm sorry for the lie, and I'm sorry for making you tend a wound with a story that wasn't true, and I'm sorry it took me this long to put the truth in your hand instead of the tale. You always did right by me, and I ain do right by you that day, nor the hundred days after when you'd ask and I'd answer smooth and false. I'm asking you now, straight out, to forgive me for it.
