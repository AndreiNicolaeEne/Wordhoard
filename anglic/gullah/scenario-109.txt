I ain't even set my other foot on the step good when the door bust wide open, and my grandmother standing there in the frame with the porch light behind she, and she face just crumple up like she seeing a spirit.

Grandmother: ...Who dat? Who. Lord have mercy, dat ain't...

She stop, blinking, one hand coming up slow like she got to touch me fa believe it.

Grandmother: I yeddy your voice 'fore I even see you good. SHUM! Oh my sweet Jesus, I SHUM! Come here, come here to me right now, don't stand out there!

She grab me 'fore I could even set the bag down proper, and she hold on. She hold on so long I feel she shoulder shaking against me, and I ain't rightly know if it's she crying or she laughing, prob'ly both.

Grandmother: Look at you. Look at you! Oona done grow into a whole nother somebody, I ain't hardly know my own child standing at my door. I bin waiting on you since day clean, since way 'fore the sun even come up good.

Mother's voice, from back in the house: Who dat at the door, who...

Then the mother come round the corner wiping she hand on she apron and she just stop dead.

Mother: ...No. No, e ain't...

Mother: MY BABY! My baby done come home to me!

And she on me too now, both of them, arms going all which way, and the bag slide right off my shoulder onto the floor and don't nobody care, e just sit there by the door where e fall.

Mother: Eight year. Eight whole year, and now you standing right here in my house. Let me look at you, let me look...

Aunt, from the back room, coming fast, dish towel still in she hand: E here? E really here?

She come round the corner and take one look and she hand fly straight up over she mouth.

Aunt: Lord, I ain't gon cry, I ain't gon cry...

But she crying already, tears running down while she trying to laugh same time, wiping she face with the back she wrist and still can't stop.

Aunt: Look how you done fill out. You was skinny as a cootuh when you left dis house and now look at you, e done turn into a whole grown somebody.

Eldest, coming in behind, arms cross, cutting e eye at me slow and looking me up and down: Mm-mm-mm. Look at dis one. Oona think oona too fine for we now, enty?

Everybody talking now, all at once, on top each other, don't nobody waiting they turn:

Grandmother: When you get in? You eat yet? You hungry?

Mother: You call and tell me you was coming dis morning, why you ain't tell me sooner, I coulda...

Aunt: You still take you coffee black or you done change dat too?

Eldest: How long was the flight? Where your other bag, dat all you bring wit you?

Grandmother: Oona see the cousin yet? E done...

Question on top question, and I ain't could even open my mouth good 'fore the next one land on me.

Then I notice, off to the side, half-hid behind the aunt leg, a little one peeking out.

Me: Who dis right here? Look like e 'fraid a me.

Eldest: Dat's she baby. E ain't hardly know you, e da barely walk good when you left, e wasn't even born a full year.

The child just da stand there, one hand fisted up in the aunt dress, watching me with a big eye, not coming no closer, not saying nothing, just studying me like I'm a stranger walk in off the road, which I reckon to em I truly am.

Grandmother: Go on, go say hey to, go on now, e ain't gon bite you none.

But the child ain't move, just duck behind the leg a little more, and everybody laugh soft and let it be.

Me, sniffing the air: Wait now, that red rice I smell? Oona cooking red rice right dis minute?

Mother: I put it on soon as your grandmother call and say you was on your way. And I got benne cake done too, I know good and well you was gon want some the second you walk through dis door.

Me: I been thinking on dat smell for eight year straight. Ain't nothing like it nowhere else I been, I promise you dat much.

Eldest: Well set on down a minute, let we tell you everything, cause a whole heap done happen while you was gone. You know cousin done marry, enty? Married good two year now, got a baby coming 'fore Christmas.

Aunt: And you 'member uncle boy, the one used to be so little e couldn't hardly walk straight? E done grow taller than e own daddy now, e playing ball up the high school.

Mother: And old Miss down the road, she done pass, God rest she. And the family cross the creek, they done move off to town, ain't nobody living in dat house no more a-tall.

Me, shaking my head slow: I can't keep up wit all a dat. Eight year and everybody done gone and changed on me while my back was turned.

Eldest: You done change too, tittuh, don't act like you ain't.

Me: I look round dis room and some of it just like I left e, same table, same picture up on the wall, same chair grandmother always set in of a evening. But then I look out the window and dat whole lot next door where the old buckra store used to stand, e gone, e ain't there no more. And the road done pave clean out past the church now, e wasn't like dat before I left.

Grandmother: Things change, baby, but dis house don't hardly change none. I keep e just how you 'member e.

Me: I can tell. Smell the same, feel the same underfoot even. Just, some the people in it done get bigger and some done get smaller and some ain't in it a-tall no more.

Aunt: Don't say dat, don't make me start up again...

She wiping she eye again, laughing at sheself now for how she can't hardly stop.

Grandmother: Now. How long oona staying dis time? Tell me true, don't come tell me no three day and gone again like last time.

Me: I got two whole week dis time, grandmother. Ain't got to rush back so quick.

Grandmother: Two week! Lord, dat's something now, dat's really something.

Mother: Two week mean we got to do e right. I'm calling everybody, we having a whole gathering come Sunday, cook enough for the whole road to eat. I ain't playing bout dat one bit.

Eldest: You know she bin planning e in she head since 'fore you even walk through dat door good.

Mother: I got to. My child home, we celebrating, dat's all e is to e.

Grandmother, laughing, shaking she head: Cootuh in the pot, best fish somebody can catch down the creek, benne candy too, all of e. We doing dis right dis time.

Eldest, grinning, reaching to pinch my arm like old times: Remember what grandmother used to call you when you was little and wouldn't never come in from outside? "My little sweet-mout," cause you'd talk you way outta any whooping coming to you.

Grandmother, laughing: E still got the sweet mout on em, look how e sweet-talk me at the door just now 'fore e even set the bag down.

Me, laughing too: Oona ain't forget nothing, enty.

Eldest: We ain't forget one thing bout you, not one.

And for just a little bit right there, the whole room gone quiet all at once, don't nobody saying nothing, just standing there in the front room looking on me and me looking back on all of them, grandmother with she hand still resting on my arm like she making sure I real and not gon disappear, the aunt wiping she face one more time, the little one still peeking round the leg but not looking so scared no more, and outside the window the evening light coming in low and yellow cross the floor.

Then grandmother pat my arm and turn toward the kitchen.

Grandmother: Well. Bag can set right there by the door long as e need to. Come on in the kitchen fa eat something, you must be starving after all dat traveling you done.

Mother: Yeah, come on, set down, tell us everything proper now, ain't no need fa everybody hollering at once no more.

And just like that the fuss ease up some, everybody moving toward the kitchen table together, the little one finally letting go the aunt dress and trailing along behind, close but not too close, and the bag stay right there by the door like grandmother say, forgotten a while longer yet, cause ain't nothing setting inside e matter more than dis right here.
