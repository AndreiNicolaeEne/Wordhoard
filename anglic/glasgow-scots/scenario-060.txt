Right, so it wis the grannie's hoose we were clearin the day, the wan up the auld tenement stair she'd had since afore ony of us wis born, and she'd been deid since the spring, near enough four month noo, and it had jist sat there empty wi the leccy aff and the windaes gaun aw manky, and naebody had the heart tae dae onything wi it till the whole clan got theirsels thegither on the wan day. No jist us either, mind. The aunties came, the uncles, ma cousins and their weans forby, a right stooshie of folk trailin up they stairs wi bin bags and cardboard boxes fae aboot nine in the mornin, because the hoose wis gettin sellt, there wis nae way roond it, naebody had the money tae keep it on and naebody wis gonnae live in it, so that wis that.

We started in the hall, which didnae take long, jist coats and a wee table wi nothin on it but a dish for keys that hadnae seen a key in years. Intae the front room efter that, and it wis the mother who found the first thing, doon the side of the auld chair, a photie gone aw broon at the edges of the grannie as a lassie, standin ootside a shop that wisnae there nae mair.

The mother: Ohhh, look at this. She's the spit of oor youngest there, is she no.

The aunt: Gie's a look. God, aye, that's her arright. She'd have been aboot fourteen there, I'd say.

The mother went right quiet efter that, jist sat on the airm of the chair haudin the photie, and naebody rushed her, ye jist let a body have that.

Then intae the kitchen, and this is where it got strange, because in among the tins and the auld biscuit boxes there wis a wee brass key, no big, aboot the size of yer thumb, and it didnae fit onythin in the hoose that onybody could find. Naebody kent whit it wis for.

The uncle: Whit's this fae, then. Onybody?

The eldest: No a scooby. It's no fur the front door, I tried it.

The uncle: Might be fur a case or somethin. A wee jewellery box, mibbe.

The eldest: We never had a jewellery box.

Naebody could place it, and it went intae a poke wi the other odds and ends, unsolved, and that wis that, a wee mystery naebody would ever get tae the bottom of noo.

The good room wis next, and that's where the argument started, over the sideboard, the big dark yin wi the glass front that had sat there since afore ony of us mind.

The aunt: We're no throwin that oot, it's solid wood that, they don't make them like that nae mair.

The uncle: Naebody's room's big enough for it but. Whaur's it meant tae go?

The aunt: Mine. I'll take it.

The uncle: Ye said that aboot the clock an aw.

The aunt: Aye well I'm sayin it aboot this an aw. It's goin tae mine and that's the end of it.

And that settled it, naebody argued back, she'd made up her mind and that wis her mother's sideboard efter aw, so it went doon the stair wi four of them cursin and sweatin ower it on the landin turns.

The disagreement that didnae settle sae easy wis ower the wally dugs on the mantelpiece, they wee china dogs that sat there since forever.

The mother: They're gaun in the skip, they're hideous.

The aunt: They are not gaun in ony skip, they were her mother's afore her, they've been in this family a hunner year.

The mother: They're chipped and they're ugly and naebody wants them on their mantelpiece, be honest.

The aunt: I want them.

The mother: Ye said yersel ye couldnae stand them.

The aunt: Disnae matter whit I said, they're no gettin binned.

That wan went back and forth a good while, up and doon the room, and it never did get properly settled, they jist got wrapped in a tea towel and put in a box marked "sort later" which everybody kent meant naebody would ever sort it.

It wis under the flair in the back bedroom that the hidden thing turned up, a loose board the eldest's fit went right through, and under it a wee tin box wi a stack of letters in it, tied wi string, and nane of them had ever heard a word aboot ony of it.

The eldest: Here, come and see this. There's a hale box of letters under here.

The uncle: Whit dae ye mean under there, under the flair?

The eldest: Aye, hidden, look, somebody put a board back doon ower it an aw.

They didnae read them there and then, jist sat looking at each other, aw a bit stunned, wonderin whit their mother or grannie had gone and hidden fae the lot of them, and that box went straight in somebody's bag tae be gone through proper, later, quiet, no wi the hale hoose watchin.

The back bedroom took the langest of ony room by a mile, because it wis fu tae the ceiling, boxes on boxes, and every second thing somebody pulled oot had somebody else sayin haud on, whit's that, and it turned intae less a clear-oot and mair a hale afternoon of rakin through a life, cardigans and knittin patterns and a hunner photies and papers that naebody could thraw awa withoot lookin at first.

By the middle of the day everybody wis foundert, so we stopped and ate thegither, sat roon on boxes and the flair because the chairs were aw wrapped up or away doon the stair already, sandwiches somebody's wean had went and got fae the shop in a poke, and a big pot of tea gaun roond, and for a wee while naebody talked aboot ony of it, jist sat, and it wis the first time the hale day the hoose felt like it used tae feel, full of folk and noise and weans greetin somewhere doon the hall ower a toy.

The three piece suite in the front room, naebody wanted that in the end, too big for onybody's flat and the fabric wis awfy, so that got carried doon and left for the men tae take away, and there wis a wee moment on the pavement where everybody stood and looked at it sittin there in the daylight, oot the hoose for the first time in decades, lookin awfy sad and shabby oot in the street like that, and naebody said much, jist went back up.

The clock on the mantelpiece, mind, naebody argued ower that ain at aw. The eldest jist said she'd take it, and everybody said aye, fine, right enough, because it had been her that wound it every Sunday for the grannie these last few years, and that wis that decided in aboot two seconds flat, nae stooshie, nae fuss.

The last room tae go wis the wee bedroom at the back, the box room, and by the time that wis done, near five o'clock, every room in the hoose wis empty, bare flairs, marks on the waws where the pictures had hung for years, and it's an awfy strange feelin, a hoose wi naethin in it, aw they echoes.

We divided up whit wis left oot on the front room flair, the last of the odds and ends, everybody takin a photie here, a bit of china there, the aunt got her dugs efter aw, naebody had the heart tae argue it further by that point, and the wee brass key went hame wi the eldest, still a mystery, still unsolved, gonnae sit in a drawer somewhere the rest of its days probably.

Then the mother went roond every room wan last time, jist tae check, and locked the door, and stood a wee minute wi her haun still on it afore she turned away, and that wis the hoose closed for good, and we aw went doon they stairs the gether wan last time and oot intae the street, and that wis that.
