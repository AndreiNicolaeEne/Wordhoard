It started cause we were bursting oot the place, plain and simple. Three weans and the two of us in a hoose that was fine for a wee family but no fine for what we'd turned intae. The eldest was sleeping in whit used tae be a cupboard, I'm no exaggerating, and every morning it was like getting dressed in a phone box. So one night at the table the wife says to me, that's it, we're no living like this another winter, we're building oot the back and up the side, and that was that, decided in aboot ten minutes ower a cup of tea gone cold.

We sat and worked it oot on the back of an envelope, the two of us, how many folk we'd need and how long it would take. I says, right, you, me, my brother at the weekends, maybes a joiner for the tricky bits, four months tops, done by the summer. The wife just looked at me. Four months, she says. Aye. She was right and I was wrong, cause it ended up taking the best part of eleven month all in, and by the time it was done there'd been six different folk through that garden at one point or another, no just the four I'd counted on my fingers that night.

The very first wrong turn came within the first fortnight and it near broke my heart. We'd marked oot the foundations and started digging and my brother says to me, that's no deep enough, that's away too shallow for this ground, and I says away and don't be daft, I've done this before. Two weeks of digging, barrowing, all of it, undone and started again. I could have greeted, so I could. The wife just handed me the spade and says, less time moaning, more time digging, hen, we've no got a fortnight to spare feeling sorry for yersel.

Funny thing was, the day extra hands turned up was one of the best days of the whole job and naebody had even asked for them. My brother-in-law and his two mates just appeared at the gate one Saturday morning wi a flask and their auld overalls on, and my brother-in-law says, heard yese were stuck on the block work, we're no doing anything the day, and that was that, six pair of hands instead of two for the whole weekend. Never forget it. That's just how it works round here, ye don't ask, folk just come.

Then the weather turned on us and that was near the whole of a week gone, dreich the entire time, no the kind of rain that lets up for an hour so ye can get something done, the kind that just sits ower ye grey and endless. Everything sitting under tarps going nowhere, materials getting drookit even under cover, and me pure crabbit the whole week cause every day lost was a day I could feel ticking away at the summer we were meant tae be finished by.

Ran oot of blocks halfway through the side wall an aw, cause the second delivery got the order wrong, so we made do wi what we had, mixed in some of the auld reclaimed ones fae the original hoose that had been sitting stacked at the back of the garden since we started. Looks a wee bit different if ye know where tae look, a slight change in the colour aboot four courses up, but naebody notices unless ye point it oot tae them, and I quite like it noo, it's like the auld hoose is still in there somewhere, haudin up the new bit.

Had a right scare an aw, near the middle of it. I was up on the scaffold doing the ridge and my foot went fae under me, and I came doon aboot eight foot, landed on a pile of sand my mate had left there that morning, thank god, cause if that hadnae been there I'd have hit the concrete path. Just lay there for a second wi the wind knocked oot of me and my mate's face looking doon at me white as a sheet. Ye alright, he's shouting, ye alright. Aye, aye, I'm fine, just gie us a minute, and I got up shaking like a leaf, nothing broken, just bruised aw doon one side and shook up something terrible. The wife wasnae even there for it and she still goes on aboot it noo, says every time I go up a ladder in this hoose she thinks aboot that sand pile.

In the end we did it my way and I've never once regretted it, and even he admitted after, when the eldest came flying doon there one morning and stopped dead on the landing instead of going straight through the wall, aye, that was probably the right call.

Worst bit though was the gable wall. Stood there looking at a pile of rubble that used tae be a wall and thinking, is this ever gonnae be finished.

But then the day the frame went up, that was the turn, that was the day it aw started tae feel real. Stood there in the garden wi the wife and the weans, looking up at the timber frame against the sky where there used tae be nothing but grass, and naebody said much, we just stood there. The wife took my hand and squeezed it and that was worth mair tae me than anybody telling me well done.

The last four nights before it was finished we were oot there past dark every one of them, working under a work light strung fae the fence, freezing, hands numb, but that near-done feeling carrying us through it. My mate came back for those last nights an aw, no even asked that time either, just turned up wi his toolbox and says, we're finishing this the night whether it kills us.

And then it was done. Just like that, one Sunday, standing back looking at it wi the last bit of paint still wet, and it was finished, actually finished, efter aw that.

Counted it up efter. Eleven months fae the first spade in the ground tae the last coat of paint, and six different folk through that garden helping at one point or another, no counting the wife and me that never left it. Some weeks it felt like it would never end and some days it felt like the whole scheme was in and oot that garden.

I stand in that new bit noo of an evening, the weans running aboot daft in the space they never used tae have, and I think aboot the digging done twice and the rain and the wall that had tae come doon and go back up, and it disnae feel like a hoose tae me, it feels like the whole eleven month of it is standing there roond me every time I walk in.
