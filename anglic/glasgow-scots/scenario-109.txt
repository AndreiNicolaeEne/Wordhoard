The key wouldnae even go in right, my hand was that shaky, and before I got it turned the door was open frae the inside and there she was.

The mother: Is that, naw. Naw, is that you?

She just stood there a second, haundin on the door like it was haudin her up, and then it went fae her face no kennin me tae greetin all at once, and she near knocked me back doon the step wi the hug of it.

The mother: Aw son, aw ma wee son, get in here, get in here tae me.

She didnae let go. Went on that long the eldest had tae near prise her aff.

The eldest: Mammy, let the poor sowel breathe, he's only just in the door.

The father come through fae the living room wi his reading glesses still on his heid and stood there a second just looking, like he was checking it was really me and no some eejit come tae the wrong hoose.

The father: Well would ye look at the size of him. Whit have they been feedin ye, hen, ye've filled oot something rotten.

The mother: He has that. Look at they shoulders on him. Ye left here a laddie and ye've come back a man, so ye have.

The eldest: Aye and taller an aw, I swear ye were never taller than me and now look at ye, ya cheeky get.

Then it all went at once, naebody waiting their turn, questions fae every side of me like weans at Christmas.

The father: How was the flight, was it long? Did they no feed ye on it? Whit's the weather like ower there, is it as hot as they say?

The eldest: Naw never mind the flight, is your back still bad? Ye said in the letter your back was bad.

The mother: Are ye hungry, ye must be hungry, sit doon, sit doon, I'll get the kettle on. Naw wait, are ye needin something stronger than tea after aw that?

The auntie had come ben fae the kitchen wi a tea towel still in her hand and stood in the doorway greetin, no even trying tae hide it, well, trying and no managing it.

The auntie: Don't mind me, don't mind me, I'm fine, I'm just, eight years, eight years and here ye are stood in the hall like it was nothing.

The mother: Away and get the man a seat afore ye flood the place, ya big saftie.

The auntie: I cannae help it, I'm no like youse hard-hearted lot, some of us has feelings.

There was a wee yin hinging back by the kitchen door, half hid ahint the auntie's leg, watching us aw wi big eyes and no saying a word. I didnae even clock at first who it was.

The mother: That's her, that's your cousin's lassie, she wouldnae mind ye, she was only a baby when ye went.

The wee one: (said nothing, just keeked oot fae behind the auntie's arm)

The eldest: Away on, go and say hello, he's no gonnae bite ye. This is your, well, he's family, hen, come and gie him a wave at least.

She gave a wee wave and went straight back intae hiding, and everybody laughed, and that seemed tae break something loose in the room, like the air went easier after it.

The father: The bags, son, are the bags no coming in, are ye leaving them oot on the step aw night?

The mother: Leave the bags, whit's the hurry, the bags'll no run away. Sit doon.

Naebody moved them. They sat there by the door the whole first hour, forgot aboot completely, while the mother went through tae put the kettle on and I could smell it afore I even seen it, that smell that's in every wall of that hoose, the same washing powder, the same something under it I never could name, and it hit me that hard I nearly had tae sit doon myself.

Me: God, it even smells the same. That's mental, that is. Eight years and it's exactly the same smell.

The mother: (shouting through fae the kitchen) That'll be the soup, I've had soup on since this morning, I said tae masel he's no walking intae a cold hoose.

The eldest: It's no the soup, it's just the hoose, it's always smelled like that, ye've just forgot whit normal smells like.

Me: Aye, maybes. It's good but. It's dead good.

The father settled himself doon in his chair, the same chair, and started right in wi the news, like he'd been saving it up for years just for this minute.

The father: Right, ye'll no believe whit's happened since ye've been away. Your cousin's married, did they tell ye that? Married a lassie fae ower the water, lovely lassie, ye'll meet her.

The eldest: And the wee yin there, her mammy's had another one since, a laddie this time, so that's two of them running aboot the place noo.

The auntie: And your uncle's moved, he's away oot tae the scheme on the other side noo, says it's quieter, I says tae him quiet's aw right till ye're bored oot your mind.

The mother: And auld yin next door's away, God rest her, that was a sad business, but her hoose has new folk in it noo, seem decent enough.

It come at me that fast I could hardly haud it aw, names and faces I hadnae thought aboot in years aw getting stitched back intae the picture at once, and I just sat there nodding, trying tae keep up.

Me: That's a lot tae take in, so it is. I'm away eight year and youse have gone and rearranged the whole street on me.

The father: Aye well, that's whit happens, son, the world doesnae stop just cause ye're no in it.

Me: I can see that awright. It's mad, but, coming up the road, half of it's exactly the same and half of it I didnae even recognise. The wee shop on the corner's shut, that was there my whole life. But the park's still the park, still looks the same, still smells of the same dampness.

The mother: Aye it's a state that park, dreich as anything, they never cut the grass.

Me: And the close still has that same crack in the step, I nearly went right ower it just noo, forgot it was there and forgot it was still there aw at once, if that makes sense.

The eldest: It makes sense tae me. Naebody's fixed a thing in this street in twenty year.

The mother: Right, that's enough news for one night, the man's just in the door. How long have we got ye for this time, hen, are ye away again next week or whit?

There was a wee quiet after she said that, everybody sort of leaning in for the answer, even the wee one keeked oot again tae hear it.

Me: A fortnight this time. Maybe longer if I can swing it.

The mother: A fortnight! Right, that's it, we're having everybody roond. I'm no having ye here a fortnight and it just being us, your gran'll want tae see ye, your cousin and his new wife, the lot of them. Sunday. We'll dae a big dinner Sunday, aw of us.

The auntie: I'll dae a pot of soup for it an aw, seeing as it went doon that well the day.

The father: Ye'll need messages for that, a whole poke of messages, mibbe two.

The mother: Aye well you can go for they messages the morra, I'm no lifting a finger the day, I've done nothing but greet since he walked in.

The eldest: Mind whit ye used tae call him, when he was wee? Ye've no said it yet.

The mother: (laughing, wiping her eyes again) Aye, come here, ya wee scunner, gie's another cuddle afore I start greetin again.

And naebody had tae explain that tae onybody, it just went roond the room like it had never stopped being said, and for a second there naebody spoke at aw, just aw of us sat looking at each other, the fire going, the soup smell still hinging in the air, the bags still sat forgot by the door, and it felt like the whole hoose had been haudin its breath and only noo let it oot. Then the wee one come oot fae ahint the auntie's leg at last and sat doon on the flair right next tae my feet, no saying a word, just there, and the mother got up tae see tae the soup, and it aw settled intae something quieter, something that felt, for the first time since I'd come up that road, like just being hame.
