See it wisnae even close, this year's harvest oan the allotment. Every year ye keep a wee note in the jotter - whit came aff the plot, how many bags, how many stane o tatties - and this year fae the first shoots ye could tell it wis gonnae leave the rest away behind. By June it wis clear as onything: near double whit we'd ever pulled afore.

But it wis the tatties that done it. Every other year ye're happy wi a good few bags, naw bother, but this year the drills gied up that much we ran oot ae boxes tae put them in. The auld best, years back, wis when the wean's granda still hud the plot hissel, and even that yield got dwarfed intae the ground next tae this yin.

Had tae get extra hands doon tae help wi it aw, so it didnae sit and turn in the ground afore we got roon tae it. My workmate came doon two Setterdays runnin, and the wee yin fae along the row wis oot there in her wellies giein us a haun an aw. Once the hoose wis stocked up for the winter, the rest went spare - some intae sacks for the freezer, some gied away roon the neighbours, some swapped fur a poke ae onions aff the man twa plots doon.

The oldest grower on the site, been there since afore any ae us wis even born, stood at the fence lookin at it aw and just shook his heid.

The oldest grower: In aw my years on this plot, hen, I've never seen the likes ae it. No even close.

Asked whit he put it doon tae, he says it wis the rain comin at the right time, no too much, no too wee, and a mild stretch right through the season that never let onything sit and rot in the ground.

Folk still talk aboot it noo. Every year since, good or bad, gets measured against it - aye, that wis a good yield right enough, hen, but wis it as good as that year. It wisnae, and everybody kens it wisnae.
