She sat the two ay them doon on the settee, close in aside her, afore she said a word ataw. Nane ay the usual patter, nane ay "right, whit huv yese been up tae" - just quiet, and that in itsel telt them somethin wis comin.

The mother: Right, come here tae me, the both ay youse. I've something I need tae tell yese, and I need yese tae listen close.

The eldest: Whit is it? Is somebody in trouble?

The mother: No, hen, naebody's in trouble. It's yer granda. He died this mornin.

The eldest: But... whit does that even mean but? Like, is he no comin back? No ever?

The mother: No, son. When somebody dies their body just stops, it disnae work nae mair, and they're no here wi us the way they used tae be. It's no like sleepin and it's no like when he's away for a while and comes back. He's gone, and that's the sad, hard truth ay it. It's aw right tae feel rotten aboot it, so it is, I feel rotten aboot it masel.

The youngest: Is he cauld?

The mother: Aye, wee yin, his body's cauld now, but he's no feelin nothin. He's no feart nor sair nor nothin at aw. Come here tae me, the pair ay yese.

She pulled the two ay them in close, tight intae her, the wee one's heid against her shoulder.

The mother: Ma wee pet. I know, I know.

The youngest: Will we see him again but?

The mother: No the way we're used tae seein him, hen. No here. That's the honest truth ay it and I'll no tell yese ony different. But we'll mind him every single day, and that's a kind ay seein him tae, in its ain way.

The mother: Right, now listen. Ower the next wee while there's gonnae be folk comin roon the hoose, and we'll go and see him afore they take him away, and there'll be a funeral efter, so youse'll see aw the family thegither. I'll tell yese every bit ay it as it comes so nane ay it jumps oot at yese oot the blue. Away and play now, the both ay yese, and come tae me onytime yese need tae, awright?
