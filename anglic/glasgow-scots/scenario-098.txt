Right, so this is the story the way it's aye been telt in oor family, the way my granny telt it tae my mother and my mother telt it tae me, sat at the table wi a poke of messages between us and the weans greeting through in the next room because naebody would gie them their tea early enough.

Before ony of us was here, the family was ower the water, on a wee bit of land that never gied them enough back for whit they put intae it. It wisnae a bad life exactly, just a hard yin, the kind where ye're aye one bad harvest away frae naethin. And then they got that one bad harvest, and then another, and the rent still wanted paid regardless, and the man that owned the land didnae care whit the ground could or couldnae dae. That was the push, plain as that. No steady work tae be had, no food tae be sure of, and a landlord that would as soon put ye oot as look at ye.

My granny used tae say it like this.

Granny: There wisnae a decision in it, hen. It wisnae like sittin doon and weighin it up. It was go or starve, and folk don't sit lang ower a choice like that.

So it was the eldest brother that went first, my great-great-grandfaither, though naebody in the family calls him that, he's just "him that went first" even noo. Went on his lone, years afore any the rest followed, left a wife and a wean ahint him wi a promise he'd send for them, which is a big thing tae promise when ye've naethin in yer pocket and nae idea whit's on the other side of that water.

The crossing was the hard bit, and it's the bit that gets telt slowest, the way ye'd tell somethin ye don't want tae rush through. Packed doon in the boat wi hunners of other folk aw in the same state, naethin tae eat that was worth caain food, sick the hale way, weeks of it. And there was a cousin on that boat and all, went ower wi him, and that cousin didnae make it. Died on the crossin. Naebody in the family says the name of that cousin tae this day, it's just "the cousin that didnae survive it," said quiet, and ye know no tae ask mair.

The mother:
That's aw that's ever said aboot it, love. There wis a cousin, and the cousin died, and that's the end of whit gets said.

He landed here wi next tae naethin. No money, no work lined up, nae place of his ain tae go. A family that had come across afore him, no even kin, just folk frae roughly the same airt, took him in and gied him a corner tae sleep in, a borrowed roof ower his heid till he could stand on his ain feet. That's how it went in they days, ye took in whae needed takin in, because ye'd been the one needin it yersel not that long afore.

There wisnae steady work at the start, no even close. It was a day here, a day there, whitever was gaun, carryin this, liftin that, standin at a gate hopin tae be picked for a shift. Cauld, drookit maist days, comin hame peely-wally and done in, and still nae guarantee the morra would bring onythin either. That went on lang eneuch that he must have wondered mair than once if he'd made a right eejit of himsel comin ower at aw.

But then the work came, doon by the yards, and once it came it stuck. He took up a trade there and he kept it for the rest of his days, and it was passed doon after him and all, faither tae son, the way trades used tae go. That was the turn, the point where things stopped bein day tae day and started bein somethin ye could build a life on.

Soon as there was enough saved, put by bit by bit, he sent word back for the wife and the wean tae come ower and all. It was the guts of two year afore that happened, two year of him here his lone and them there waitin, which is a lang time tae be a family split doon the middle like that, letters gaun back and forth slow as onythin, and nae way of knowin how the other half of ye was gettin on in between.

The eldest:
Two year's a lang time when ye're wee. She telt me she could hardly mind whit he looked like by the time the boat came in for them.

When they did come ower, it was a rough crossin for them and all, though no as bad as the first yin, thank god, naebody lost that time. And they landed intae the same next tae naethin he'd landed intae, and the same rooms borrowed aff other folk till they got their ain feet under them, till there was a place found that was theirs tae rent, a room and a kitchen, no grand, but theirs, and they settled intae it within they first few years and never moved on frae it.

There's one thing that come ower on that first crossin and it's still kept in the family the day. A wee tin box, nothin tae look at, had her needles and thread in it, the mother's, and it come across in her pocket the hale way and it's sat in a drawer in every hoose this family's ever had since. Naebody uses it for needles noo, but naebody would ever think of pittin it oot either. It just sits there, and every so often somebody'll lift it and haud it a meenit and no say much.

Me: How'd ye know that's the same box, but? After aw this time?

The mother: Because it's aye been said it's the same box, hen. That's how ye know.

Weans came, in time, right there in they same rooms, and that's the bit my granny aye said was the real turn of it, mair than the trade even, mair than the room finally bein theirs. She'd say it wisnae a hame till there was a wean born in it. Afore that it was just a place they were stoppin, whitever they cried it. Once a wean was born there, that was it, that was hame, and there was nae mair talk of the auld place bar the tellin of it, which is a different thing athegither frae livin it.

And that's how it's aye come doon tae us, one tellin at a time, sat roond a table or in a kitchen somewhere, whoever's listenin that day gettin it a wee bit different depending on whae's daein the tellin, but the bones of it never change. The bad land, the man that went first his lone, the cousin that's never named, the crossin that took the guts of two year tae close up again, the tin box in the drawer, the trade that stuck, the weans that made it hame.

I mind sittin as a wean masel, gey wee, listenin tae my granny gaun through it, and no really taking in the half of it, just likin the sound of her voice gaun on and the poke of sweeties gettin passed roond while she talked. It's only years efter that ye realise whit ye were actually bein telt, and that noo it's yours tae tell and all, whether ye're ready for that or no.

The mother: And when you've weans of yer ain, hen, you'll tell it tae them. That's aw it is. That's the hale job of it.

Me: Aye. I know.
