Sit doon a meenit, hen, ye'll no believe the day of it. That big auld tree at the tap o the back court, the yin's been there since afore any of us wis born, came doon in the night wi the wind, roots and aw, right across the lane and hauf intae twa back gairdens forbye. Naebody could get a motor in or oot, the bins couldnae be lifted, and it wis that big and that heavy there wis no wan hoose gonnae shift it their lane. Wisnae a wee joab, I'm tellin ye. Too big for wan family, no even close.

It wis the neighbour twa doors up that seen it first, oot for her messages first thing wi her poke still in her hand, and she near dropped the lot wi the fright of it. Didnae even go back in for her coat, just stood there in the street shoutin up and doon till windaes started openin wan efter another. You never seen word travel that fast in yer life. Fifteen meenits, mibbe less, and there wis folk comin oot the closes in their slippers still, hauf-drookit fae the rain that hadnae even stapped yet.

We aw thought it'd be the same wee handful that's aye up for onything, but naw. By the time the clock got roond tae nine there wis weel ower a dozen mair folk staunin aboot that tree than onybody had reckoned on, and a fair few of them we'd nivir have guessed. The joiner fae the end block brought his big saw doon, the postie left his bag at somebody's door and rolled up his sleeves, even the fella that never says two words tae onybody wis oot haudin a rope like he'd done it aw his days.

It got sortit quick enough efter that, whae wis daein whit. The weans wur set tae gaitherin up the wee branches and the leaves intae bags, that gallus aboot it, in among the men's feet the hale time and no feart of onybody. Some wur haudin ropes, some wur clearin oot the gairdens where the branches had landed, some wis away for mair tools, and it wis the joiner's brother that took on the heaviest of it aw, sawin through the trunk itself near the hale mornin, drenched through his jaicket even though it wis that dreich a day ye'd have thought he'd be droont afore he wis done.

Aboot midday there wis a right stooshie. A big bough that everybody thought wis sound gied way aw of a sudden, skited across and took a hale section of somebody's fence wi it, near had the joiner's brother's heid if he hadnae jumped clear in time. He wis staunin there efter wi a right glaikit look on his face, no even a scratch on him but white as a sheet. That set aff a right argument, twa of the men squarin up ower whether tae cut the rest doon fae the tap or roll the trunk hale doon the lane first, wan cryin the other a bampot for even suggestin it, "whit dae ye think yer daein, ya eejit, that'll roll straight intae the wall," till the postie got in atween them and telt them baith they'd end up deid if they didnae calm doon and think aboot it. That shut it, mair or less, though there wis a face or twa gey crabbit aboot it for a good while efter.

The food stertit comin oot no long efter that. My mother brought oot soup in the big pot and a stack of rolls, and the wumman fae the corner hoose brought a flask of tea big enough for the hale street twice ower, and later on somebody's da appeared wi a poke of cans for the men that wanted wan efter aw that liftin. Naebody went hungry the day, that's for sure, there wis mair food than the joab needed if I'm honest wi ye.

The auld boy fae the grund flair, must be near ninety if he's a day, widnae hear of sittin it oot on his chair at the windae like everybody wanted him tae. Insisted he'd tae have a joab tae dae the same as onybody else, so they gied him the traffic, staunin at the corner wi his stick tellin motors tae go roond, and he took tae it like it wis the maist important joab of the hale day, widnae let onybody near it aff him. Peely-wally lookin by the end, mind, but he stuck it oot tae the last.

The moment everybody wis laughin thegither wis when somebody's dug got a hold of a branch still hingin wi leaves and widnae let go, drug the poor beast hauf doon the lane afore it finally gied up, stood there lookin that pleased wi itself, tail gaun like the clappers. Even the men that wur still crabbit fae the argument couldnae keep a straight face at that. The auld boy at the corner wis laughin that hard he'd tae haud ontae his stick wi baith hands.

By the time it wis aw done it wis a good three hours later than onybody had thought it'd take, the light nearly awa and folk's hands that cauld and sair they could hardly feel them. But the tree wis gone, sawed intae bits and stacked at the side for whaever wanted it for the fire, the lane clear enough for a motor tae get through again, the twa gairdens tidied back up as best onybody could manage, the fence propped though it'll need a proper mend the morra, and every last branch and leaf bagged up and away. When the last big bit got hoisted ontae the joiner's brother's van there wis a cheer went up fae everybody staunin there, the weans jumpin aboot like they'd won something aff the pools, and even folk that hadnae liftit a finger aw day came oot their doors tae clap.

The street wis different that night, I'll tell ye that for nothing. Folk that hardly say twa words tae each other on a normal day wur staunin aboot the closes efter, blethering away like auld pals, naebody in ony hurry tae go in oot the cauld. It'll no last, mind, by the morra it'll be back tae normal, everybody keepin tae theirsels again the same as ever. But the night, for a wee while there, it felt like the hale street belonged tae the wan family.

That's how I'm that done in the night, hen. I'm away tae my bed.
