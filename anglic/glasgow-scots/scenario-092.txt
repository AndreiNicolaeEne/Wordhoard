By hauf twelve the hoose had thinned right doon tae just us five, sitting roond the wee table wi the big light aff and just the lamp on, everybody else away hame lang since. The mother had her cardigan pulled tight even though it wasnae cauld, and naebody had the heart tae say we should aw away tae oor beds. The teapot had gone cauld twice and naebody had bothered tae boil it again.

It was the mother that started it, quiet, no even really talking tae anybody in particular.

The mother: Dae yous mind he used tae stand at that windae there every single morning wi his tea, and he'd no even be looking at anything, just standing.

The eldest: Naw but it wasnae that windae, it was the kitchen one. You're aye getting that wrong.

The mother: Am I no. See, I always say the front windae and it's the back yin. Every time.

She laughed at hersel, a wee tired laugh, and shook her heid.

The mother: I've been telling that story for twenty year and getting it wrong the whole time.

The auntie let a laugh oot then, sudden, too loud for the room, and clapped her hand ower her mooth right away like she'd done something wrong. Naebody minded. The neighbour even smiled at it.

The auntie: Sorry, sorry, it's no funny, it's just, the windae. Of course it's the windae that's wrong and no anything else aboot the story.

A wee bit of quiet after that, comfortable enough, and then the neighbour spoke up, leaning forward wi his hands roond a mug that had nothing left in it.

The neighbour: Dae yous mind how he used tae say it though. Every single time, disnae matter whit it was aboot, "aw it'll dae, it'll dae." Kettle wi a crack in it, coat wi nae buttons left, didnae matter. "It'll dae."

Me: Aw it'll dae, it'll dae, he'd say it just like that, wi the wee shake of the heid.

I said it exactly how he used tae say it, and for a second it was like he was in the room again, and naebody said anything for a moment, just letting it sit there.

The eldest: Right, here's one no everybody's gonnae ken. When ma was in the hospital that time, years back, he never told any of us this but I found it oot after, he went and slept in the car park three nights running so he'd be first in when they opened the doors. Never said a word aboot it tae anybody.

The auntie: He never!

The eldest: He did. I only found oot fae the man at the barrier, he mind't him fae the night shift.

The auntie: I never knew that. Aw ma God.

It went quiet again after that, a proper quiet, the kind that sits heavy for a wee minute before anybody can move it on. It was the mother that broke it in the end, getting up wi a wee groan and lifting the plate of sandwiches fae the sideboard.

The mother: Right, come on, get intae these, there's nae point them sitting there going stale. And there's more messages in the poke in the kitchen if anybody's wanting anything else, I bought far too much.

That got everybody moving a wee bit, plates passed roond, somebody topping up a glass that didnae need topping up, just for something tae dae wi their hands.

It was over the sandwiches that the auntie turned tae the eldest, and there was a wee edge tae it that hadnae been there aw night.

The auntie: You still never gave me back that money for the flooers at ma's funeral, mind.

The eldest: That was fifteen year ago.

The auntie: Aye well.

The eldest: We're no daeing this the night.

The auntie: I'm no daeing anything, I just said it.

And that was that, naebody pressing it any further, the two of them going quiet and letting it drop, because whitever it was it wasnae for the night, and they both kent it.

The neighbour, sensing the room needed moved on, cleared his throat and near laughed before he'd even started.

The neighbour: Right, right, but yous mind the thing wi the shed door. He'd been telling everybody for aboot three year he was gonnae fix that hinge, and every single person that offered tae dae it for him, he'd say naw, naw, he had it, he was daeing it himsel. Wee bit of WD-40 here, a screw there, propping it wi a brick half the time. Three year, that door. Deid stubborn ower a two-pound hinge.

The mother: He'd rather the shed fell doon than let somebody else fix it.

The eldest: The shed nearly did fall doon.

The neighbour: It did fall doon, hen, the back corner of it, he just never told yous.

Everybody laughed at that, a real laugh, no the awkward kind, and it felt like the first proper laugh of the night that didnae come wi a wee catch in it after.

The mother: That was him though, wasn't it. Never one for asking. Whitever it was, big or wee, he'd rather struggle on his own than let anybody else in tae help.

The eldest: Aye. Stubborn as they come. But he'd give you the shirt aff his back if you needed it, he just wouldnae take one aff you.

Naebody argued that, and naebody added onto it either, like the room had just quietly agreed that was the thing, the one thing that summed the whole of him up, and there was nae need tae say any more aboot it.

It was the auntie that started greeting first, quiet, no making a show of it, just the tears running while she still had the sandwich half eaten in her hand. Naebody stopped talking or made a fuss, the mother just put a hand on her arm for a second and kept on wi what she was saying, and the talk went on gently roond her the way it does, because stopping it would have made it bigger than it needed tae be.

After a while it turned practical, the way it always does in the end.

The mother: Right, somebody's gonnae need tae phone the undertaker's the morra and confirm the time, and I cannae dae that masel, I'll no get the words oot.

The eldest: I'll dae that first thing.

The neighbour: I've got the van, I'll take care of getting the flooers and that sorted, and I can run anybody where they need tae go.

The auntie: I'll dae the sandwiches for after, dinnae anybody else worry aboot that.

The mother: And somebody's gonnae have tae go through his good suit, see it still fits right, it's been hinging in that wardrobe for years.

The eldest: I'll dae that the morra as well.

It was quiet and plain, naebody making a big performance of any of it, just each of them taking a bit of it away tae carry, the way you dae.

It was the neighbour that was last awake, near enough everybody else's eyes hauf shut by then, and he lifted his mug even though it was empty, and the rest of them lifted whitever they had left in their glasses.

The neighbour: Right. Tae the man that never once fixed that hinge properly. He was a crabbit auld bugger some days and an eejit aboot asking for help, but there wasnae a better man tae have at your back, and I'll miss him every day. Tae him.

The mother: Tae him.

The eldest: Tae him.

And they sat on a wee while longer after that, no saying much else, just the four of them and the quiet of the hoose settling roond them like it kent the night was aboot done.
