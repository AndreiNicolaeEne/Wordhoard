Right then bab, siddown a minute and I'll tell yer the whole tale of the plot this year, cause it's been a right one, up and down like the Rea in flood.

Started back end of February proper, me and the allotment mob all stood round with our tea going cold arguing the toss over what wants going in. I'd had it in me head all winter I wanted spuds and beans and a decent row of onions, none of yer fancy stuff off the telly, just good honest grub our mom would've put on the table. Wrote it all down on the back of an envelope, seed order off the market, and left it in the shed for weeks cause the ground was still hard as owt. You cor put nothing in when it's like that, no point, it'll just sit there and rot. So I waited. Everyone waited. Old boy on the next plot but one, he's proper Black Country stock him, kept saying "it ay ready yet, bab, it wo be ready for another fortnight," and course he was right, he generally is about that sort of thing, been doing it forty year.

Put it all in about three, four week after the last frost, first week of April near enough, once the ground had warmed and give a bit under yer boot. And would you believe it, we got a right good run of weather straight after, sun out most days, not too hot, just right, and everything come up lovely. The beans were away like nobody's business, the spuds shooting through in no time. I was made up, proper made up, thought to meself this is gonna be a bostin year and no mistake.

Course it never stays easy long, does it. First bit of bother was blackfly, all over the broad beans, thick as anything, top of every stem black with the little sods. I tried the soapy water spray me mom always swore by, washing up liquid in a bottle, and it knocked some back but it never sorted it proper, they kept coming back every time it rained off. Half worked, is what I'd say, kept it from being a total loss but I lost the top growth on a good few plants and had to pinch it all out in the end, which the old boy said I should've done from the off anyway, typical him, wise after the event.

Then come the dry spell in the middle of it all, June into July, not a drop for weeks. I was down there of an evening every single day, lugging the cans up from the tap by the gate, up and down, up and down, donnies raw off the handles by the time I'd finished, arms like jelly. Everything wanted watering and there just wasn't enough hours nor enough water pressure to go round with everyone else after the same tap.

Which is where it all kicked off with the fella on the plot next to mine. He'd got this idea he was owed the tap of an evening cause he worked days and I didn't, and he'd be down there filling every can he owned, six of the buggers, while my rows sat there gasping. Words was had, I don't mind telling yer.

Him: Yo've had that tap every night this week, it's my turn now, I've got a family to feed off this an' all.

Me: I ay been hogging nothing, I'm down here same time as anybody, first come first served same as it's always been.

Him: Well it ay fair, is it, some of us can't get down till six and by then yow've drained the trough dry.

It went on a bit like that, back and forth over the fence, neither of us giving no ground, till the woman who does the secretary job for the committee come over and sorted a rota out, chalked up on the shed door, half six to seven mine, seven to half seven his, and that was that, no more bother after. Should've done it from the start really, saved us both a lot of hot air.

Anyroad, out of all that watering the tomatoes come good beyond anything, proper bostin crop, more than I knew what to do with, giving em away up and down the entry to anyone as'd take a bag. Best thing I've grown in years by a mile. Set against that though the plums went and let me down, birds had the lot near enough before I'd even thought to net em, whole top branch stripped in a weekend, and what they left the wasps and the rot got into anyway. Yampy of me not to net earlier, I know, saft as owt, but there y'are, live and learn.

Middle of August we had that storm come through, proper wild one, wind howling like summat off a gambol gone wrong, and it flattened the whole back row of beans and half the sweetcorn besides, laid flat as a table. Spent best part of a Sunday out there staking it all back up, tying it with bits of old tights the wife had saved, hands filthy, knees soaked, but got most of it standing again by teatime, bar a few stalks snapped clean through that were past saving.

Come the end of it all, when I sat down and tallied up what come off that plot, it weren't a bad show at all considering. Traded a load of the spare tomatoes and a sack of spuds to the fella two plots down for a length of hosepipe I needed, cause mine had gone and split, and that felt like a fair swap all round, nobody short-changed.

Put it all to bed for winter last weekend just gone, dug the manure in, covered what wanted covering, cleaned the tools off before they went back in the shed. Stood there a minute looking at it, all bare and tucked up, and thought, well, that were a proper year that were. Not without its aggro, mind, the fly and the dry and the row over the tap and the birds having the plums, but bostin all the same when you weigh it up. Better than going all round the Wrekin to say it plain, worth every blartin evening of hauling water, worth the lot.

Right, I'll keep out the oss road, bab, kettle's calling. Tara-a-bit.
