It was dark still when the mom come banging on the door telling us to get up else we'd miss the good of the day, and nobody argued back because when the mom says get up, bab, you get up, harvest day or no harvest day. Half five it was, black as the inside of a boot out the window, and the kettle going while everyone found their boots in the dark, treading on each other in the passage getting dressed.

Our dad had the flask done and the bait wrapped before any of us were even proper dressed, and he had the tools loaded on the barrow and the trailer hitched to the car ready for off. We went the back way, down the entry between the houses and out onto the road, round two islands and out past where the shops finish, and dad went the long way an' all, all round the Wrekin near enough, on account of the main road being up. Nobody minded, it give us another ten minutes kip in the car.

The uncle had the kettle going up his place already, and soon as we pulled up he had the jobs sorted before we'd even got the gate open. Our kid got give the easy end first, on account of him being the youngest and only just old enough to be trusted with owt at all: going up and down the top of the plot picking the stones out the ground that had worked up over the winter, chucking them in a bucket so the fork wouldn't go catching on one later and somebody's wrist wouldn't cop it. He didn't mind, he had it done quick and was made up with himself for being first one finished.

The heavy end went where it always goes, onto the eldest, on account of her being built for it and never one to mither about hard graft. Her had the digging fork off the uncle and went down the far row where the ground was claggy from the rain the week before, turning the row over one length at a time, and the rest of us come behind her on our knees picking the spuds up into the buckets and tipping them into the sacks. Nobody said nothing about who got give what, that's just how it's shared, the one as can carry it gets give it, same as always.

Once the top end was cleared we all shifted down, dad and the uncle swapping onto the fork turn and turn about so the eldest could get her back straight a minute, and the rest of us moved with them, same job different bit of ground.

My mate had come along an' all to earn a bit, and about midmorning her started falling behind, not saying nothing, just slower and slower up her row till there was a proper gap opened up between her and the rest. Nobody said a word about it, the mom just moved down quiet and started picking the far side of her row with her, and the eldest come across after her length was done and took the near side, and between the three of them the row come good again without a word passed about it. That's just how it's done, you don't shout at somebody for flagging, you go and help.

Dinner come out about one, the mom and the uncle's woman bringing bread and cheese and a great pot of tea down to the plot on a tray, and we all sat on the grass at the top by the hedge to have it, the young ones having a gambol about on the lawn after while the tea was still too hot to drink. That was when the joke started, our kid saying the eldest dug her row crooked as a dog's hind leg, and the eldest telling him he was yampy in the head if he thought his stone bucket counted as a morning's work, and it went on a good while, half laughing half arguing, dad telling the pair of them to give over and get the tea drunk before it went stone cold.

The Uncle: "You'm going to want your coats on again in a bit, look at that coming over."

The Mom: "It ay going to hold off, is it. I said it wouldn't, first thing."

The Uncle: "We'll get the top end covered while it's dry, move the rest under the lean-to."

Dad: "Right, everybody up, leave the tea, we ay got time to lose."

The sky had gone that flat grey colour over towards the cut, and the wind changed sudden, and there was no arguing with it, so we all got moving, carrying sacks under the lean-to two and three at a go while the first spots come on, and for a good half hour it looked like the whole day was going to be a wash-out. But it blew over quicker than it come, just a heavy shower and gone, and the sun had a proper go at drying the ground out after, so by half two we were back at it with the ground steaming a bit and everybody glad of it.

We pushed on hard through the afternoon then, on account of losing the half hour, and it was going well till the fork handle went on the uncle, split clean up the shaft on a stone he didn't see, and him nearly went over with the shock of it. There was a minute of him swearing summat rotten and our kid nearly blartin with laughing at the state of his face, and the mom telling him to give over an' all, but the uncle went and got the other fork off the shed and we lost no more than ten minutes over it, one row left half done where he'd been but the eldest went and finished it off after on top of her own.

By four the light was already starting to think about going, that flat gold coming in low over the hedge, and the word went round without hardly needing saying, everybody just quickened up of their own accord, less talking and more picking, and when dad did shout it, it was only the once.

Dad: "Come on now, get your skates on, we ay got the light we had an hour back."

By the time we got down to the last section, the bottom corner by the cut, we were all of us on it together, the uncle and dad on the forks either end and the rest of us strung out across it, no rows left to speak of by then, just everybody grabbing what come up till it was bare ground and nothing left in it.

We tallied it up on the trailer after, going by the sack, and it come to nineteen full sacks and one three-quarters, which the uncle reckoned was a bostin haul for the size of the plot and better than last year by a good two sacks, and nobody argued with that either.

Then it was just sitting, proper sitting, backs against the trailer wheel not moving for a good few minutes, nobody saying much, just glad it was done. Donnies washed under the tap before anybody was let near the tea the uncle's woman had going, the water running black off the lot of us.

The Uncle: "Right then, who's fetching us summat from the outdoor before we get off, my throat's like sandpaper."

Our kid was sent, being fresh enough still to run the errand, and he wasn't best pleased about it after the day he'd had, but off he went all the same.

Last thing before anybody sat down proper for good, the tools all had to go back under cover and the trailer sheeted over against the night dew, and that fell to me, seeing as everybody else was flat out on the grass by then. Did it quick enough, then it was tara-a-bit to the uncle's woman who was staying on, and keep out the oss road called back and forth as we loaded into the car in the last of the light, everybody half asleep before we'd even got to the first island for home.
