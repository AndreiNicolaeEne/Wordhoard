Sit yerself down a minute, bab, cos me legs am gone. I'll tell yer what state the street's in, and why I'm this shattered.

It was the old fella's garden down the end, the one by the entry what goes through to the cut. It'd been going wild for years, ever since his missus passed, and nobody had the heart to say owt to him about it, but it'd got past a joke. Ivy right through the fence panels, a dead pear tree leaning over towards next door's shed, brambles thick as your arm coming up through the path an' all. It ay just his garden no more by that point, it was everybody's problem, and a job like that, well, it's too big for one pair of hands, especially at his age.

It was the woman two doors up who said enough's enough. Her'd had a builder round to quote it and he wanted daft money, four hundred quid just to drop the tree, never mind clear the rest, so her went round knocking doors of an evening, just asking would people give a Saturday, bring what tools they had, and see what we could do between us. Word went up and down that street quicker than owt, quicker than the ice cream van, I swear. By the next morning half the road knew, and it ay just knowing either, everybody was on for it.

I day think we'd see more than a dozen turn out, tell you the truth, family and that, if we was lucky. What we got was more like thirty. Thirty-odd folk stood on that pavement by nine o'clock, some I hadn't even clocked lived on the street, must've come off the next one over cos word'd gone that far. Somebody's brother-in-law come with the trailer hitched on, had to loop round two islands to get it back down here cos the main road was still shut off from the storm. One fella said he'd gone all round the Wrekin to get to us for the same reason. Even our kid turned up straight off his shift, still in his work boots.

We split up quick enough, sort of natural, nobody organised it much, everybody just found the bit that suited em. Some was on the brambles, hacking and pulling, chucking it all in a heap by the entry. Some was stripping ivy off the fence panels to see what state they was in underneath. The fella with the chainsaw, he took on the pear tree, and that was the heaviest end of it by a mile, cos it was leant right over and nobody knew which way it'd drop till it went. He was up that ladder best part of the morning, taking it down bit by bit, branch by branch, cos you couldn't just fell the whole thing with the shed and the fence that close. His arms must've been like lead by dinnertime.

There was words over that tree before it even come down, mind.

The bloke from the garage: Yo cor drop it straight back like that, not with the shed that close, you'll put it through the roof.

The fella with the chainsaw: I've dropped bigger than this before, it'll be reet.

Proper argument, the two of em, right there in front of everybody, going on about ropes and which way the weight was leaning and all sorts. In the end the oldest fella there just says it, quiet like, and that was the end of it.

The oldest: Yow'll do it the safe way or yow wo do it at all, cos it's my shed it's leaning towards.

Nobody argued with him after that. They did it his way, slow, piece by piece, and it was fine, bar the one branch.

Cos course it never all goes to plan. About midday the last big bit of trunk come down wrong, caught the wind or summat, and it clouted the top of next door's shed roof, took a good chunk of felt clean off and put a dent in the guttering. One of the little ones started blartin, thought the whole shed was coming down on top of em, and it took her mom a good five minutes stood there cuddling her to get her calm again. Everybody else just stood there a second not saying nothing, then somebody says thank God nobody was under it, and we got on with sorting the shed roof out an' all, cos now that was on the list too.

By the afternoon there was food out on tables that'd come from three different kitchens at least. The woman two doors up done a big tray of chips, there was sausage rolls from somewhere, cans of pop and mugs of tea going round non-stop, and later on somebody fetched a crate up from the outdoor, few cans of bitter for the ones doing the heavy end. Nobody went hungry, put it that way, half the street never went in for their dinner at all, just ate off paper plates sat on the wall.

The kids had their own job, collecting up all the little twigs and bits into bundles for kindling, and they took it dead serious, marching round with their arms full like it was proper work. Their donnies was black as coal by the end of it, filthy, mind you they was made to wash em before they was let near the food, before tea an' all. A couple of the little ones had a right gambol on what was left of the lawn once it was cleared enough, rolling about in it laughing their heads off, till somebody's mom shouted em back to the twigs.

The oldest on the street, must be gone eighty, he weren't having it that he just sat and watched neither. Everybody kept trying to get him to sit on the wall with a cup of tea and mind the operation from there, and he weren't none of it, kept saying he could still hold a rake same as anybody. In the end they gave him the job of raking the cleared ground level and keeping the little fire going by the entry for the small stuff, and he stood by that fire the whole day like it was his own kingdom, wouldn't let nobody near it but him.

The one thing that had the whole street in stitches, the dog from across the road got in among the brambles after summat, a ball or a rat or who knows, and come out the other side wearing about six foot of ivy round its neck like a garland, trotting up the path proud as anything like it'd won a prize at a show. Set everybody off, even the fella up the ladder was laughing that hard he had to stop and hold on to the rung.

We reckoned it'd be done by three. It wo done till gone six, three hour over what anybody said, easy. But by the end of it that tree was down to a stump, the brambles was gone right back to bare soil, the fence was stripped and two panels swapped for spares somebody had lying in their own yard, the shed roof was patched enough to see it through, and there was a trailer and two car boots' worth of green waste ready for the tip. When the last of it went in the trailer somebody give a shout and the whole street just went up, cheering and clapping like we'd won summat ourselves, and the old fella stood there in his own garden looking at grass he hadn't seen in years, just looking at it.

It wo just a garden sorted, though, not really. That evening the whole street was still out, on the walls and the pavement, having a natter that went on till it got dark, folk who'd lived doors apart for years and never said more than hiya to one another. It ay stayed like that for long after, mind, few weeks maybe, then everybody's back to their own business same as ever. But that one evening, bab, straight up, it felt like a different street altogether.

Anyroad, that was my day, and a bostin one it was in the end, tired as I am with it. Keep out the oss road, bab. Tara-a-bit.
