Bab, sit there a minute and I'll tell yer about the checkpoint, though I ay gonna tell it big, cause it wo big, not really. It just felt big while it wor happening to me.

We'd set off early, the coach full up, and our kid stood on the step waving us off, shouting keep out the oss road as we pulled away, same as he always does. We went right round past the islands on the ring road and out the other side of town, and it wor a fair old drive before we come anywhere near the checkpoint. They'd put it there on account of the bother further up the road, some to-do that had been going on a few weeks by then, roadblocks and that, and they wor't havin just anybody roll through without their papers looked at first. Made sense when yer thought it through proper, even though it did put the wind up me a bit, thinking about it the night before.

By the time we got there the queue had built right up. A good half hour we sat, engine ticking, everybody on the coach gone dead quiet, just looking out at the line of cars and a pickup at the front with its boot open getting gone through bit by bit. I'll say it plain, I wor a bit yampy with nerves by then, me stomach going summat rotten, me donnies gone all clammy in me lap. I kept telling meself, there ay nothing wrong here, yow day do nothing wrong, just keep yer head down and answer straight when they ask yer. Kept thinking of daft things and all, our kid doing a gambol on the lawn that morning before we left, just to keep me mind off it.

When it come to me turn, the one at the little window held his hand out and I give him me papers.

The one at the window: Where are yer travelling to.

Me: Just up to see me mom's people, over the other side.

That first one wor easy enough, cause it wor the truth and there wor no messing about needed for it. The second one took more care though.

The one at the window: And how long are yer meaning to stop.

Yer cor just say any old number to that, yer got to think, cause if it dow match what's on the paper somewhere else yer in proper bother. So I took a breath and give him the right one, careful, the same one I'd told them when the papers wor first sorted.

Me: Bout a fortnight. Back before the month's out.

Then he just looked at it. Day say nothing. Stood there turning the pages, checking one bit against another, and that quiet, bab, that quiet went on and on, felt like it wor going all round the Wrekin and back, when really it wor probably no more than a minute by the clock. And it wor while he stood there that it come to me, sudden like, that I ay put down the reason for the visit proper, left that one line blank, and I had to say it there and then, on the spot, quick, before he got to asking.

Me: Oh, and that's for me nan, her's not been well. That's the reason for it.

He just nodded and wrote it in hisself, day even make a fuss of it, which wor a kindness really, cause he could have sent me back to the desk to fill it in proper and cost me me place in the queue.

There wor a woman ahead of me had it worse. Her papers gone through twice, three times, another fella called over to have a look and all, and her stood there, near enough blartin by the end of it though her held it in good. Nobody said nothing about it out loud, everyone just staring straight ahead like they'd not noticed a thing, but yer couldn't help watching her out the corner of yer eye. I remember thinking, that could be me stood there, that could be any one of us, and being glad and a bit ashamed of meself for being glad it wor her and not me.

I kept meself steady though it wor a proper job to do it. Kept me face still and me voice level, cause I know if yer voice goes wobbly they take more notice, read into it summat that ay even there. So I stood straight and answered plain and day let on how hard me heart wor going under me coat.

He handed the papers back over in the end, folded proper, and just said go on then, and give a little tip of his head towards the road. That wor it. One word and a nod and I wor through.

I walked on, past the barrier, past the last of the trucks waiting their turn, and it wor a good twenty minutes down that road, right past where the houses started again, past the outdoor on the corner and an entry running between two of the houses, before the tightness in me chest let go proper. I hadn't even known I wor holding it that tight till it went. Me legs went saft under me sudden, like they does after yer been stood too long in one spot, and I had to stop a minute by a wall along the cut and just breathe.

That's the toll of it, bab. Yer dow feel it while it's happening, yer too busy holding yerself together for it. It's only after, safe on the other side, that it catches up with yer proper and has its go.

And that's why I tell it careful, no bigger and no smaller than it wor. It wor half an hour in a queue and two questions and a woman I never even spoke to held back longer than me. Nothing happened to me, not really, nothing yer could point to. But I know now what it is to stand in a line like that and feel yer whole self go small, waiting on somebody else behind a bit of glass to decide whether yow go on or not, and it'd be a lie to make more of it than that, same as it'd be a lie to make less.

It wor bostin to be through it though, I'll say that much. Tara-a-bit for now, bab, I'll put kettle on.
