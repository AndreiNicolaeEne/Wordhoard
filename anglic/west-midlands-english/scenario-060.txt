Right, I'll tell yo the whole of it, start to finish, cause it wo just us four, it was everybody, aunties, uncles, our cousins down from Wolverhampton way, the lot. Nan's gone in the home now, her hip give out proper after Christmas and there's no way her's managing them stairs again, and Granddad's been gone three year already, so that was that, house had to go. Nobody wanted to say it out loud but it needed saying. House been in the family since before any of us was born and now it's getting sold to some young couple who'll prob'ly rip the fireplace out.

We started in the front room cause that's where everything starts, ay it. The mom went straight for the sideboard, the one with the good glasses nobody was ever allowed to use, and her pulled a drawer out and just stood there.

The mother: Oh bab, look at this. That's her thimble, that is. I remember her sat in that chair of an evening doing my school skirt up, going on about how I'd grow out of everything before it wore out.

Her went a bit quiet after that, just held it a minute, then put it in her cardigan pocket like it was made of glass.

Then my auntie's digging through the same drawer and her comes out with this little brass thing, flat, with a picture stamped on it, foreign writing round the edge, and none of us had a clue.

My auntie: What in the world is this. Anybody know what this is? It ay one of ours is it.

The eldest: Never seen it in my life. Might've been off one of his mates from the army. Could be owt.

Nobody could place it. We passed it round the room and everybody just shrugged and it went in a box marked "sort later" which we all knew meant nobody would ever sort it.

Kitchen was quick, mostly tins and that, but the argument started proper when we got to the dining room, over the table. Big oak thing, seats ten easy.

Our kid: I'm having the table, I've got the room for it and nobody else has.

The middle one: Yow ay having it, our kid, I've wanted that table since I were a kid, Nan said I could have it.

The eldest: Yow can both stop it. It's going wherever's got the space and the space is our kid's, end of. Nan'd say the same, her hated a fuss.

And that settled it, cause nobody argues with the eldest for long, it ay worth it.

Then there was the picture over the fireplace, this big dark oil job of some hills, horrible frame on it, and half of us wanted shot of it and half didn't.

My uncle: That's coming to mine, that is, that's hung there my whole life.

The youngest: It's grim though, ay it, proper miserable looking thing. Nobody's even sure who painted it or where them hills even are.

My uncle: I dow care if it's grim, it's stopping in the family.

So that one nobody agreed on, went round and round, and in the end it just went with my uncle cause he was the loudest about it and the rest of us couldn't be bothered arguing all afternoon over a picture of some hills.

Upstairs in the back bedroom, under the mattress if yo can believe it, my auntie finds an old tobacco tin stuffed with notes, proper old ones, out of circulation years back, and a few bits of jewellery wrapped in a hanky. Nobody knew that was there. Her went white as owt.

My auntie: I ay being funny, but there's money under here. Actual money. Since when did he keep money under the mattress like something off the telly.

The mother: That's him all over though, ay it, he never trusted a bank in his life.

That got shared out fair, split even between everybody there, cause nobody could say who it should've gone to more than anybody else.

The box room though, honest to God, that took us longer than every other room put together. Boxes and boxes of photos, none of them labelled, half of them nobody in could even name, and every single one had to be looked at and passed round and argued over whether it was worth keeping.

The middle one: Who even is this, look, is that Nan? That ay Nan, is it?

The eldest: That's her sister, God rest her, look at the state of that hairdo.

We was in there hours. Proper hours. Everyone kept saying "just chuck what we dow need" and then not chucking a single thing.

Round about two we packed it in and had us dinner, sat wherever there was space, some on the stairs, some on the garden step, sandwiches the mom had brought and a big flask of tea going round, and for a bit nobody talked about clearing owt at all, just sat there having a laugh about Granddad and his gambol he used to do on the lawn when he'd had one too many at Christmas, showing off in front of the grandkids, saft old sod.

After dinner it was the wardrobe in the main bedroom, great big dark thing, must've weighed a ton, and not one of us wanted it in the end.

The youngest: I don't want it, I ay got the room and I don't even like it if I'm honest, it's proper yampy trying to shift a thing that size.

My uncle: Nobody wants it, do they. Right, it's going, somebody's gotta get a van round.

And that was that, big as it was, out it went, no fuss, cause when nobody wants a thing there's nothing to argue about.

The clock on the landing though, that one nobody even discussed. My auntie just said her'd have it and everybody just went yeah, alright, go on then, cause it made more sense on her wall than anywhere else and nobody felt strong about it either way. Easiest thing all day.

Last room to go empty was the box room again in the end, cause of how long it took, but once it was cleared, standing there with just the carpet marks where the boxes had sat for donkey's years, it went dead quiet. Whole house did really, once every room was bare. Funny how a house sounds different empty.

We stood in the hall a bit at the end, dividing up what was left in a few carrier bags, bits and bobs nobody had claimed, and there was a bit of "yo have that, I don't mind," "no go on, you have it," back and forth, but nothing like an argument by then, everyone'd gone soft with it by the end of the day.

The mother: Right, that's it then. That's the lot.

The eldest: Yeah. That's the house done.

Somebody went round and shut every window, checked the back door, and the mom stood in the hall with the keys a minute, not saying much, and then we all went out the front, down the entry one last time past where the bins go, and locked up.

The eldest: Right, keep out the oss road then, all of yer. Tara-a-bit.

And that was it. House shut for good, and everybody went off their own ways home, and none of us went all round the Wrekin about it in the car, straight home for once, quiet the whole way.
