Where the family started weren't here, and I ay gunna dwell on it long because there's little enough left of that place to tell now, the islands, out past everywhere, cane fields gone quiet, no wage in the ground no more once the big houses stopped paying for it. That's the reason, plain as owt, why the old man before the old man had to go. No work there, plenty of mouths to feed, and a boat leaving from the docks that took folk over the water for the promise of a proper wage at the other end. He went first, alone, our great-grandad, generations back now, before any of us round this table was even thought of.

He day speak much of the crossing hisself, not to me, that come down through my nan, who had it off her own mom, who was on that same boat, though her day marry him till years after, just a girl from two roads over going the selfsame way for the selfsame reason. Her used to tell it sat in the chair by the fire of a winter, comb going through her hair, and this is how her told it:

The grandmother: "It wo like nothing yow'd credit now, bab. Weeks on that water, and half on us sick the whole way, and no room to lay down proper, packed in like it or not. There was a cousin of the old man's on there an' all, a young lad, and he took a fever about a week out and never come off it. They put him over the side out past sight of land, and nobody never said his name again after that, not once, not in all the years I knew the old man, he'd just say 'the cousin' if it ever come up at all, and yow knew not to ask him no more than that. That's the one bit of that crossing I'll ever tell twice, so listen it the once, bab, because I ay saying it again."

So that's the one what never made it, spoke of only the once by the one what was there, and never a name give to him in all the tellings since, not by her, not by nobody, just "the cousin," and that's how he's stopped, and that's how he'll stay.

They landed with next to nothing, what fit in a bag between the two on 'em and not a lot more, and it wo like there was a house waiting or a job waiting or anybody waiting neither, come to that. The first bit here was rough, proper rough, no steady work going for love nor money, just whatever day's graft a man could pick up, standing about of a morning hoping to get took on for a load here, a delivery there, donnies raw off it by teatime and nothing saved to show for most weeks. They stopped in with another family from back home who'd got here a year or two before and had a room going spare, three and four bodies to a bed some nights, borrowed roof over borrowed roof, and grateful for it an' all, because there wo nowhere else going, and yo cor be choosy when yo'm skint.

It took the old man the best part of a year before he got took on proper, in one of the foundries down towards the cut, and once he was in he stopped in it for good, brass work, hot and hard and forty year of it after, and he never went looking for owt else again once he'd got that trade under him. Steady wage at last, even if it wo much of one to start, and he put every spare penny by, living tight as owt, one bottle from the outdoor between the whole street of a Friday instead of one apiece, and word sent back that he was working now, and saving, and it'd not be long.

It was the best part of two and a half year, near enough, before there was enough put by to bring the rest over, her that become his wife later, and the little ones her already had by him before he ever went. Two and a half year of letters and waiting and her raising them alone back home on whatever he could send, blartin some nights her told us, when the money was late and the little ones was asking after him and her day have an answer that'd satisfy them. Then finally the crossing come through for her an' all, and her landed on a boat of her own with the children hanging off her, and that was the family whole again for the first time since he'd gone, all stood on the same wet ground together at last, and nobody blartin that day, or if they was it wo the same kind.

The one thing her brought with her, out of everything her could've carried and mostly day because there wo the room for it, was a little wooden comb, carved plain, nothing fancy about it, that had been her own mom's before her. It's bostin, that comb, and it's still in the house now, wrapped in a bit of cloth in a drawer, took out and shown to every one of us as come along since, and it'll go on down further yet, I'd put money on it.

They got a place of their own within a few year of her landing, just two rooms up an entry off the main road, past the second island if yo was walking it from town, rented, not owned, but theirs to shut the door on of a night, which is more than they'd had going a long while. And it was there the children started coming, proper Birmingham children this time, born right here and nowhere else, and that's the bit the old man always said made it home, not the arriving, not the trade, not even the two rooms of his own. It was the first one born under that roof that made him stop calling it "over here" and start calling it just where we live.

He'd tell folk after, when he'd tell it at all, that yow can call a place home when yow stop measuring it against the old place every single day, and the day a child of yourn is born under your own roof is the day that measuring stops for good, whether yow meant it to or not. Donnies washed before tea, same as anywhere, same as always, and that was that, home, plain and simple, no more "over here" about it.

Our kid and me was raised on it, the pair of us, gambolling about on the bit of lawn out the front of that same house years after while the grown-ups sat with the door open telling it again for whoever was new enough to ask. Don't be saft and go asking about the cousin's name, my nan'd say, we ay telling that twice neither. And we never did ask twice, because some things get carried the whole way over and some things get put over the side, and yo don't go fishing one of them back up just 'cause yo'm nosy or yampy for a name that ay coming.

I'm telling it the same road now to mine, bit by bit, same fire, different house, the islands, the boat, the year rough as owt, the two rooms up the entry, the comb still wrapped in its bit of cloth in the drawer, and the cousin with no name that only ever gets said the once. That's ours. That's where we're from and where we ended up an' all, and there ay no going all round the Wrekin way of telling it shorter than that, it's the whole road or none of it.

And when it's told, same as ever, that's the end of it for the night, keep out the oss road to whoever's leaving, tara-a-bit to whoever's only popping round the corner and back, and the fire banked down till next time somebody's little enough to need telling it all again from the start.
