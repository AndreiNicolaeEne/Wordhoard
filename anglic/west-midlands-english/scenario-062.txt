The new neighbour: Ay, go on then, what's gone off with next door? Nobody round here'll tell me nothing, they just go all quiet and start talking about the weather instead.

Me: Well bab, sit yerself down a minute and I'll tell yer my side of it, cause it's the only side I've got and I ay pretending different. You cor expect me to give yer theirs an all, can yer.

See, it wo always like this. Years and years, us two houses was in and out of one another's like we was one family, not two. Kids doing gambols on the lawn together till it went dark, donnies black as anything and having to be washed before tea, and nobody minded whose garden they'd been playing in half the day. Her mom and my mom used to walk the cut of an evening, pushing prams, gassing away about nothing. If one house run out of sugar or tea bags you just went round the entry and knocked, no messing, no phoning first. Bonfire night we done together every year without fail, and if I was popping down the outdoor for a crate I'd shout over the fence and ask did they want owt an all. That's how it wo. I ay making that bit up to make meself look good, everybody on this street'll tell yer the same, we was proper close, the two houses.

Then it started with summat that sounds that daft when you say it out loud. Their lad put a shed up, right ont line, near enough sat on top of our fence, and he'd planted one of them big fast-growing trees an inch or two over onto our side an all, without so much as a word to us first. I ay one for making a fuss over nothing so I left it a good while, thought it'd sort itself, but it kept getting worse, roots coming under and everything.

So I went round, polite as anything, just asked could he shift the shed back a foot and see to the tree before it got any bigger. Her said course, no bother, leave it wi' me. Weeks went by. Nothing moved. I asked again, same as before, still polite, and got told he'd get to it when he got round to it. Third time I went, I ay gonna lie to yer, I'd got an edge on me by then, and I said it a lot less nice than the first two.

After that it all come to me sideways, through the one that carries word between the two houses, cause by then we wo really speaking direct no more. And I'm telling yer, bab, the tale had gone all round the Wrekin by the time it landed on my doorstep. What started as me asking nice for a shed to be moved a foot had turned, by the time it reached me, into me apparently going round shouting the odds and threatening all sorts. I never said none of that. It'd picked up bits and pieces off everybody it went through, same as these things always do.

And when I put it to her straight, face to face, that I'd asked three times and been ignored three times, her stood there bold as brass and said her'd never even heard me ask in the first place, not once. Flat denial, no blinking, nothing. It wo true and her knew full well it wo true, and that's what got me more than the shed or the tree ever did.

So I did summat back, quiet like, nothing anybody could point a finger at. I'd always swept the leaves off their side of the entry along with mine, every autumn, years I'd done it without a word said either way. I just stopped. Never told nobody, never made a show of it. Her'd have noticed though, her ay saft.

Then the comment come back to me, same as before, carried by the one in the middle. Her'd apparently said I was turning into me own mom, minding everybody's business but me own. That one stung worse than anything else said the whole time, worse than the shed, worse than the denial, worse than any of it, and I still cor tell yer exactly why it landed the way it did, only that it did.

I tried to put it right an all, I want yer to know that. Made a cake, took it round meself, stood on the step like a lemon. Her opened the door about six inch and said no ta and shut it again. Wo even give it a chance to go cold on the side.

By this time the whole street had gone and took sides, near enough straight down the middle, and everybody stopped saying hello proper, just a nod if that. It all come to a head one Saturday out the front where anybody walking past or hanging out the window could hear every word of it, and there wo no keeping that one quiet. Voices going, one of the little ones from next door blartin her eyes out on the step cause of the shouting, and in the middle of it her turns round in front of everybody and says I'd been in her garden and took a load of her plant pots. I told her straight, same as I'm telling you now, I ay never touched a single thing of hers in me life, and I still say that today.

That's getting on for eight year gone now, this silence between us, and it's daft when yer think what it started as. Still shows itself an all. I keep out the oss road when her's out sweeping, cross over rather than pass close, and her does the same to me, and neither of us has ever said a word about doing it, we just do. The kids that used to do gambols together on that lawn are near enough grown now and they cor tell yer either what it was even about, only that it's how it's always been far as they remember.

And if I'm honest wi' yer, bab, sat here now, I ay even sure no more who started it proper. I could tell yer it was the shed, or the tree, or the leaves, or the cake, but the truth of it is I've gone over it that many times in me head I cor swear to yer which bit come first any more. That's my side though, for what it's worth, and it's the only one I'm giving yer. Tara-a-bit, love, mind how yer go.
