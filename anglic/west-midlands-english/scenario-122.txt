Me: Right, sit yerself down then, bab, and keep them donnies still five minutes, cause once I start on this one I ay stopping halfway through for nobody. This is the one always comes up of an evening, ay it, when it's gone dark and one of the young ones wants to nip out the entry for summat and somebody older says leave it, wait till morning, and the young one goes why, and that's when it gets told. Every family round here's got a night when somebody asks that why and gets landed with the whole of it, same as I'm landing it on yow now.

Now nobody I've ever known has clapped eyes on the thing proper. That's the first thing to get straight in yer head. It ay got a shape people agree on, cause the shape ay the point of it. What it's got is a voice, and the voice comes first, always, before owt else shows itself, if owt ever does show itself at all. You feel it before you hear it and you hear it before you see a blind thing, and by the time you'd see summat, if you ever did, you'd already be gone too far to matter.

What it does, right, is call. And it don't call in its own voice, cause far as anybody's ever worked out it ay got one of its own, it takes the voice of somebody you love. Yer mom, yer dad, our kid, whoever it reckons'll pull you hardest. Calls yer name in that voice, exact as owt, and it's calling you off the path, away from the road, away from anybody who'd hear you holler back if it all went wrong. That's the whole of what it wants, near as anyone's ever made out, get you off where nobody's looking.

And it ay calling any old time neither. Only after midnight, mind, never before, and only on a night with no moon in it at all, pitch black, not a sliver, cause it needs the dark same as it needs the quiet. Round here it's tied to the cut, down past where the old lock gates have gone to rot and the towpath narrows off by the entry to the allotments. That's the stretch. I ay saying it's the only stretch anywhere, I'm saying it's the one that's ours, and that's enough for me.

I first heard the whole of it off our nan, sat in her kitchen, must have been about seven or eight year old, blartin half through it if I'm honest cause her told it dead straight-faced, no smile in it, no wink, nothing that let you off the hook. Some of the aunties'd tell it laughing, like it was a laugh and nothing more, but you'd watch their eyes while they laughed and the laugh never once got up that far. Our nan just told it plain, like her was telling you which bin day it was.

First bit of proof anybody ever offers is the ground. There's a patch down there, right by where the towpath bends, and nothing has ever grown on it, not once, not never mind how many folk have tried. Somebody's mom put bulbs in years back, daffs I think, good as anywhere else round it, and not one came up, not one, while everything either side of that patch come up bostin same as always. People have tried grass seed, tried nothing fancy, just grass, and it just sits there bald as owt.

Second bit's the sort you always get secondhand, off somebody who got it off somebody else, the way these things travel. There's a tale about a bloke walking his dog late, dog wouldn't go a step further than that bend, sat down and wouldn't budge, and the bloke swears he heard summat call out plain as day though there weren't a soul about for streets. I never met the bloke. Whoever told me it got it off somebody who knew somebody who knew him. That's how it always comes, though, that one, never straight from the mouth it happened to.

But the one that gets told in full every time, no shortcuts, is what happened to our kid. Years back now, our kid was walking home past the cut, gone midnight, no moon, and heard their own name called out clear as anything from off the path, down towards the water. And our kid, and this is the bit that gets me every time, however many times I've heard it, our kid started walking towards it. Three steps, four steps off the path, before summat, our kid never could say what, made 'em stop dead and turn round and walk the other way sharpish, not looking back once.

And to this day our kid'll tell you the same words, same as always: "It sounded exactly like our mom. Not near enough. Exact. And our mom weren't within two mile of that cut that night, I checked after, and I still turned towards it before I caught meself."

That's the bit makes even the doubters go quiet a second, the checking after. Our kid actually went and found out where the mom was that night before ever telling a soul the story, wanting proof it weren't just her calling from somewhere daft. That shuts people up better than owt else in it.

The bit that gives the doubters their laugh, mind, is the dog and bloke tale, cause it's secondhand twice over and everybody knows it, and there's always one who'll say a dog won't walk nowhere near water at midnight regardless what's calling, dogs is saft like that, yampy over nothing half the time. Fair one, that. I've never minded it.

Truth is the whole story shifts a bit dependent who's telling it. Some tell it softer, more a caution than a scare, more like keep off the towpath after dark, love, than owt about voices and patches of dead ground. Others tell it harder than I've told it now, add bits I ay never believed and won't be repeating. I've never minded the difference, cause it's still ours whichever way it's shaped, still gets told the same nights, still does the same job.

And the job, near enough, is this: round here, if you hear yer name called from somewhere dark and you cor see who's calling, you don't answer. Not straight off. You wait till you've clapped eyes on whoever it actually is calling, and only then do you answer back. Everybody does it, near enough, even the ones who'll tell you to yer face they don't believe a word.

Me included, bab, and I'll say it honest, I don't reckon I believe it, not properly, not in the way I believe the kettle's on the stove. But I've walked that towpath more nights than I could count, and I have never once, not one single time, answered a call in the dark before I've seen with me own eyes who's doing the calling. Not once. Make of that what yow will.

Tara-a-bit, love, mind how yer go, and keep out the oss road on yer way past that bend.
