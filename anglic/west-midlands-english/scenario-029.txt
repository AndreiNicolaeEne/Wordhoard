Right, seeing as yer stopping over, yer gonna hear all this happening round yer own self in a few hours, so I might as well give yer the guide first, save yer standing there gawping like a saft ha'porth wondering what's gone on.

It's me up first, every single day, alarm or no alarm, don't matter if I've had four hour's kip or eight, my eyes just go. Down I come in the dark, kettle on, and I stand at the back door two minutes with me tea while the dog does its business and a gambol on the lawn after next door's cat, and that's the only two minutes of the whole day that's properly mine. Nobody else is up. House is dead quiet. I like it best like that, if I'm honest, though I'd never say as much to any of 'em.

Second one up is him, and he wakes to the same grumble every day of his life, regular as the milk float used to be.

Him: “Five more minutes. It's still dark out there, it ay even proper mornin' yet.”

Every day. Same words near enough. I tell him it's gone half six and he tells me it's still dark, like the dark's ever stopped anybody working a day in their life. Then it's him and the eldest going at it over the wash-room, who was in there longest yesterday, who's in there first today, and it never once gets settled, not properly, not in all these years. He reckons seniority. The eldest reckons speed. Neither of 'em's right and neither of 'em'll shift.

Then it's the two kids, and they get woke in the same order every single mornin', eldest first, cause if you do the babby first her wanders round like a little zombie getting under everybody's feet, and the eldest can at least dress themself without supervision. Same three things called out, same order, every day, up the stairs:

Me: “Up! Teeth! Bag by the door!”

Never varies. I could say it in me sleep. I probably have said it in me sleep.

Then the babby, second woke, and her's a different animal altogether of a mornin'. Her'll go from flat out to blartin' in about four seconds flat if her's woke wrong, if the light goes on too sudden or her favourite bit of blanket's ended up under the pillow. Once her's up proper though, her's no bother, chattering ten to the dozen about a dream her can't half remember.

And most mornings, close enough to the same words each time, the two of 'em go at it an' all, over the cereal usually.

The eldest: “Mom, our kid's had the last of the good stuff again and left me the one nobody wants.”

The babby: “I day! There was loads left!”

The eldest: “Yow cor lie to me, I saw the box, it's basically empty.”

Same argument, different day, and I referee it exactly the same way every time, which is to say I don't, I just put more bread in the toaster and let it blow itself out.

The dog's got its own part in all this and God help anybody who tries to leave it out. It's stood at the bottom of the stairs the second the babby's feet hit the floor, like it's been assigned, and it walks her down every mornin' same as a little bodyguard. Then it sits under the table for crusts, and it knows exactly whose crusts are worth begging off and whose are a waste of time, and it is never once wrong about that.

Breakfast's the same every day, toast mostly, donnies washed first at the sink before anybody's touching a plate in this house, that's not up for discussion. And him, out of the whole lot of us, never varies his order once, not by a hair, not in however many years we've been doing this. Two rounds, done dark, nearly burnt if I'm honest, brown sauce, never red, and don't even think about offering him owt else, he'll look at yer like yer've lost the plot.

Something goes missing most mornings an' all, regular as the toast. Usually it's the eldest's other shoe, or the PE kit that was definitely by the door last night. We've gone all round the Wrekin looking for that shoe some mornings, checked under the sofa, checked the entry where the coats hang, checked the dog's bed twice cause that dog is a thief for anything left at floor height, and half the time we've near enough checked the outdoor down the road before somebody finds it exactly where it should've been the whole time.

And there's a sound, every house has got one, and ours is the toaster popping twice in a row, cause it never does both rounds even, one always goes before the other. That's the sound that means it's really happening now, everybody's up, everybody's dressed near enough, and we're on the run-in.

The eldest's the one always running behind, same few minutes every single day, doesn't matter what time they're woke, they find them minutes from somewhere.

Him: “Yow'll make us late again, I'm telling yer.”

The eldest: “I'm nearly ready!”

Which they never are. And then it's the rush at the door proper, coats grabbed off the hooks in the entry, bags shoved into hands, and I go down the same list in me head every day, count of three, coat, bag, shoes, and nothing's ever forgotten only cause I've never once trusted any of 'em to remember it themself.

One day a week the whole thing just stops though. Sunday morning and the house goes proper quiet, no alarm, no toaster popping, nobody hollering up the stairs, just everybody drifting down whenever they drift down and breakfast dragging on for an hour with the radio on low. It's the strangest thing, after six days of that racket, how quiet a house can go, like it's holding its breath.

But every other mornin', same as this one'll be for yer tomorrow, it ends the same way at the door. Coats on, bags checked, and I say it, same handful of words, every single day of my life near enough:

Me: “Got everything? Tara-a-bit then. Keep out the oss road.”

And off they go, the pair of 'em, and the door shuts and it's just me and the dog again for two minutes before I have to be somewhere myself. And honest, when I stand there and think about it, the whole performance of it, same order, same words, same daft argument over the cereal every single day of the year bar the one, it's proper bostin daft when you say it out loud to somebody who's never once stood through a single mornin' of it. Yampy, the whole lot of it. But it'd feel all wrong without it.
