Gone one in the morning and the house has thinned right down, just the five of them left now, sat round the front room with the good cups out and the plate of sausage rolls nobody's touched since eight. The mother's got her cardigan pulled tight round her even though the gas fire's on full. Our kid's on the arm of the settee cause every chair's took. The eldest keeps getting up to do summat with the kettle that dae need doing.

The eldest: I keep thinking of him stood at that cooker of a Sunday, stirring the gravy with that wooden spoon gone all black at one end, singing summat under his breath, dae even know what song it was.

Our kid: It wor a song, it wor him doing the football scores. He'd go through em like a little tune. Dae you remember that?

The eldest: Nah, go on then, that wor singing that.

Our kid: It wor the classifieds off the radio, bab, he used to do it in that voice.

The eldest: (laughing) Oh ar, yeah, yow'm right, I always get that wrong, I've told people it wor a song for years.

The neighbour lets a laugh out sudden, one hand straight up over her mouth like her never meant it to come out that loud, and nobody minds, the mother even smiles at her.

The neighbour: Sorry, sorry, it just come out.

The mother: Dow be sorry, love, it's nice to hear it in here.

The uncle: I'll tell yer summat he said every single day of his life, bab, every day, rain or shine. He'd come through that door of an evening and go, "Ay half thirsty, put the kettle on, bab, I could sup the cut dry." Every night. Same words.

The mother: (saying it exactly as him, low and dry) Ay half thirsty, put the kettle on, bab, I could sup the cut dry.

Quiet for a second, and then the eldest laughs properly at hearing it said back like that, and it does sound like him again for a moment, right there in the room.

Our kid: D'yer know summat none of yow know. He used to go down the outdoor of a Friday and get a can for hisself and one for the old fella two doors down, the one his cor walk far no more, and just leave it on his step, never said nothing, never told nobody. I only found out cause I seen him do it once.

The neighbour: He never!

The mother: (quiet, surprised) I day know that. Forty year and I day know that.

Nobody says much after that for a minute, just the fire ticking and somebody's cup going down on the saucer too loud.

The eldest gets up and starts the plate round again, not cause anyone's hungry, just for summat to do with her hands, and the uncle takes one he don't want and the neighbour takes two.

The uncle: Yow still on about that fence, our kid, or has that died a death finally.

Our kid: I ay on about nothing.

The uncle: Yow was on about it at the do in March.

Our kid: That's got nothing to do with tonight, that.

The uncle: I'm only saying.

Our kid: Well don't.

And that's it, nobody presses it, the mother just gives em both a look and it drops, same as it always drops, not tonight of all nights.

The neighbour: I'll tell yer the best one though. Yer know when he decided he wor having a new latch on that gate and it took him three Saturdays cause he wo have anyone show him how, wo have it, "I've put more gates up than yow've had hot dinners," and by the third Saturday it's hanging off worse than when he started and yower mother's stood there going just get a man in and he's still saying no, no, I've nearly got it, and it's on backwards.

The mother: It wor on backwards for a fortnight before he'd admit it.

The eldest: Bostin stubborn, he wor. Yampy about it and all, wouldn't budge an inch.

The uncle: That wor him all over though, wor it. Stubborn as owt but never a cross word behind it, just, this is how it's getting done and that's that.

Nobody says it out loud, not in so many words, but it settles over the room anyway, that that's the bit of him that'll stay, the not budging, the doing it his own road even when it wor the long road, all round the Wrekin and back rather than take the short way somebody else showed him.

The neighbour's gone quiet, and when the eldest looks over her's blartin, not loud, not making a show of it, just tears running while her still holds her cup, and nobody stops talking or makes a fuss, the mother just reaches over and squeezes her arm once and carries on.

The mother: Right, well. Somebody's got to ring the doctor's in the morning and cancel that appointment card that's still sat behind the clock. And the eldest, yow said yow'd sort the bank, dae yer.

The eldest: Ar, I'll go down there Monday.

The mother: Our kid, can yow do the flowers, yer know what he'd have wanted, nothing daft.

Our kid: Ar, I'll sort it.

The uncle: I'll do the cars, I know a fella.

It's said plain, no fuss to it, just the next thing and the next thing, same as owt else needs doing.

Eventually it's only the uncle left awake, the others gone off to nod where they're sat or slipped up for an hour's kip, and he looks round at the room, half asleep round him, the plate empty now bar one sausage roll nobody wants, and he lifts his tea like it's summat stronger.

The uncle: Go on then. To the daftest, stubbornest, kindest man I ever knew, and I never had a pint with him I day enjoy. Tara-a-bit, our kid.

And he sups the last of the tea like it's a toast and all, and sits back, and lets the fire do the rest of the talking.
