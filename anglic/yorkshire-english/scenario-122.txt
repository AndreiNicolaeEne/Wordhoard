Me:

Ey up, siddown then, get thissen warm, put wood in t'oil behind thee, it's parky out there tonight. Aye, go on, ask us again, I heard thee the first time, I were just fettling t'fire while I thought how to start it right.

It allus comes up same road it allus has. Somebody young wants to walk down past t'beck of an evening on their own, or they've heard summat off one of their mates at school and they come home wanting it telled straight, and one of us older ones says nay, not after dark, not down there, and they ask why, and it goes quiet a minute, and then it gets telled. Allus of an evening an' all. Allus when it's gone dim outside and t'fire's about the only light worth having and nobody's in a hurry to be elsewhere.

Now I'll say this plain, because tha'll want to know what tha's dealing with before owt else. Nobody living has ever properly seen it. That's the first thing and the truest thing. It isn't a shape that walks about int' dark for folk to clap eyes on, it's a voice first, always a voice first, and if there's ever owt more to it than that, nobody's stopped long enough to find out what. Tha feels it before tha hears it, they say, a kind of pulling-in of the air, like the night's gone tight round thee, and then t'voice comes after.

And here's t'wicked part of it, love, the bit that makes it summat worse than any tale about a shape in a hedge. It doesn't call in its own voice, because it hasn't got one of its own to call in. It calls in the voice of somebody tha loves. Not a stranger's voice, not owt that'd make thee wary straight off. It'll be thy own mother calling thee, or a brother, or whoever it is tha'd drop everything for, and it'll say thy name the exact road they say it, every little turn of it right, and it'll sound like it's coming from just off the path, close enough tha could get to it in a few steps. That's the whole trick of it. It wants thee off the path and away from anybody who could get to thee, and there's no better road of doing that than putting a voice tha trusts more than thy own sense right there in t'dark, calling.

It only comes after midnight, mind, never before, and only on a night wi' no moon at all. Give it a moon, even a sliver, and there's nowt. Folk round here have allus said that much the same, whatever else they change telling it, so I'd hold that bit as close to fact as owt in this story gets.

And it's tied to one spot, not the whole road, not the whole valley, just the one stretch, down where t'beck bends past where t'old sheep-wash used to be, before the stones went. I'll not tell thee it's owt bigger than that. I've heard offcumdens try and stretch it over half the dale like it's some grand curse on the place, and that's nowt but them not knowing any better. It's that bend. That's all it's ever been.

I first heard it off an elder in us own family, when I were about thy age, maybe younger, sat where tha's sat now on a neet it were siling it down outside, proper coming down, and I remember being roaring by the end of it, really roaring, and being sent to bed with a face like a smacked backside for making such a fuss. And I couldn't tell thee to this day whether it were telled to me as plain truth or whether there were a laugh in it somewhere I were too young to catch. There were a laugh, aye, there allus is with that one, but it never once reached the eyes, and I've thought on that a lot since, what that means, a person laughing wi' half their face and not the other half.

First proof anybody offers thee, and it'll come quick, is the ground. There's a patch right at that bend where nowt has ever grown, not grass, not weed, not even the sort of scrub that'll take root in owt. Folk have put things in there over the years just to test it, tha knows, and it comes to nowt every time, bare as the day before. Make of that what tha will. I've no clever answer for it, only that it's true, I've seen the patch missen more times than I could count.

Second proof, and this one's softer, because it's the kind that's allus come to somebody else first. There's not a soul round here who heard it themselves, it's allus a friend of a friend, or somebody's aunt who knew somebody who worked t'land down that way, who heard it once walking home and never went that road again after dark for love nor money. Secondhand every time, tha knows, and that's just how that sort of proof allus comes, so I'll not pretend it's owt stronger than what it is.

But there's one telling that gets telled in full every single time, no shortening it, and that's what happened to an aunt of ours, years back now, long before thy time. She were walking home past that bend of a neet, no moon out, and she heard her own name called, clear as owt, in a voice she'd have sworn on her life were her own mother's, coming from just off t'path in t'dark. And she went. Three steps, maybe four, off t'path toward it, before summat made her stop dead and turn back, and she couldn't tell thee to this day what that summat were, only that it stopped her.

And what she swears, word for word, the road it's allus repeated in this family, is this: she says, "It said my name right, every bit of it right, but it said it wi' no breath behind it." That's it. That's the whole of what she'll say, every time she's asked, not a word added nor took away in all the years I've heard it telled.

Now here's the detail that makes even them as don't believe a word of this go quiet for a second when they hear it: her own mother, the one whose voice it borrowed, were three mile off in her own bed the whole neet, tha knows, nowhere near that beck, and there's them still living who'll vouch for that. That's the bit that catches folk.

And here's the other detail, just as real, mind, I'll not leave it out for the sake of a tidier story, that gives the doubters their opening to laugh it off same as ever. It were a black neet, no moon, thick wi' mist off that beck, and our aunt were never what tha'd call a steady walker after dark even before that neet, allus a bit jumpy, allus hearing summat in a hedge that were only a sheep. So there's plenty round here, gormless or not, who'll say it were nowt but mist and nerves and a mind playing tricks, and I'll not tell thee they're wrong to say it, only that it's not the whole of what were said that neet.

I'll not pretend it comes out same from every mouth either. My own mother telled it softer than I'm telling it thee now, more a warning than owt to fear, and there's an uncle of ours who'll not tell it wi'out making thee jump twice before he's done. Softer from some, harder from others, and I've never minded the difference, because it's still the same bend, and it's still the same voice, however it's dressed up in t'telling.

But I'll tell thee what it's left behind, and it's a small thing, nowt dramatic, just summat folk here still do wi'out much thought. If tha hears thy name called from off in t'dark somewhere, from a stretch tha can't quite place, tha doesn't answer it, not straight off, not until tha's checked wi' thy own two eyes who it is that's calling. Every last one of us does it, even them as'll laugh about the story to thy face in daylight.

And since tha's asked, aye, I'll say it honest, same as I allus do. I don't fully believe it missen, not in daylight, not sat here wi' thee warm by t'fire. But I've walked past that bend more neets than I could count in my life, and I've never once broke that rule, not once, and I'll not start now just to prove a point to thissen. It'll be reet not knowing for certain. Some things tha just leaves be.
