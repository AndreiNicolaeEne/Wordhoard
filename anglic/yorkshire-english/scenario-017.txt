The aunt: Now then, love. Sit thissen down a minute, there's spice in t' tin and I've brought thee a breadcake, get that down thee while we talk.

The niece: Ey up. What's tha after, then, tha's got a face on thee like tha's got summat to say.

The aunt: I have an' all. I were on t' phone to the woman who does us hair Tuesday, and she got talking about her sister's lad, tha knows, the one up at t' garage. She says he's a grand worker, first in, last out, never a day off sick. Champion natured with it, calm as owt, nowt rattles him. And his family's proper folk an' all, chapel every Sunday, his mother side t' table and fettle that house like nobody's business. Three things going for him, love, work, temperament, and where he's come from. Tha'll not find better round here.

The niece: I'm sure he's champion, aunt, I'm not saying owt against him.

The aunt: Well go on then. What's up wi' thee? What could tha have against a lad like that?

The niece: There's nowt wrong wi' him. I've just got enough on mi plate right now, what wi' work and everything, I've no time for laiking about wi' courting.

The aunt: Time! That's what your cousin said an' all, sat where thou's sat, arms folded, "I've no time, I've no time." And look at her now, four year wed and glad as owt she didn't win that argument. She'll tell thee herself she were daft to hold off as long as she did.

The niece: I hear thee, aunt, I'm not being funny wi' thee. But I'll do it in mi own time, when I'm ready, not before.

The aunt: Aye. I'll say nowt more on it today. But it stands, love, whenever tha changes thi mind, he'll still be there. It'll be reet, tha'll see.
