We weren't from round here, not at the start of it, tha knows. Where we came from before were nobbut a scrap of land and a cottage with a roof that let more in than it kept out, and no ground of us own worth speaking of, enough to scrape a living off in a kind year, and next to nowt in a hard one. There were no work to be had that would keep a family in owt, and that's plain, no dressing it up. So it were go or starve, near enough, and one of ours went first, generations back now, on his own, missen and nowt else for company the whole road.

He were young when he went, younger than I am telling this now. His mother were roaring the whole way down to where the boat went from, by all accounts, and he never looked back, because looking back were no use to a man as had nowt to go back to. He didn't say much about the crossing itself, not in all the years after, only that it were the worst thing he'd ever done in his life and he'd do it once and no more. Cold as owt out on that water, nithered through to the bone the whole way over, and packed in tight as fish in a barrel down below.

There were a cousin went over on that same crossing and never came off it. Nobody in this family has ever given him back a name, not once, not even now. Only the one who was there ever spoke of him at all, and he only ever called him "the cousin," never a word more.

Me, as a bairn, once asked straight out:

Me: What were the cousin's name then?

Grandad: Never you mind that. He's the cousin, and that's all as needs saying.

And that were the end of that, every time it come up. Some doors you don't go knocking on twice.

He landed with nowt but the clothes he stood up in, and one thing carried close the whole way over: a little tin box, dented down one side, that had been his mother's before him. We've still got it. It sits on a shelf in the front room to this day and nobody's ever thrown it out and nobody ever will.

He'd nowhere of his own to lay his head that first bit, so he lodged in with another family, three and four to a room some nights, sleeping top to tail, and glad of a roof over him at all, borrowed as it were, not his own. He'd nobbut his own two hands and whatever work he could pick up. A day here, a day there, carrying, digging, owt that paid, and nowt steady for longer than anybody liked to say out loud after. He took whatever come and kept his mouth shut doing it, chelp were not in him at that stage of his life, a man in his position hadn't the luxury of answering a gaffer back, he'd have been out on his ear and likely brayed for his trouble besides. Round here we were the offcumdens ourselves then, first off, before this ever felt like our place. Offcumdens is what the neighbours called us, not unkind, just what we were, and he said himself he must've looked gormless enough stood there those first weeks, not knowing one street off another.

Then, bit by bit, the work steadied under him. He took up wi' a joiner, learned the trade off him proper, and stopped at it for good after that. Right down to my own dad, that were the family trade, one hand teaching the next.

He waited while there were enough put by, best part of two year it were, tha knows, before he'd saved out of his wage what it took to send for the rest. Two year and a bit over, of a wife and bairns on one side of the water and him on the other, and neither side able to do owt but wait and hope the letters kept coming.

When they landed at last it were siling it down, so the story goes, him stood on the quay mafted with the crowd and nithered with the rain both at once, not sure which of the bairns he'd even recognise, they'd grown that much in two year. His wife saw him first and got the first word in.

His wife: Now then. Ey up. Ow do, stranger.

Which is exactly the sort of thing you'd say to a man you'd not set eyes on in two year, half laughing and half roaring both at once to see him again.

The landlady where he'd first lodged, so it's told, were the one stood him a hot drink the very day he first came off that boat looking like death, and all she'd said to him then were this.

Landlady: Ey up, love, tha looks half starved. Sit thissen down.

He never forgot that, not in all his years after, and told it to his own after him, that the first kind word he got in this place were off a woman as owed him nothing at all.

They found their own place inside them first few years, a little back-to-back, two up two down, and set about fettling it up proper, side t'table under the window, whitewash on the walls, the lot. That were the making of it as a home in the bricks-and-mortar sense, but not the whole of it, not yet.

The bairns started coming once they were all under one roof again, and that were what finished the job, tha knows. The first one born right there in that house is what turned the place from somewhere they were stopping into somewhere they belonged. Tha can own the walls all tha likes, but it's the bairn born inside them as makes it thine.

I heard all this off my own grandad, sat by the fire of a winter, and one of us allus getting sent to put t'wood in t'oil because there were a draught coming under it. He'd tell it the same road every time, near enough word for word, and we'd sit there being fed a breadcake and a bit of spice off my nan while he talked, not allowed to laik about or chelp while he were mid-story, because tha didn't interrupt Grandad once he'd got going.

Grandad: It'll be reet, he allus used to say, that were his very words. He landed here with nowt, tha knows, and it were reet in the end. Champion, he'd call it, looking back. Grand, even.

I'm telling it the same road now, near enough, to them as'll listen, because that's the only way a thing like that stays alive, passed hand to hand, mouth to ear, same as it were passed to me. It weren't owt out of the ordinary, mind, our story, half the houses on that street could tell thee their own version of it, near enough the same shape. But it were ours, and it happened, and here we still are to say so.
