So. You've asked for t'whole story, start to finish, so you're getting it proper, none of your edited version.

We met at t'bus stop, top of t'hill by t'chippy, both of us waiting on the same bus every morning for the best part of a fortnight before either of us said owt to the other. Then one morning it come down siling, proper hammering it, and I'd nowt but this daft little brolly, more hole than cloth, and she just come and stood under it wi' me. Never asked, mind. Just stood there, bold as brass. And I said summat like "make thissen at home then," dead sarcastic, and she looked at me straight-faced and said, "well it's a rubbish brolly, but it's better than nowt." And that were it. Twenty-odd year on and I still think about that..."better than nowt." Champion way to start owt, that were.

A cousin: Tell 'em about t'brolly itself, go on.

Me: I'm getting there, give us a chance. Aye, we've still got it, that same brolly, stood in t'porch to this day, more hole than brolly now if truth be told, and every time it sials down her mam says get t'good one out and we won't. Refuse point blank. Some of you'll have seen the pair of us stood at that bus stop in a downpour looking like right gormless articles under it, and we don't care one bit.

Now, I'll not dress it up, I were wary of her at first, and I'll say why. I'd only just come out of a right mess of a do before that, and I'd told missen I wanted nowt to do wi' anybody for a good while, proper done with the whole business. But she just kept turning up, same bus stop, every morning, and after a bit she must have twigged summat were up, 'cause she never chelped me about it once, never pushed, just let me be. And that's what wore it off, really, weeks and weeks of her just being there, easy, no fuss, no questions. One day I thought, sithee, stop being so daft, and asked if she fancied a pint after work.

Only trouble were, I were on earlies at t'warehouse and she were nursing, on lates, so most weeks all we got were about twenty minutes crossing over, her coming in as I were going out. Went on like that best part of a year, snatched dinners, texts at daft hours, one of us always mafted wi' being that tired. We got past it, in t'end, by just being bloody-minded about it, if I'm honest. She'd swap a shift, I'd swap one, and we made Sunday ours no matter what. It'll be reet, we kept telling ourselves, over and over, and eventually it were.

Her being not even Leeds born, mind, bit of an offcumden, over Wakefield way originally, we let her in anyway.

Now, I reckon I knew it had gone serious the day I took her round to meet my mam, and mam had t'kettle on and t'good cups out and a breadcake buttered before we'd even got us coats off, which she's never once done for anybody else brought through that door. And as for how she found out I'd gone soft, she says she knew for certain the Sunday she come round mine and found the whole flat sided and fettled, hoovered, table cleared, everything, which weren't like me at all, and she just stood in t'doorway and said, ey up, who's died.

That were it. Champion, that were, exactly us.

Bout a year later, give or take, we were wed.

Her dad took a bit of winning round, I'll say that plain, he'd sized me up early on and decided I were fairly gormless, which, fair play to him, he weren't wrong, but he come round proper once he'd seen how I were with her, and by t'wedding he were roaring more than anybody, couldn't get two words out at t'top table for it. My mam loved her from t'off, mind, no bother there at all, took to her like she'd always been one of us.

Thing that's stuck with me from t'day itself, and no photo got this, not one, is stood outside t'church waiting to go in, and it comes down siling, proper hammering it, everybody running for cover under coats and whatever they'd got, and I'm just stood there getting drenched thinking, well, this is us all over, isn't it. And by t'time she come up t'path the sun were out again like nowt had happened. Nobody got a picture of that. But I've kept it.

Me gran, when she finally met her, looked her up and down, said she were "appen a keeper" and give her a bag of spice out of her tin, which if you knew my gran is about as high a compliment as she ever paid anybody.

We've had us hard stretch since an' all, same as anybody does. Few year back I got laid off wi' no warning, whole department gone overnight, and it were a rough do for a good while, proper nithered feeling if I'm honest, not knowing which way owt were going to fall. What carried us through it were just each other, really, and her mam's stew turning up on t'doorstep more weeks than not, and the pair of us just keeping on saying it'll be reet, even the weeks we weren't sure we believed it. And in t'end it were.

The little ones can carry on laiking about under t'table there, take no notice of them, I'm nearly done.
