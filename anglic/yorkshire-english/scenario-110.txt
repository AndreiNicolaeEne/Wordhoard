Mother: Ey up, look who it is. Now then, come here and give us a hug, tha's got thinner.

Me: Ow do, Mother. I'm not thinner, I'm just tall in't wrong places.

Uncle: Ey up lad. Sit thissen down, tea's not while six but there's a brew going.

Grandad: Now then. How's city life treating thee?

Me: Champion, honestly. Different, but champion.

The eldest: Go on then, what's it actually like? Proper tell us, none o' that "oh it's fine" chelp you gave us on t'phone.

Me: It's just… big. Bigger than owt round here, tha knows. Takes best part of an hour some days just to get from one side of it to t'other, and that's on a good day.

Auntie: An hour? To get across one place?

Me: Aye, an hour, sometimes more if summat's up wi' t'trains. Back home you can walk across whole of Leeds in that.

The eldest: Bloody hell.

Uncle: And I bet it's never quiet neither.

Me: Never. Not once. There's always summat going, sirens, or music from some flat, or just people. You forget what quiet even sounds like. First week back here I couldn't sleep for how quiet it were, if tha can believe that.

Mother: Well I never. So where is it tha's actually living then, love, cause in my head I've got thee in some tiny box.

Me: It's smaller than folk think, actually, one room, kitchen's a cupboard with a hob in it. But rent for that cupboard'd have thee crying into thy tea. What we'd pay for a whole house here, that's what I pay for t'cupboard.

Grandad: Give over.

Me: I'm not messing. Milk's dearer an' all, and a breadcake, don't get me started on what they charge for summat you'd get for nowt here practically.

The eldest: So it's not all glamour then.

Me: It's not nowt, but it's not glamour either.

Auntie: Are folk friendly down there, though? That's what I want to know. You always say people up here'll talk to owt that stands still long enough.

Me: It's not a straight yes or no, if I'm honest. Some are lovely, proper look out for thee. Others walk past thee like tha's invisible, won't even catch thy eye on a morning. It's not that they're rude exactly, it's just, everyone's rushing, tha knows, nobody's got time to stop and chelp on a corner like folk do here. You have to know where to look for the friendly ones.

Mother: Well that sounds lonely.

Me: Sometimes it is, and sometimes it's grand, honestly, depends on t'day.

The eldest: What about doing your shopping and that, where do you even get your bits?

Me: There's a market near us, not unlike t'one here really, same sort of stalls, veg and that, bloke shouting his prices same as at home. Makes us feel less like an offcumden when I'm down there, tell thee true. Rest of it's these big shops though, open till all hours, nowt shuts when it should.

Uncle: And work, how'd tha land that then, in a place that size?

Me: Mostly it's who tha knows, same as anywhere, you graft at getting thissen known. I sent word out to about forty places before owt came back. Now it's early starts, long days, and everyone dashing about like their backside's on fire, but it's steady work and it pays, so I'm not complaining.

Grandad: Tha got lost much, first off?

Me: Lost three or four times just in't first fortnight, ended up miles from where I meant to be, proper gormless wandering round with my bags, asking strangers which way. Took us a good while to get t'layout in my head, which street runs into which, where t'station actually spits thee out. I know it better now, but it took some learning, I'll say that.

Auntie: And what about t'food down there, is it any good?

Me: Better in some ways, worse in others, if I'm honest. You can get owt tha fancies, any hour, any sort of food from anywhere in't world practically, and some of it's proper lovely. But it's nowt like a proper Sunday dinner here, and don't get me started on trying to find a decent breadcake, you'd think I were asking for gold dust.

The eldest: Right, straight question then, none of your dodging. Is it better or worse than here?

Me: Eh, it's just different, in't it. Depends what tha wants of a place.

The eldest: That's not an answer, that's you being a politician.

Me: I know, I know, tha knows I've always been like that.

Mother: Will tha come back for good, then, one day? Proper come home?

Me: I don't know, honest. Never say never, tha knows. Might do, someday, when I've had my fill of it down there. It'll be reet whatever happens, one way or t'other.

Grandad: Aye well. Whichever way it goes, get door shut, put wood in t'oil, tha's letting all t'heat out stood there talking.

Mother: Sit thissen down properly then and have summat to eat, you look nithered, and don't be roaring off again in three days like last time, we've barely had thee five minutes.

Me: I'm not roaring off anywhere yet, Mother, I'm mafted in here if owt, you've got fire going full blast.

Uncle: That's home for thee, lad. Tha forgets.

Me: Aye. I do a bit. It's grand to be back, though, tha knows. Proper grand.
