Reight, I'll tell thi how it went, start to finish, cos it were a right long day and no mistake.

We were all stood on t'step by nine, half on us with a flask and t'other half moaning they'd not had their breakfast. Grandad's been in t'home since spring now, chest not up to it any more, and there's nobody left as can keep this house going, so it's going up for sale, simple as that. It were never going to be easy, clearing it, but there's no sense letting it stand empty and rotting while solicitors chelp on about paperwork. Better it's us that does it than some house-clearance lot who'll not know a good teapot from a bit of tat.

Auntie: Ey up, is everyone here then? Ow do, love, tha made it.

Cousin's husband: Now then. Bloody freezing out, isn't it, I'm nithered already.

Mother: Put t'wood in t'oil, will tha, there's a right draught coming through.

We started in t'front room, cos that's the one everybody could bear to do first, being as it's the one grandad hardly sat in these last years, too grand for every day, kept for best. Mother went straight to t'sideboard and pulled out an old tin, and soon as she saw what were inside she went dead quiet. Dominoes. Proper bone ones, worn smooth.

Mother: Eh, look at these. He used to laik at dominoes wi' me on that little table there, every Sunday while t'dinner were on. I must've been about seven. I'd forgot all about it till just now.

She had a right roar over that one, no shame in it either, nobody expected owt else.

Then there were t'other thing, found in same drawer, wrapped up in a bit of newspaper gone yellow. A little brass thing, like a compass but not quite, with letters scratched on t'back that don't match anybody's initials we could work out.

Uncle: What in God's name is that when it's at home?

Auntie: No idea. Never seen it in my life. Was it Grandma's from before she wed him, dost tha reckon?

Uncle: Could be owt. Foreign, by look of it. We'll never know now, will we.

Nobody could place it. It went in a box marked "keep, ask about", which everybody knew meant nobody would ever get round to asking.

T'front room were also where t'argument over t'furniture happened, proper argy-bargy an' all, over that big oak table. Two of t'aunties both wanted it summat rotten.

Auntie: I've the room for it, mine's twice t'size of hers.

Other auntie: Aye but I've had my eye on that table since I were a lass, tha knows that.

It went on a good twenty minutes, back and forth, till t'eldest cousin put her foot down.

The eldest: Right, whoever's just got their own place and hasn't a table to eat off, it's going to her, end of. Rest of you have got tables already.

And that were that, settled, champion, nobody sulked over it for more than a minute.

T'other row, that one didn't get settled so tidy. It were over them flying ducks on t'wall, three of 'em in a row, ugliest things you ever saw.

Cousin: They're not coming to my house, I'll tell thi that now. Gormless things.

Uncle: Tha's not binning them, they've been up there since before tha were born.

Cousin: Doesn't mean they're not hideous.

Uncle: It's tradition, is that.

In t'end nobody would take them and nobody would let 'em go in t'skip either, so they went back on t'wall for whoever buys t'house to deal with, which weren't really a decision at all, more like giving up.

Kitchen were next, and that's where we found summat hid that nobody were expecting. Behind a loose bit of board at back of t'cupboard under t'sink, a biscuit tin, and inside it a bundle of letters tied wi' ribbon, not in grandad's hand nor addressed to him.

Auntie: Well I never. Look at that, then.

Mother: Don't read 'em out, whatever tha does. Whoever that were, it were before his time, leave her that.

We just looked at one another a bit, and put t'tin to one side, unopened past that first letter, and nobody chelped on about it after. Some things aren't ours to go poking at.

By dinnertime we were all mafted with t'work even though it were parky out, so we stopped, sat round what were left of t'kitchen table on boxes and stools, and had us butties, a breadcake each and a flask of tea gone lukewarm, and somebody found half a packet of spice in a drawer that nobody had the heart to throw and we ate that an' all.

Cousin's husband: Champion, this. Best breadcake I've had all week.

The youngest: Can we laik in t'garden after?

Mother: Nay, love, there's no time for laiking, we've the whole upstairs yet.

Upstairs were t'box room that took the longest by a mile, longer than every other room put together nearly. Grandad had shifted half his shed in there years back, tins of screws, magazines back to when they were black and white, tools nobody knew t'use of, and every box had summat in it that made somebody stop and go through it proper instead of just chucking it. We were at it two hour and still weren't done.

Uncle: Sithee, there's no end to this room, is there. He never threw owt away in his life.

Auntie: Well thank God for that, look what we found under t'letters.

We let t'main bedroom go last, once t'box room were finally cleared, cos there weren't as much decision needed in there, just t'bed frame, t'wardrobe and t'curtains coming down, and it went quick once we started.

T'wardrobe were t'one big thing nobody wanted, in t'end. Lovely bit of oak once, but t'back panel had gone with damp and it wouldn't fit through nobody's door anyroad.

Cousin: Nobody's got room for that, have they.

Uncle: Nay. It's for t'tip, that, and no arguing over it either.

And nobody did argue, which felt strange after all t'to-do over t'table, but there it were, everybody just nodded and it went out.

T'one thing that went without a word said either way were t'clock off t'mantelpiece, cos everybody knew, without owt being said, that it were going to t'eldest lad, seeing as he were t'only one who'd ever bothered winding it right when he stopped over as a bairn.

The eldest lad: I'll have that, if nobody minds.

Nobody minded. Nobody even answered him really, they just let him take it down.

Main bedroom stood there empty at last, stripped right back to bare boards and a mark on t'wallpaper where t'bed had stood thirty year, and for a minute nobody said owt at all, just stood in t'doorway looking.

Then it were down to what were left on t'kitchen table, bits and bobs, tea towels, a rolling pin, odd cups, a torch that worked, a torch that didn't, and everybody just took summat, no fuss, no fighting over it, cos by then we'd all had our fights over t'big things and this were just tidying up.

Mother: Right, that's us done, I reckon. Give t'floor a last going-over, fettle it up a bit for whoever's viewing it.

Last of all we stood on t'step again, same as we'd started, and t'eldest turned t'key in t'lock for t'last time, and nobody said much, cos what is there to say, really, standing outside a house that's not yours any more.

Auntie: Well. It'll be reet.

And we all went us separate ways home, tha knows, quiet like, carrying us bits between us.
