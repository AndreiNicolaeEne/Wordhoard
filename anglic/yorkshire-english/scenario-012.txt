Ey up, Mam. Sit thissen down a minute, will tha, I've summat to tell thee and I've been carrying it that long I can't carry it no further. Don't say owt while I've finished, right, just let us get it all out, then tha can say what tha wants.

Tha knows the scar on me leg. Tha's asked us about it I don't know how many times over the years, twenty-odd year now it must be, and every time I've told thee the same tale, that I came off me bike and went into the kerb and split it open on the pedal. Tha believed it dead straight off, took us to get it seen to and all, stood there holding me hand while they stitched us up, and never once did tha doubt a word of it. Well. It weren't true. None of it were true. I've let thee believe a made-up story for more years than I care to count and I'm stood here now to put it reet, because it's been sat on me chest like summat heavy and I'm sick of carrying it round.

It were the tree down at t'water, the big one at us usual spot, tha knows the one, where us lot used to laik about after school and on a Saturday when there were nowt else on. There were four of us, maybe five that day, and it were one of them long summers where it were mafted from morning to night, everyone in their vests, nithered only if tha went in the water too long. There were a dare going round, there always were with that lot, and this one were an old one dusted off again: climb the tree to the highest branch tha could get to and jump in off it. I'd backed out of it once before, months before that, stood up in the branches and just frozen, come back down the trunk with me legs shaking and me face red, and they'd not let us forget it since. Chelp about it near every time we went down there. "Ey up, here's the one what can't jump." "Tha going up today or is tha stopping down here again holding coats." That sort of thing, on and on, and a lad only wants to hear it that many times before summat in him says right then, today's the day.

So I went up. And because I'd backed out the once, I went higher than any sense would tell a body to go, past where the others had ever gone and all, because I wanted it dead clear to them that I weren't frightened, not one bit.

The eldest: Go on then, tha said tha'd do it this time, get on with it.

One of the others: He's not going, watch, he'll come down again.

Me, up in the branches: I'm going, hold on, gie us a minute.

The eldest: There's nowt to think about, just jump, it'll be reet.

And I jumped. And I misjudged it something rotten, Mam, I'd not looked proper at where the water were deep and where it weren't, and I hit it flat and wrong and it knocked every bit of wind clean out of us, and under the water there were summat, a bit of old fencing or a bedstead or summat rusted, nobody ever went down to look after, and it opened me leg right up on the way past. I came up not able to get a breath in us at all, just floundering, and the water round us going red, and it were the others that got hold of us and dragged us out onto the bank, all of them panicking same as I were, one of them roaring proper, tears and everything, thinking I were going to bleed out there and then in front of them.

One of the lads, soaked through and shaking: Oh my god, oh my god, look at his leg, look at it.

The eldest: Shurrup, shurrup a minute, let us think.

Another one: We have to get him home, we have to tell someone.

And it were me that said it first, Mam, I'll own that, sat there on the bank holding me own leg together with me hand, still fighting for breath. I said, if I go home and say what really happened, I'm done for, I'll never be let near that spot again and neither will any of you, and worse than that, I'll never hear the last of it, going higher than I meant just to prove summat and nearly drowning missen for it. So I said, we tell 'em I came off me bike.

The eldest: Aye. Aye, that'll do it. Fell off his bike, went into the kerb.

Me: Everyone say the same thing, right, don't none of us chelp about the tree, not now, not ever.

Another one: Not a word. Sithee, not one word, ever.

And we all shook on it there, soaked through, blood and lake water down me leg, and we walked us home practising the story between us like it were lines for a play, and it worked a treat, better than any of us thought it would. I came in limping and white as owt and told thee I'd come off me bike, and tha never questioned it once, tha just got us sat down and got on with sorting us out, fussing over us, telling us it'd be reet, champion, grand, brave lad and all that, while I sat there letting thee believe every word.

The mother, in me head, the way I remember it: Eh, tha daft ha'porth, how many times have I told thee to mind that kerb, look at the state of thee.

And I said nowt back that put it right. I just took the fuss and the sympathy and let it bed in as the truth, and it stopped there. Twenty-odd year, Mam. Every single time it's come up since, at family do's, at the doctor's, tha even telling other folk the tale yourself, come off his bike as a lad, split his leg right open, I've sat there and let the lie do the talking for us, because by then it were that old and worn in it felt more true than the truth did.

What's pushed us to tell thee now, if tha's wondering, is I've a lad of me own coming up to that age, the age where his lot'll start daring one another to do daft things down wherever they laik about, and I keep thinking on what I'd want him to do different to what I did. I'd want him to be able to come home and say what actually happened, even the frightening bit, even the bit where he went higher than he should to prove summat to a couple of lads who'd forgot about it themselves within a week, and not have to sit there for twenty year carrying a made-up story because the true one felt too daft or too dangerous to own up to. And I couldn't stand there and tell him that while I'd never done it missen.

So there it is, that's the whole of it, the real dare, the real jump, the real reason for the scar, and I'm sorry, Mam. Proper sorry, not just for the lie itself but for how long I let thee carry it and all, worrying over a story that weren't even real while the real one sat there the whole time, never spoken. Tha deserved the truth off us a lot sooner than this, and I'm giving it thee now because I couldn't put it off no further, not with a lad of me own watching how I do things.

The mother, quiet a minute, then: Well. Tha daft ha'porth.

Me: Aye. I know.

The mother: Tha could've drowned, tha knows that.

Me: I know that an' all, Mam. I know.

The mother: Right. Well. It's out now. Put t'kettle on, love, I want a brew while I get me head round this.

Me: I'll put t'wood in t'oil while I'm up, it's gone parky in here.

The mother: Aye. Go on then.
