Me: Nay, I'm just sayin. Were her cooking not up to scratch for thi mother? "Ooh, it's a bit dry, love." Dry? I stood three hour at that oven.

The wife: She were only saying summat. She don't mean owt by it.

Me: She never means owt and she says it every flippin time we go round. Same as thi father wi' that tale about t'lorry and t'roundabout. Third time tonight, that were. I could've told it missen by t'end, word for word.

The wife: He likes tellin it. He's champion when he's in that mood, is thi father.

Me: Champion for him happen. I were sat there noddin like a gormless article, half asleep on t'settee, and he's off again.

The wife: Aye, well.

Me: And then goodbye at t'door. Forty-five minutes, that were. Sithee, we'd said we were off, coats on and everything, and she's stood there in t'cold telling us mind how we go, and mind t'roads, and did we want owt for us tea, and has tha got petrol, and is that coat warm enough...

The wife: She does go on, I'll give thee that.

Me: Go on? Thi father put t'wood in t'oil twice while she were talkin, he were that nithered, and still she carried on chelping.

The wife: I know, I know. Ey up, don't set us both off now.

Me: I'm not bothered. I'm just sayin. Tha knows what she's like.

The wife: I do know what she's like better than thee. It'll be reet, she means well, she's just made that road.

Me: Aye, she means well and she'll do exact same next time an' all, tha mark my words.

The wife: She will an' all, God love her. Standing there in t'porch chelping on about summat while we're stood freezing, nithered to t'bone, waiting on nowt.

Me: There, tha's said it thissen now.

The wife: Aye. Someone had to.
