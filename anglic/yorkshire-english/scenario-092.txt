By the middle of the night the house has thinned right down to the five of them, sat round the kitchen table with their coats still hung on the backs of chairs from hours back. The good cups are out, the immersion's ticking away, and there's a plate of breadcakes going soft under a tea towel that nobody's touched since six.

The wife: Ey up, do you remember he used to stand right there by the kettle of a morning and moan the tea were never hot enough for him. Every single day, same complaint, forty year running.

The eldest: It were the toast, Mother, not the tea, tha knows. He'd sup the tea and say nowt about it. It were always the toast were too pale for him.

The wife: Aye, you're reet, it were the toast. I always get that mixed up, don't I, after all this while.

The youngest lets a laugh out of him, quick and sudden, like it got away before he could stop it, and puts a hand over his own mouth after, a bit shamed, but nobody minds it, if owt it loosens summat in the room that wanted loosening.

The neighbour: He'd say it like it were an insult an' all. Is this toast, or is it a warning.

The brother: Forty year he said that. Word for word, every Sunday breakfast at us mam's table. Is this toast, or is it a warning. I can hear him say it now.

Quiet for a second, everyone half smiling at the sound of it come back, exact.

The eldest: D'you know summat nobody else here knew, I don't reckon. He used to ring me every Tuesday dinnertime, not to say owt in particular, just to ask were I warm enough. Every Tuesday for years. Never once told our mam he did it.

The wife: He never did.

The eldest: He did, I'll swear it.

The wife: Well I never.

The room settles quiet again a moment, properly quiet, everyone looking at their own cup.

The neighbour breaks it, pushing the breadcakes back into the middle and topping cups up that don't want topping up.

The neighbour: Now then, get summat in you, go on, there's ham left an' all, no sense it going begging.

The brother, to the eldest: Tha still owes me for that lawnmower, tha knows.

The eldest: I do not, that were sorted years back.

The brother: It were not sorted, it were forgot about, there's a difference -

The eldest: Less of your chelp.

The wife: Eh, not tonight you two, I'll bray the pair of you, sithee. Leave it be.

They leave it, easy as that, nobody wanting it, not really, not tonight.

The youngest: Do you remember the shed door. Wouldn't shut proper for three year, and he'd stand there mafted in front of the fire bawling put wood in th'oil the minute anybody so much as cracked the back door open, and never once thought to fettle the one door that were letting the cold in all that while.

The wife: Three year I asked him. In the end I had the joiner in behind his back and told him it were done, and he just nodded like it were nowt to do with him at all.

The brother: Gormless over it, weren't he. Same as cards, mind. Wouldn't laik for owt but tuppence a game and took it like it were the world championship, sat there thinking on his hand for ten minutes over tuppence.

The neighbour: He kept spice in his top pocket an' all, always did, went round giving it to bairns at work that weren't even his own, forgot half their names an' all.

Quiet settles a moment, and without anybody saying it outright, that's the one they've landed on between them, that stubbornness, over the daftest little things, no bother at all over the big ones, and there's no edge left in it now, only fondness.

The eldest starts roaring, quiet, partway through it, not making a show, just letting it come while she sits there, and the talk carries on gentle round her, nobody stopping to fuss. The brother just reaches over and tops her cup up without a word.

The wife: It'll be reet, love.

That's all that's said about it.

The wife: Right, well. Somebody'll want to ring the undertaker back in the morning about times. And somebody'll have to sort the flowers, I can't be doing with that missen. Cars for Thursday want confirming an' all.

The brother: I'll do the undertaker.

The youngest: I'll sort the flowers, our lass, I've got the car free.

The wife: Right. That's summat off my mind then.

The neighbour, the last one still up, finds the good stuff at the back of the cupboard and pours five small ones without being asked, sides t'table of the empty cups to make room.

The neighbour: Go on then. To him. Champion fella, daft as a brush over nowt, and none of us'll ever get toast right again without thinking on him.

They drink to that, quiet, and outside it's started siling it down, proper nithered out there by the sound of it, the kind of night he'd have stood at the window moaning about, and nobody says it but they're all thinking it, and that's the last of it before anybody thinks on bed.
