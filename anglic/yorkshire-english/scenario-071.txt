Ey up, love. Tha knows I've been on us feet since six this morning, but let me tell thee how yesterday went, because it's not summat I'll forget in a hurry.

It started wi' that big old ash at t' bottom of us gardens, the one that's been leaning happen ten year and everyone said someone ought to see to it before it saw to us. Well, wind got up something wicked in t' night, siling it down and gusting fit to take t' roof off, and by morning it were down. Not just down neither, it'd took out three garden walls on its way, and it were laid right across t' ginnel so no bugger could get car nor bin through, and there were branches gone straight through next door's greenhouse and all.

Now that's too big a job for one house to sort on its own, tha knows. You can't just get t' bow saw out for a tree like that, not shift it missen if tha tried all day. So it were the fella two doors up who went round first, still in his dressing gown, banging on doors while it were barely light, shouting through letterboxes, ey up, now then, ow do, get thissen up, tree's down and it's blocking t' ginnel. And sithee, word went up and down that street quicker than owt I've ever seen. Half hour, and there were folk coming out in wellies and old coats, some still with toast in their hand.

We reckoned on five or six of us turning to, the usual ones. What we got were nearer thirty, easy well over a dozen more than anybody had banked on. Even them offcumdens who's only moved in a few month back were out an' all, which surprised a fair few. Tha knows how it is, most days you'd be lucky to get two words off some of that street, but there they all were, stood about in t' ginnel scratching their heads at this great trunk laid across it like summat out of a nightmare.

So it got divided out between us. Them with chainsaws did the cutting, obviously, and the rest were shifting logs, stacking brash, one lot on the wall seeing what stone could be saved and what were smashed beyond it. And it were her husband from over the road, built like a barn door, who took on the heaviest end of it, near enough on his own hauling the big rounds up out of t' ginnel onto the trailer, one after another, hour after hour, never once stopping for a brew while the rest of us were having ours.

Course it weren't all smooth sailing. About dinnertime there were a right disaster. One of the branches they were cutting, still under tension from where it had been resting on t' wall, sprung back sudden and took the fence panel clean off next door's garden, near caught the young lad who were stood too close. No harm done, thank God, just a fright, but it put wind up everyone for a bit and there were a few gormless faces stood about after, wondering what to do next.

And then there were the argument, which I could've told thee were coming a mile off. One of the older fellas reckoned they should be cutting it length ways for logs to split later, and the fella with the chainsaw were having none of it, said he'd been doing this thirty year and knew what he were doing thank you very much, and for a bit there were proper chelp flying between them, looking fit to bray one another over the noise of the saw.

The neighbour: Now then, you two, pack it in, it's a tree not a war.

The old fella: I'm only saying, if tha cuts it there tha's wasting good wood.

The one with the chainsaw: And if tha wants to hold t' saw thissen, be my guest.

It settled itself though, way these things do, once somebody put a brew in each of their hands. It'll be reet, that's how it goes round here, always is.

There were food and drink out on t' street all day and all, more than one house brought summat out. There were a big pot of tea going from one kitchen, then bacon breadcakes appeared from another about eleven, and by afternoon somebody had a crate of bottles out the back of a car for them that wanted it. Kids had a job and all, mind, nowt too heavy, gathering up the little twigs and kindling into bundles and stacking it by t' side gate, roaring when they scratched theirsens on a bramble and straight back at it two minutes later. One of the older ones slipped them a bit of spice each for their trouble, which went down champion.

And the old lad from the end house, must be gone eighty, he weren't having any of this sitting-it-out business. Everyone kept telling him get in out the cold, sit thissen down, tha's done enough, but he wouldn't hear of it, stood there directing where the wood should go, telling folk they'd fettle it better stacked this way than that. And honestly, love, he were right an' all. Nobody argued with him twice.

There were one moment had the whole lot of us in stitches, mind, and I don't think I'll forget it. Somebody's dog, one of them daft little terriers, got hold of a glove off the top of the log pile and went streaking off down t' street with it like it were the crown jewels, and there were four grown men chasing after it in wellies, slipping about, shouting, and it just kept dodging them, tail going like mad. By the time they got it back everyone were doubled over, tears running, couldn't hardly speak for laughing. Even the old fella were laughing, and he doesn't laugh at owt normally.

We reckoned we'd have it all cleared wait while dinner, easy as owt. Dinnertime came and went and we were still at it, a good three hour past when anybody thought we'd be done, getting nithered as it got dark and the last light went off round about half seven with folk still out there in coats and torches held in their teeth. But by the end we'd cleared the whole ginnel right through, cut and stacked more logs than any of us knew what to do with, two walls built back up proper and the third under a tarp while it waits on more stone, and the greenhouse fence back up and all.

When the last log went on that stack there were a cheer went up right down t' street, folk banging bin lids and all sorts, daft as owt but it felt grand. Somebody shouted put t'wood in t'oil, tha's letting t' warm out, and that were that, everyone drifting back in their own doors at last.

And I'll tell thee, love, walking back in that night, street felt different somehow. Everyone knew everyone's name of a sudden, folk who'd not said two words to each other in years stood there laughing over a brew like they'd known each other forever. Might not last, these things never do, not properly, but for that one evening it were like the whole street were one house. Champion, that were. Absolutely champion.
