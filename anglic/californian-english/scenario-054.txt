Okay so people ask me all the time, like, how'd you even do this, and honestly the short answer is you just start and you don't let yourself think too hard about how long it's actually gonna take. Because if I'd known it was gonna be eleven months I don't know if I start. But I'm glad I didn't know.

So the whole thing kicked off because we just did not fit anymore. Four of us in a place that was built for like two people and a dog, and the dog didn't even live there anymore. The eldest was sleeping in what used to be a closet, I'm not exaggerating, we'd converted an actual closet into a bedroom situation and told ourselves it was cozy. It was not cozy. It was a closet. And the wife finally sat me down one night, we're doing dishes, and she's just like...

The wife: We can't keep doing this. Somebody's gotta get their own room before they turn into a bat.

Me: A bat.

The wife: A bat. No sunlight, sleeping standing up basically. We gotta build.

So that was it, that was the whole decision-making process, standing at the sink. We figured, okay, worst case it's a room and a bathroom off the back, maybe four months if we hustle, and it'll probably take like three of us, me, my buddy who'd done framing before, and whoever we could rope in on weekends. Four months. I want that on record because it is hilarious now.

Two weeks in, we get our first real gut punch. We'd laid out the footing and started framing the back wall, and my buddy's out there with the level going quiet, which is never good, he's a talker normally. And he just goes...

The buddy: Dude. This whole wall's like an inch and a half off plumb over the run. We built it off the wrong line.

Me: Off the wrong line how.

The buddy: I measured off the fence instead of the house. The fence isn't square, man. Nothing about that fence is square.

So that's two weeks of framing, gone, just tear it back down to the plates and start over measuring off the actual house like we should've done to begin with. That one stung. That was the first time I sat in my truck for a minute before going back in the house because I didn't wanna say anything I'd regret.

Then, like, the opposite thing happens maybe a month later. We're out there on a random Saturday, just me and my buddy again, and my brother-in-law shows up with his neighbor's kid who's maybe nineteen, strong as an ox, and neither of them were asked, they just showed up with a cooler and tools.

The brother-in-law: Heard you guys were still out here. Figured you could use hands that don't complain as much as mine usually do.

Me: You didn't have to do this.

The brother-in-law: I know. That's kinda the point.

That day we got more done than the previous two weekends combined, no joke. Six hands instead of two changes everything.

Somewhere around month four we got hit with a stretch of rain that just would not quit, which, this is LA, we don't get rain like that, everybody was acting like the sky owed us an apology. Whole week, basically, we lost, the framing was open, no roof deck yet, so there was just nothing to do but tarp it down every night and watch the weather app like it was gonna change its mind. It didn't. That week set us back more than the wrong-line thing did, honestly, because at least with the wall we could work while we fixed it.

Then right after that we run short on lumber, because obviously between the redo and losing a week we'd blown through what we bought. And prices had jumped since we bought the first batch, and neither of us wanted to drive all the way out for a small run, so we just kind of made do, used shorter studs doubled up where we'd normally use one long one, borrowed a stack of two-by-fours off a guy down the street who was tearing out a fence, stuff like that. It's not textbook. It works fine. You'd never know looking at it now, but I know, every time I walk past that section I know.

Somewhere in there I fell off the ladder putting up a header, not even that high up, maybe eight feet, but I came down wrong on my wrist and knocked the wind clean out of myself, and for about ten seconds nobody said anything, which is the scariest ten seconds of the whole project honestly. The wife had come out to bring drinks and she just froze.

The wife: Don't move. Don't you dare move.

Me: I'm okay. I think I'm okay.

The wife: You're not getting back on that ladder today.

I wasn't hurt, wrist was fine by the next week, but man did that scare everybody straight for like a solid month. Ladder rules got a lot stricter after that.

We had a real blowup too, over the roofline where it ties into the old part of the house. My buddy wanted to frame it one way, cleaner on paper, and I wanted to match the pitch of the original roof so it didn't look bolted on, and we went back and forth for like two days.

The buddy: It's gonna look like an afterthought if you don't match the pitch.

Me: It IS an afterthought, dude, that's literally what it is, it's an addition.

The buddy: Doesn't mean it has to look like one.

We ended up doing it his way and honestly he was right, but we did not speak about lumber for like a day and a half after that argument, we just worked.

The worst part, though, was the side wall. We'd already sheathed it, already had the window opening cut, and then the inspector's buddy who was helping us eyeball things pointed out the whole thing was out of square by enough that the window was never gonna sit right and the roof load above it was gonna be sitting wrong too. So that came all the way down. Not patched, not shimmed, all the way down to the studs, and we built it again from the bottom. That one hurt worse than the first mistake because we thought we were past making that kind of mistake by then.

But then there's the day the frame finally stood up all the way around, roof trusses set, and we're all just standing in what's gonna be the new room looking up at open sky through the rafters, and nobody said much, we just kind of stood there. My buddy cracked a beer and handed me one and we just looked at it for a while. That was a good day. That was the day it stopped feeling like a hole in the side of the house and started feeling like a room.

The last stretch was brutal though, the last four nights before we were trying to get it closed in, we were out there past dark every single one of those nights, shop lights clamped to the framing, running extension cords out the kitchen window, everybody's tired, everybody's short with each other, and we just pushed through it because we were so close.

And then it was just done. One evening we swept the floor and stood in the doorway and it was a room. A real room, with a real door, with the eldest's stuff already half moved in before we'd even painted.

When we added it all up after, it came out to eleven months start to finish, and six different people who put in real hours on it at some point along the way, not counting the folks who just came by with food, which, honestly, that helped too in its own way. Now I just walk in there sometimes for no reason, just to stand in it. It's quiet in there. You'd never know what it took to get quiet like that.
