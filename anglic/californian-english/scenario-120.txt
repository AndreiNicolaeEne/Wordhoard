Me: Okay so you know how I went around telling literally everybody I was gonna run a marathon, before I had done one single training run, not even like a lap around the block? Yeah. That was the dumbest and also kind of the smartest thing I've ever done, because once you've told your whole family and every group chat you're in, there's no quiet way to just, like, not do it anymore. So I was locked in whether I wanted to be or not.

And I did not want to be, the first couple weeks. I don't think I understood what I was asking my body to do. Like I thought, okay, I've walked around this city my whole life, I've done a spin class here and there, how bad can it be. Super bad. That's how bad. Every single run left me more wrecked than the one before it, which is not how I figured training was supposed to work. I thought it was supposed to get easier as you went. Nobody tells you the first few weeks just kick your whole ass and you have to survive that part before anything gets better.

But I settled into it after a while. Got a routine going and I actually stuck to it, which if you know me is not really my thing. Didn't matter if it was raining, didn't matter if I was in a mood about work or whatever, I just laced up and went. Same loop most days, down along the river path, out and back, didn't even care that it was boring because the boring was kind of the point. You're not chasing a good time in week three, you're just trying to teach your body this is a thing we do now.

And then, dude, I got hurt. Like really hurt, not just sore. Something in my hip just gave out on me on an ordinary run, nothing dramatic happened, no fall, nothing, it just went, and I had to stop cold. For a couple weeks I genuinely did not know if I was gonna get back to it at all. I remember lying on the couch icing it thinking, this is it, this is how the whole thing ends, before it even really started, and I'm gonna have to tell everyone I flaked. That was almost worse than the hip.

I did come back, eventually, but way more careful. Cut the plan down, backed off the mileage, gave myself actual rest days instead of just powering through everything like before. Learned that the hard way, obviously, the way you learn most things.

Part of the season I had somebody training with me, which honestly made the whole thing survivable. She was up from the Bay area for work for a few months and got roped into it same as I got roped into telling everyone. And having somebody there on the bad days made such a difference, even on the days it didn't seem like it should matter. Like there'd be mornings neither of us wanted to be out there, freezing, tired, whatever, and one of us would just go "we're already dressed" and that was enough. She always said everything was hella this or hella that, hella early, hella far, and it caught on with me a little, I'd catch myself saying it too.

The Training Partner: Dude, if we stop now we just have to start warming up again tomorrow, that's worse.

Me: Which, annoyingly, was always true.

There was one training day though that stands out over literally everything else, and it almost ended the whole attempt right there, no race required. Long run, and about two thirds in, my body and my head both just quit on me at the same time. Like usually it's one or the other, your legs are fine but your head's not in it, or your head's fine but your legs are toast. This time both went at once. I sat down on a curb somewhere off the 134 corridor, just sat right down on the sidewalk, and I remember thinking super clearly, I don't actually have to finish this. Nobody's making me. I could just walk home right now and quit the whole thing and eventually people would stop asking about it. I sat there a long time before I got back up.

The middle of the whole season was the worst for doubt, way worse than that one bad day even. The finish still felt so far off it barely felt real, like a thing that happened to other people, not something on my actual calendar. That's the stretch where you're too far in to quit clean but not close enough to feel any pull forward. Just grinding through weeks that don't seem to add up to anything.

And then there was this one run, nothing special about it on paper, where everything just clicked. Same route, same pace I'd been failing to hold for weeks, and it just felt totally different, like my body had finally caught up to what I was asking it to do. Nothing dramatic, no fireworks, just a normal Tuesday run that went better than any run before it. But it fixed something in my head that had been broken for weeks. I remember calling somebody after just to say, I think I can actually do this.

Toward the end I got real strict with myself, more than I'd been the whole season. Watching what I ate, actually sleeping, saying no to stuff I would've said yes to before. And then the last week, which felt so wrong, I backed way off instead of pushing harder. Every instinct in me said train more, you're running out of time, and instead I did basically nothing, trusted that the months already in the bank were the thing that mattered now, not some last minute save.

The night before I barely slept at all. Just laid there running the whole thing in my head over and over, way earlier than there was any reason to be thinking about it, like at two in the morning going through what I'd eat, what I'd wear, what if my hip did the thing again. None of it useful. Just lying there awake with it.

And then the day itself. Fine for a long time, honestly, better than fine, like all that training actually held. But the last stretch, the last few miles, that's where it all came due. Everything in me wanted to stop, just stop right there and walk it in, and I didn't, I don't even totally know how. Just kept the legs moving when there was nothing left telling them to.

And then it was just, over. Which is such a weird thing to say about something you build up in your head for a whole season. I thought I'd feel like some huge wave of something, and instead I just felt kind of hollowed out and confused, standing there with a medal on not really processing anything, everyone around me hugging people and crying and I just felt totally blank for a good ten minutes before anything hit me at all. Nothing like what I'd pictured.

Took days after before my body felt like mine again, honestly. Walking down stairs was a whole production for like four or five days. But underneath all that, the thing that actually stuck with me wasn't the finish line, it was everything before it, the whole grinding middle of it that I almost quit twice. I know now I can do stuff I have zero idea how to do yet, which sounds like nothing, but it's not nothing, it changed what I think I'm actually capable of when I get it in my head to try something stupid again.
