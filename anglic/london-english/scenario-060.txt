Right, so nan's house finally got cleared out proper, the whole thing, top to bottom in one day, because the sale was going through and if it never got done by the weekend the whole chain was gonna fall apart, and none of us could keep the place going anyway, not with the roof how it was and the wiring nan never let a soul touch. She'd been gone since the spring but the house just sat there like she'd nipped out the shops, curtains shut, milk gone off in the fridge months back and nobody had the heart to even open that door. So everyone linked up for it. Not just the close lot either, my mum, my two aunties, my uncle, but the other auntie's husband and all, the cousins, the little ones dragged along, plus the neighbour from two doors down who used to do nan's shopping for her every Thursday, and the family friend who sorted her boiler out every winter for donkeys years, said he wasn't missing this for the world, and fair enough, he'd been round that yard more than half of us these last few years.

We started in the front room, cause that's where you always start, innit, first room you walk into. My uncle's going through the sideboard drawers and he pulls out granddad's old lighter, the silver one that never even worked, granddad used to keep flicking it anyway like one day it might catch. My uncle just went quiet holding it, turning it over in his hand.

The uncle: Blimey. Ain't seen this in donkey's years. Used to let me have a go on it when I was small, told me not to tell your nan.

The eldest: You alright there?

The uncle: Yeah yeah, man's alright, just took me back a bit is all. That's my dad's, that.

We left the front room half done after that, cause it's the room everyone ends up sitting in with a brew all day, and went up to nan's bedroom next. That's where the second thing turned up, one nobody could make head nor tail of. Tucked inside an old rent book like a bookmark, a little black and white photo, a woman none of us had ever clocked in our lives.

The auntie: Who is this then. Never seen her in my life, and I've known your nan longer than any of you lot.

The cousin: Rah, is that not nan's sister though.

The auntie: Nan never had a sister, love, don't talk daft.

Went round the whole family twice that photo did, and by the evening still nobody had a clue. Someone's got it in a pocket now probably, still a mystery, forever gonna be one.

After the bedroom we did the landing cupboard, and that's where the argument got settled, over the big oak dresser that's stood there since before any of us were born. Two of the aunties both wanted it, going back and forth a good half hour, taking the mick out of each other over who'd actually got the wall space, till the eldest put her foot down.

The eldest: Right, that's going to her, end of, she's got the room for it and the rest of us ain't, I'm not hearing it no more.

The auntie: Fine. Calm. I weren't even that bothered.

The eldest: You was proper bothered two minutes ago.

The auntie: Say less.

Settled just like that, nobody brought it up again the whole day.

The one that never got settled that clean was up in the box room, nan's old fur coat, still on its hanger under a sheet. One auntie wanted it kept no matter the state, one of the cousins reckoned it should go straight in the skip.

The auntie: You are not binning that, that's a proper coat, they don't make them like that no more.

The cousin: It stinks of damp though, swear down, moths have had it, look at the state of that sleeve.

The auntie: So air it out then, don't just chuck it.

The cousin: Nobody's wearing that, man, allow it, it's had its time. Keeping every clapped old rag in the family's a bit long if you ask me.

The auntie: Nobody did ask you.

Went round in circles all afternoon that one did, and in the end it went in a bag "for later," which everybody knows means it sits in somebody's shed the next ten years and nobody ever decides.

The loft nobody wanted to do, cause you had to go up the little ladder and it was bare dusty up there, spiders and all, and that's where the family friend found the thing nobody was expecting. A biscuit tin taped right up in the eaves, wedged behind the water tank, stuffed with old notes, proper old ones, folded up tight, must've sat there decades.

The family friend: Oi. Oi, you lot need to see this. Your nan's been sat on a little goldmine up here and never breathed a word.

The cousin: Oh my days. No way. That is mental, that.

The uncle: That is so like her and all, secretive old cow, God rest her.

No note with it, nothing to say who it was even for, so that got set aside to sort properly, no rushing that one.

The kitchen took longer than every other room put together, easy, no competition. Tins going back years past the date, a whole cupboard of nothing but carrier bags stuffed inside other carrier bags, a drawer that was just rubber bands and twist ties and dead batteries, four sets of scales, every mug ever given free with a jar of coffee. Hours we were in there, bag, keep, bin, bag, keep, bin, and by the end everyone was well done in with it, moaning the whole way through.

The little cousin: This drawer ain't even real, bruv, why's she got forty of the same elastic band.

Me: That's nan for you though, innit, never binned nothing in her life.

We broke for food about two, everyone piled out in the garden on whatever chairs weren't loaded in the van already. The family friend had brought a proper spread, sandwiches, a flask of tea, biscuits going round, everyone gassed at the sight of it after that kitchen. The neighbour told stories about nan that half the young ones had never heard in their lives, everyone creasing up, the little ones running round the garden, proper nice bit of the day if I'm honest, before we all trooped back in and got stuck in again.

Back in the front room for the last stretch, and the piano was the big one nobody in the end wanted, out of tune years back, heavier than anything else in the house. Every single one of us said the same when it come up.

The uncle: I ain't got the room for that.

The auntie: Nor me, and I can't even play the thing.

The eldest: Nobody's touched that piano since I was at school, it's a violation even calling it a piano at this point.

In the end a bloke off one of them apps come and took it away for the wood, no fuss, gone on the back of a van, nobody looking twice as it went.

One thing we all agreed on with zero argument at all was the clock off the mantelpiece, the one nan wound up herself every single Sunday without fail her whole life. Soon as it come up, everyone just looked at the same auntie.

The eldest: That's got to be hers, she's the one who kept it going all them years.

The auntie: Alright then, if nobody's fighting me for it.

The cousins: Nah, go on, take it.

Nobody said another word about it, done and dusted.

By early evening the front room was the last one standing with anything left in it, just nan's chair by the window, and for a minute nobody moved to touch it. My mum sat in it a bit before it got carried out, didn't say nothing, just sat there quiet a minute, and then it went out with everything else and the room stood there properly empty, echoing the way empty rooms do when they've had a whole life in them.

What was left after that, odds off the windowsills, tea towels, ornaments nobody had strong feelings on either way, all got spread across the one table still standing in the kitchen and everyone just took what they fancied, no arguing left in anybody by that point, calm about the whole thing, everyone had had their moment earlier on.

Last thing, my uncle did one final walk round on his own, every room, checked nothing left in any cupboard, and then he locked the front door and stood on the step a minute with the key in his hand before he handed it over to whoever was doing the paperwork on it. And that was it. House shut for good. Everyone off home in cars loaded right up, quiet on the drive back if I'm honest, quieter than anyone let on all day.
