Well since you asked, and you fixin' to be out here a good while I reckon, might as well hear it right instead of the half of it y'all been catchin' on porches. Pull that chair on up. This gon' take a minute.

It started, oh, back in the spring some. The mail carrier was the first one to say anything, told the lady two doors down she seen somethin' low and quick cut across at the end of the street right at dusk, said it moved like a dog but wasn't built like one, too long in the leg and too mean-lookin' in the face. Everybody just laughed her off. Said she'd been out in that sun too long, said her eyes was playin' tricks, bless her heart, she does wear them thick glasses. Didn't nobody think another thing about it.

Then not two weeks later the deacon seen it. Now you gotta understand, when the deacon says he seen somethin', the street believes it, 'cause that man don't lie and he don't exaggerate neither, he's about the most level-headed one we got out here. He was cuttin' his grass right at that gray part of the evenin' and he stopped his mower dead and just watched it walk the fence line, calm as you please, like it owned the place. He come on over that same night, still had his cuttin' clothes on, and said:

The deacon: "I'm tellin' you what I seen. Wasn't nobody's imagination. That thing is real and it is out here."

After that it wasn't a rumor no more, it was a fact everybody had to deal with. And deal with it we did, or tried to, 'cause the street split clean down the middle over what that dealin' ought to look like. One side, mostly the older kinfolk who done seen everything twice already, said leave it be, it ain't botherin' nobody that ain't botherin' it first, God don't make nothin' for no reason. The other side, and this was mostly the younger families with the little young'uns runnin' round barefoot till dark, said no ma'am, somethin' that size don't belong loose on a street with children on it, and it needs to be dealt with, one way or another.

And Lord it did keep showin' out. Got in trash cans up and down both sides, tipped over three or four of 'em in one week, had coffee grounds and chicken bones drug clean across the widow's front yard. Got into somebody's garden too, tore up a whole row of tomatoes, and there was a mess of greens some lady had comin' in nice that got trampled flat one night. Nobody actually seen it do the tomatoes, mind you, but wasn't nothin' else it coulda been, so it got the blame same as if they had.

Then the little dog went missin' off the porch two houses down from me. Just gone one mornin', gate still latched, no blood, no nothin', just gone. And that family was tore up about it, and half the street was ready to hang that varmint right then without a trial, and the other half kept sayin' now hold on, we don't know that, coulda been a car, coulda been anything, ain't no proof it was that animal at all. That argument got loud. We had us a get-together, was s'posed to be just a cookout, and it turned into a whole meetin' right there in the yard, everybody talkin' over everybody.

The mother of that family: "I don't care what proof y'all need, my child's dog is gone and that thing has been in this yard, and I want somethin' done about it."

Her husband: "Baby, we might could just wait and see, we don't even know for sure..."

The mother of that family: "Don't you 'wait and see' me, I have had it up to here."

Somebody's granddaddy hollerin' from the back that folks was madder than a wet hen over a dog that mighta just wandered off on its own, and folks pitchin' a fit like the world was endin'. It got cattywampus real quick, everybody talkin' past one another, and didn't nothin' get settled that night except that somethin' had to change.

One fella, the one who lives at the end by hisself, decided he'd just handle it his own self, didn't want to wait on the street to agree on nothin'. Set him up some kind of trap out of a milk crate and a broom handle, baited it with lunch meat. Caught nothin' but his own dadgum trash lid, and the varmint done been in his yard again by the next mornin' anyhow, bolder than before, like it knew. He come out braggin' he'd handle it and went right back to admittin' he liketa couldn't handle a raccoon in a Havahart trap, let alone whatever this was.

Then come the night that really turned it. The widow lady was takin' her trash out late, hadn't even mashed the porch light button good, and that thing come up right at the edge of her carport, close enough she could hear it breathin', she said, and it looked right at her before it took off. She hollered loud enough three houses heard her. Scared her so bad she didn't sleep right for a week, said her heart still gets to poundin' just tellin' it.

The widow lady: "I ain't sayin' it come after me. I'm sayin' it didn't run when it seen me, and that ain't right for somethin' wild."

That's when the whole disagreement come to a head. Some folks said that proved it was dangerous, plain and simple, standin' its ground like that. Others said naw, that thing ain't dangerous, it's just unlucky in where it ended up, probably pushed out from wherever it used to be on account of all that buildin' they been doin' off the main road, just tryin' to make do same as anybody. Went back and forth on that for near a month, couldn't nobody agree, and the street was gettin' plumb tired of arguin' about it at every mailbox.

Finally somebody's cousin who does somethin' with animal control on the county side, agreed to come out on his day off and take a look, on account of kinfolk, and 'cause it was gettin' to where folks were fixin' to do somethin' foolish on their own if nobody stepped in. He walked the whole street, looked at where it'd been diggin', looked at the fence, looked at the trash situation, and told us straight.

The one from animal control: "Y'all got a coyote come down off that cleared land yonder. He ain't huntin' children, ma'am, I promise you that, but he is hungry and he is smart, and every can y'all leave unlatched is you trainin' him to stay."

And that settled it, more or less. The terms come down to: secure every can on the street, latched or bungee-corded, don't leave a bit of pet food out overnight, don't feed it out of pity and don't try to trap it out of spite, and if it come round in daylight or got too bold, call the county, don't handle it yourself. Both sides could live with that, even if neither side got exactly what they wanted first.

Since then it's been quiet, quiet as it can be with somethin' like that still out there. Everybody's got their cans strapped down tight now, cuts the lights on 'fore they take the trash out at night, and the deacon still keeps half an eye toward that fence line most evenings out of habit more than fear. We see it every now and again still, trottin' along yonder past the streetlight round midnight, and don't nobody get too excited about it no more. It's just part of the street now, matter of fact, comes up more at supper than it does at any meetin'. Somebody'll say "you hear that thing last night" and somebody else'll say "reckon he's just makin' his rounds" and that's the end of it, and everybody laughs a little now where they used to argue. Started out like it was fixin' to be a whole disaster and it settled down into just a story we tell, mostly for the new folks like yourself. God willing and the creek don't rise, it'll stay that way.
