Me: You know I ain't hardly stopped moving all day, but I got to tell you about that tree, cause you wasn't here for it and I done told everybody else already.

It was that big old pecan behind the yellow house, the one been leaning bad ever since that storm come up out of nowhere last Tuesday, you remember I told you it come up a cloud so fast we ain't had time to get the porch furniture in off the steps. Split right down near the base and laid itself over cattywampus, half across the fence line into the next yard and half hung up on the power line, and the rest of it just sprawled clean across the street. Wasn't no way one household was fixin' to move a thing that size by theirselves, not with it setting there threatening to finish falling any which way it pleased, on the wire or on somebody's carport, whichever it took a mind to.

So the man whose yard it was actually leaning into, he come out first thing that morning and didn't even call nobody on the phone, just stood out there and hollered down the row, "I need every man, woman, and child that can hold a rake, we got to get this off the wire before it takes the whole street's lights out." And Lord, word went up and down that street faster than anything I ever did see. Wasn't twenty minutes before doors was opening up on both sides and folks coming out still in their house shoes.

I figured maybe six or seven of us would turn out for something like that, that's about what I reckoned on going in. Come to find out it was closer to thirty folks total by the time it was all said and done, well more than anybody called for or expected, chainsaws and rakes and wheelbarrows all coming out the garages at once. Somebody even came up from the next street over, said they heard the chainsaws going and figured they might could make theirselves useful.

Work got divided up quick, didn't nobody hardly have to say a word about it. Two or three of the men had chainsaws and they took it down section by section, the rest of us hauling and dragging into piles at the curb, and the young'uns got sent off with the little sticks and leaves, filling up that red wagon over and over till their arms was about to fall off. One of the men backed his truck in close as he dared and directed where the big pieces ought to go so it could all get hauled off directly, once it was cut small enough to lift.

But the heaviest of it, the trunk itself, that fell to the biggest young man on the block, and I mean he worked that chainsaw near the whole day through, section after section, sweated clean through two shirts by noon. Wasn't a one of us pulled the load he did, and he wasn't showing out about it neither, just steady the whole time, cut a piece, four or five of us half carry half drag it off, and he'd already be starting the next cut before we got back to him.

Had us a scare round about midday too. A limb come down wrong, caught the wind funny or something, and it busted clean through the back glass of a truck parked too close in the driveway. Whole street jumped and went quiet a second, then everybody hollering at once was anybody hurt, and thank the Lord wasn't nobody anywhere near it. The truck's owner had every right to pitch a fit over that glass, but he just stood there looking at it shaking his head, said something wasn't fit to repeat, and somebody hollered back, "well, bless his heart, coulda been a whole lot worse," which it surely could have.

That's about when the argument kicked up, over the last big section still hanging half on the wire. Her husband was dead set it ought to come down toward the street where there was more room for it, and the man from the corner house swore it had to drop back into the yard or it'd take the wire down with it, and he got madder than a wet hen when the other one said he didn't know what he was talking about and never had. Somebody tried to say maybe they might should just leave it be till the county come out, but wasn't nobody listening to that by then. Took the oldest man there, must be near eighty if he's a day, to walk over slow and say, low and even, "don't be ugly now, both of you. Drop it in the yard, and drop it now, before this whole street loses power. Used to could clear one twice that size before dinner, back in my day, so quit your fussing and get to it." Wasn't another word said about it. Went in the yard just like he told them.

And the food, Lord, the food. Mama an' them from down the row had a whole pitcher of sweet tea out before ten that morning, and it wasn't the last pitcher neither. Another house sent out a plate of fried chicken and said they had a mess of greens already going on the stove they'd bring round come supper. Cokes coming out of two different coolers all day, didn't matter whose they was, everybody just helped theirselves. Somebody had to run to the Piggly Wiggly midafternoon for more ice, left the buggy sitting right there in the aisle when they remembered they forgot cups too. Somebody's aunt showed up round two with a watermelon big as a washtub and cut it right there on the tailgate.

The oldest woman on the street, she wasn't fixin' to sit that one out on the porch just watching, no ma'am. She come down them steps with her own folding chair and set herself up right by the curb directing where every pile needed to go, this one's firewood, that one's trash, don't you go putting them together. Somebody tried telling her she ought to rest some, and she looked at him like he'd lost his mind and said she'd rest when the job was done and not one minute before.

We all liketa died laughing one time, right in the middle of the worst of it. A limb come swinging down closer than anybody meant it to, and one of the men jumped back so hard he went down flat in the ditch, mud and all, but he had him a full glass of tea in his hand the whole time and didn't spill one drop, held it straight up over his head the whole way down like it was the only thing on this earth that mattered. Sat there in the mud holding that glass up plumb proud of himself, and the whole street just lost it. He was still finding mud behind his ear that evening and we was still bringing it up on him.

We all figured, God willing and the creek don't rise, that job would be cleared out by two or three that afternoon at the latest. Ended up running a good three hours past that, somebody finally having to cut the porch lights on so we could see to finish. But by the time it was all said and done, we'd cleared both them yards, opened the whole street back up both ways, fixed the fence that got knocked cattywampus in the middle of it, and hauled off what must have been six or seven truckloads of wood and brush stacked off yonder past the ditch waiting on pickup. Cheer went up loud when that last big piece finally come clear of the wire, everybody clapping and hollering like we'd won something.

And I'm telling you, that street felt different that evening. The oldest woman went round to every last one of us before we scattered off home and said, "gimme some sugar" and made us each stop for a hug whether we wanted one or not. Folks was still out on their porches long after dark, talking, kids still running around underfoot, people that ain't said two words to each other in better than a year sitting out sharing the same pitcher of tea like it was nothing. Might not last past the one evening. But for that one evening, it was a different street than the one we woke up on.
