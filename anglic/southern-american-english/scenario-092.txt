By the time the rest of them had cleared out it was just the five left at the kitchen table, the good dishes still stacked up from where the church ladies brought them and nobody had the heart yet to wash. The overhead light was off and just the one lamp going, and outside the kitchen window it had come up cloudy earlier and then cleared off, and now you could see stars plain as anything through the screen.

The eldest had her elbows on the table and her chin propped up, and she was the one that started it.

The eldest: Y'all remember how he'd cut them lights on in the mornin' 'fore anybody else was even up, walkin' through every room in the house flippin' switches like he owned the electric company himself. Wouldn't say a word to nobody, just cut the lights on and go on to the kitchen.

The middle one: It wasn't every room, now. He never did the back bedroom. You always leave that part out.

The eldest: He did too.

The middle one: He did not, I liketa broke my neck in there more mornin's than I can count 'cause it was black as pitch and he never once cut that light on.

The eldest: Well. I reckon you're right about that. I always get that part wrong, don't I.

The baby sister let a laugh out then that she plainly did not mean to, quick and short, and put her hand up over her mouth like she could take it back, but nobody minded it at all. It felt good in the room, even.

The baby sister: I'm sorry. I don't know why that tickled me.

The neighbor: Don't be sorry, honey. Ain't nothin' wrong with a laugh in here tonight. He'd be the first one laughin' at us for sittin' round bein' so solemn about it anyhow.

The granddaughter had been quiet a good while, turning her cup around on the table, and then she spoke up.

The granddaughter: Y'all remember what he'd say when the phone rang durin' supper. Every single time.

The middle one: Lord, don't even.

The granddaughter, in the exact voice, slow and put-on: "Now who in the world is callin' here at this hour, botherin' folks at their supper." Every night. Didn't matter if it was six o'clock or eight-thirty, it was always "at this hour."

That got the table going, a real warm kind of laughing, because every one of them could hear him say it plain as day, and for a second it was like he'd said it right there in the room with them.

Then the neighbor set his cup down slow and looked around at all of them like he was deciding something.

The neighbor: I don't guess any of y'all ever knew this, and I don't know as I ought to be the one to tell it, but he give me two hundred dollars back in. Lord, this had to be near thirty years ago now, to help me get my truck out of hock, and told me not to say a word to your mama an' them about it. Never asked me for it back. Never brought it up again in his life.

The baby sister: He did WHAT.

It came out of her sharp, surprised right out of her, and then she caught herself, and the room went quiet a minute, just the sound of the refrigerator humming and somebody's fork against a plate.

The eldest broke it, reaching for the pitcher.

The eldest: Anybody need their tea freshened up? There's more sweet tea in the icebox if this pitcher's runnin' low, and I believe there's still a coke or two left if somebody wants somethin' with some fizz to it.

She got up and went around the table topping off glasses, and that gave everybody something to do with their hands a minute, which was probably the whole point of it.

The middle one, low, to the eldest, not really meaning for it to go anywhere: You know you never did pay me back for that fence post money either.

The eldest: Don't start with me tonight.

The middle one: I ain't startin' nothin'. I'm just sayin' it's been fifteen years and some of us keep a memory too.

The eldest: Well, all y'all can take that up some other night. Not tonight.

And that was the end of it, quick as it came up, both of them letting it drop the way old business drops when there's a bigger thing sitting in the room with you.

The neighbor, easing things back, shaking his head with a grin already starting: Now let me tell y'all about the time he would not, under any circumstances, admit that riding mower was broke. You remember that, don't you.

The middle one: Lord have mercy, the mower.

The neighbor: Man drug that thing out in the yard every Saturday for near about two months, pullin' the cord, cussin' at it a little, pullin' it again, and it never once turned over. Not once. And every time somebody'd say, "Why don't you just take it in and let somebody look at it," he'd get to where his jaw'd set and he'd say...

The eldest, joining in, both of them together now: "Ain't nothin' wrong with it that I can't fix myself."

The neighbor: And he never did fix it. Bought a brand new one that Christmas and wouldn't say a word about the old one, just had it hauled off like it never existed.

The baby sister: Bless his heart, he was plumb stubborn as a mule about that thing.

The eldest: He was stubborn as a mule about most things, if we're bein' honest.

The granddaughter: I think that's just what he was, though. Wasn't it. Stubborn. In the good way, mostly.

Nobody argued with that. It sat there in the middle of the table a minute like everybody had quietly agreed on it without ever once saying the words out loud, that stubbornness was the thing, that if you had to boil the man down to one single trait to hand somebody who never knew him, that would be it, and it wasn't even really a complaint the way they said it now.

The baby sister had gone quiet, and when the eldest glanced over she saw her sister's eyes had gone wet and were spilling over slow, no sound to it at all, just tears coming down while she still had her hand around her glass.

Nobody stopped talking. The eldest reached over without a word and put her hand flat on the baby sister's back, rubbing it slow, and the middle one kept right on talking like nothing was happening, which was exactly the right thing to do.

The middle one: I reckon we're gonna need somebody to call the man about the headstone directly, and somebody's got to deal with all them clothes in the closet, I ain't got the first idea what mama's gonna want done with all that.

The neighbor: I can run the truck over Tuesday if somebody needs things hauled off to the church for the clothes closet there. Might could do it Monday too if that suits better.

The eldest: Tuesday's fine. I'll call about the stone in the mornin', and somebody needs to go on and get the death certificates from the funeral home, they said it'd be a few days yet.

The granddaughter: I can do that part. I don't mind.

The middle one: All right then. That's settled, I reckon.

It got quiet again after that, comfortable this time, the baby sister's breathing evened back out and she wiped her face with the back of her hand and didn't say nothing about it, and nobody made her.

It was getting on toward one in the morning and the neighbor was the last one still upright, the rest of them worn down soft in their chairs, and he lifted his glass of tea like it was something stronger.

The neighbor: I ain't got nothin' fancy to say. I just, here's to him. Man never did a thing halfway in his life, good or bad, and I reckon that's about the finest thing you can say about anybody. So. Here's to him, and God willing and the creek don't rise, we'll all be sittin' round some table again tellin' these same stories twenty years from now.

The eldest: Here's to Daddy.

They touched their glasses together soft, hardly a sound to it, and sat on a while longer in the quiet, none of them in any hurry yet to be the one that finally said it was time to go on to bed.
