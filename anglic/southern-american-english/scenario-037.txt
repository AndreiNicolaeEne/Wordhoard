I woke up 'fore first light 'cause my granddaddy was already bangin' round the kitchen like he was fixin' to wake the dead, and I reckon he was fixin' to wake the dead, on account of it wadn't but four-thirty and the sky out the window was still black as a skillet. He hollered up the stairs, "Y'all get up now, I ain't callin' but the once," and that was the truth, he never did call twice, so I was down them stairs with one shoe on before I even had my eyes all the way open.

Mama was already at the stove with coffee going and a pan of biscuits in the oven, and she said, don't be ugly about it, eat somethin' 'fore you go out there, 'cause ain't nobody workin' a whole day on an empty stomach in my house. So we all sat round quick and ate, and Granddaddy laid out who was doin' what soon as the last biscuit was gone.

Granddaddy: All right now. Beans is comin' in thick this year and them tomatoes ain't gonna wait on us neither, so we got to move. Uncle, you and the oldest boy get after the corn, that's the heavy part and y'all's the ones for it. Rest of us'll start on the beans and work our way to the tomatoes.

The uncle didn't say nothin' but "yes sir" and went on to get the wagon hooked up, 'cause pullin' corn and haulin' it in by the sackful is about the hardest lick of the whole day, and he's built for it, big as he is.

The youngest one, bless her heart, wadn't but seven, so Granddaddy set her to gatherin' up whatever ears fell loose in the dirt and totin' the water jug round to whoever hollered for it. Easy work, but she took to actin' like she was runnin' the whole operation, orderin' everybody round like she was grown, and my aunt just laughed and said, look at her showin' out already, and she ain't even had her breakfast good yet.

We got out to the beans while it was still cool and the dew was heavy on everything, and we went down them rows steady, me and my sister and my aunt and the mama, pickin' with both hands, and Granddaddy movin' up and down behind us checkin' our work like always. Once we cleared the first section of beans, he switched us over, put me and my sister on the tomatoes and kept the mama and my aunt pickin' the last of the beans behind, so we wadn't all bunched up on one thing.

Along about mid-mornin' my aunt started fallin' behind on her row, and I could tell somethin' wadn't right, she'd gotten quiet and slow, and her back was botherin' her somethin' terrible from the bendin'. Wadn't nobody said a word about it, no fussin', the mama just eased on over and started workin' the back half of my aunt's row along with her own, and my sister slid down and picked up the other end without bein' told, and directly the row was done same as any other, and wadn't nothin' said about who did what.

Round about eleven-thirty, the mama and my other aunt come out from the house with a wash tub full of fried chicken and a whole mess of greens cooked down with fatback, and cornbread, and a jug of sweet tea sweatin' in the heat, and we all sat down on the tailgate and in the shade of the truck and ate like we hadn't seen food in a week. My uncle come in off the corn field soaked through and said he could eat a horse and chase the rider, and Granddaddy told him he might could slow down some or he was fixin' to make himself sick, and everybody laughed.

That's when the argument started, if you want to call it that, over how my uncle was snappin' the ears off the stalk.

The oldest boy: You're doin' it wrong, you're s'posed to twist it down, not yank it straight back, you're liketa breakin' half them stalks off at the root.

The uncle: Boy, I been pullin' corn since 'fore your mama was born, don't tell me how to pull corn.

The oldest boy: Well you're still doin' it wrong.

Granddaddy: Both y'all hush and eat. Ain't neither one of you doin' it right, that's why we ain't but half done.

Everybody got a good laugh out of that and it settled down quick, wadn't no real heat behind it, just two men bein' stubborn same as always.

'Bout the time we got back up to go at it again, my aunt looked off to the west and said, Lord, look at that, and sure enough there was a cloud comin' up dark and low over the tree line, and the wind had done shifted and gone cool all of a sudden, the way it does right 'fore a storm. Granddaddy studied on it a minute and said we better get the canning jars and lids covered up under the shed roof case it come up on us quick, and quit pickin' tomatoes for now on account of a hard rain'll bust 'em where they stand. So we all pivoted over to gettin' things covered and moved, my sister and the youngest runnin' the tarps over the crates, and the mama sayin' Lord willin' and the creek don't rise we'll still get done today.

But that cloud passed on off to the north without droppin' but a few fat drops on us, and once it cleared up bright again Granddaddy said, all right, back at it, we got daylight to make up for, and we pushed on hard through the afternoon, the sun comin' back out hot and sticky and everybody sweatin' through their shirts.

That's when the trouble hit. The wagon my uncle had been haulin' corn in got loaded up too heavy on one side and went over cattywampus in a rut, dumped near 'bout a whole load of ears back out in the dirt, and my uncle come up cussin' under his breath, madder than a wet hen, kickin' at the wheel. Granddaddy just walked over calm as anything and said, well, don't stand there hollerin' at it, get it back up, and we all come over and helped set the wagon back to rights and gathered the spilled ears back up by hand, and wadn't but twenty minutes lost, though my uncle grumbled about it the rest of the day.

By late afternoon the light started slantin' long and gold through the trees, and Granddaddy hollered down the row, come on now, pick it up, we're fixin' to lose the light, and everybody moved quicker after that, hardly talkin', just workin' hand over hand.

The very last section was the far corner of the tomato patch, and by the time we got to it everybody had converged on that one spot together, all of us in a line, pickin' side by side till the last vine was stripped clean, and Granddaddy stood back and looked over the whole field, all them rows laid bare, and said, well, that's it, that's the last of it.

We tallied it up settin' on the porch that evening, near about forty bushel of beans, better'n sixty bushel of corn, and I don't know how many crates of tomatoes, plus that mess of greens from that mornin', and Granddaddy said it was as good a haul as he'd seen in a right many years.

Then everybody just about collapsed, settin' down wherever they landed, my uncle stretched out flat on the porch boards, my aunt with her feet up on the rail, the youngest already half asleep in the mama's lap. Somebody mashed the button on the radio and let it play low, and nobody said much of nothin' for a good while, just wore slap out.

'Fore anybody was let to set there for good though, Granddaddy looked over at the oldest boy and said, go on and cut the lights on out in the barn and shut them chickens up for the night 'fore it gets full dark, and the boy groaned but got up and went and did it, and only after that did Granddaddy let out a long breath and say, all right, now we can rest.
