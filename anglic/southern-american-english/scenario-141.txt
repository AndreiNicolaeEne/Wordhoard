I had everybody cut the TV off and cut their phones on silent, and I sent the young'uns out back to run around till I called em in, and still didn't nobody ask me what this was about. They just come on in and set down where I told em to set, the mother in her chair by the window, the father on the end of the couch with his knee already going, the middle uncle over by the door like he half expected to need to leave quick, the oldest uncle in the recliner with his hands folded in his lap real still, and the cousin perched on the arm of the couch next to her daddy. I poured everybody sweet tea whether they wanted it or not and I set the pitcher down and I looked around at all of em and I said what I come to say.

Me: I got something I been settin' on for going on thirty years now, and it is past time I said it. So I'm fixin' to say it, and I need y'all to let me get all the way through before anybody says a word back.

Wasn't a one of em said nothing. They already knew, the way kinfolk know, that this wasn't gonna be small.

Me: Y'all remember Big Mama's café. Y'all remember the night it burned.

The mother's hand went up to her mouth just like that, same as it done that night thirty years back, and the father's knee stopped going.

Me: Y'all remember what it done to this family. Big Mama never did get right after it, and we all know that place was everything she had, everything she built up outta nothin', and one night it's just gone, and the insurance man come around asking questions about a fryer left running and next thing you know it's whispered all over that it was arson, and this family like to come apart at the seams over it. Y'all remember who got blamed.

I looked right at the middle uncle when I said it, and he looked right back at me, and I could see him bracing like a man does when he hears a truck backfire and thinks it's a gun.

Me: Everybody said it was you. Said you was the one closed up that night, said you left that fryer going same as you always was a little careless, a little quick to get on down the road when you shoulda stayed and checked. And this family was madder at you than a wet hen for years behind it. Wouldn't nobody let it go. You went from being somebody's favorite to being the one folks got quiet around at Thanksgiving. You lost that business right along with Big Mama losing it, cause won't nobody trust you with so much as running the register at the flea market after that. I watched you carry that thirty years, and I let you.

The middle uncle's jaw was working but he didn't say nothing yet.

Me: It wasn't you.

The room went still as a held breath.

Me: I'm gonna say who it was, plain, cause you all done let this man carry a reputation that never was his to carry, and I ain't doing that to him one more day. It was the oldest uncle. He's the one that went back in that café that night, after everybody else had gone on home, after him and Big Mama had already had that shouting match out front about the money he owed her, about her cutting him outta the register count cause she didn't trust him with it no more. I was there. I was eleven years old and I'd snuck back to get my jacket outta the storeroom cause Big Mama wouldn't let me leave it, and I was crouched down back there in the dark when he come back in, cussing to hisself, and I watched him kick that fryer basket clean over and knock a rag right up against that burner and stand there a second like he half wanted it to catch, and then he walked on out and left it. I didn't understand what I was looking at right then, not all the way. But I understood it before too long.

You could hear the clock in the kitchen ticking. Didn't nobody breathe right for a second.

The father: You're certain of that. You're standing there telling us you're certain of that.

Me: I'm certain. I ain't never been more certain of one single thing in my whole life. I have laid up nights certain of it.

I could see the cousin's face going tight and her back straightening up like a fence post, and I knew exactly what was fixin' to come outta her mouth, cause I'd have said the same thing in her place.

The cousin: Then why in God's name did you sit on it while my daddy...

Me: I know. I know what you're fixin' to ask me and you got every right to ask it. Why'd I let him get treated like that for thirty years when I knew different. I ain't got a good answer, only a true one. I was scared. I was scared of what it'd do to this family to bust that anger back open once it'd finally gone quiet, cause it took years for this family to even get to where we could all set in the same room again after that fire. And I was scared for him too, if I'm honest, cause the oldest uncle wasn't in a good way back then, he was drinking hard and Big Mama knew it and I think part of why she never told it herself was she was scared of what it'd do to him if it come out. And I reckon some of it was just plain not wanting to be the one that broke this family worse than it was already broke. I told myself I was protecting everybody. Mostly I was just scared.

I looked over at the middle uncle now and he had his hand up over his eyes.

Me: There was a time, at Big Mama's funeral, standing right there at the graveside, when you made some little joke about how at least you wasn't the one that burned her whole life's work down, laughing at yourself so's nobody else would get the chance to, and I like to told it right then. Had it right up in my throat. And I looked at your face and I lost my nerve and I swallowed it back down, and I have regretted that more than about anything.

The middle uncle took his hand down off his eyes and I watched about six things cross his face at once. Relief was in there, plain as day, the kind that makes a grown man's shoulders drop two inches. But right up under it come a fresh kind of mad, madder in some ways than the old kind, cause the old kind at least had somewhere to land. This was the kind of mad that comes from knowing you can't get thirty years back no matter who apologizes.

The middle uncle: Thirty years. Thirty years I have had folks look at me sideways at every family thing we ever had. Thirty years my own children grew up hearing what their daddy done, and it wasn't never done by me at all.

Me: I know it. I am sorry for every one of them years, and I ain't saying that to make it lighter on myself, cause it ain't gonna be light on me and it oughtn't to be. I owed you the truth a long time ago and I flat did not pay it, and that is on me, not you, not nobody else in this room.

I looked to the oldest uncle then, and everybody else did too, all at once, like the room turned on a hinge.

He didn't say nothing for a good while. He set there in that recliner with his hands still folded, only now they were shaking some, and his eyes had gone down to the floor and stayed there. When he did talk his voice come out about half the size it usually was.

The oldest uncle: I have known what I done every day of my life. I did not know how to say it and watch what it'd do. I let another man wear it cause it was easier than owning it, and I am not gonna set here and tell y'all I got a reason that makes that right, cause there ain't one.

And that's when the old anger, the same anger that had gone and gone quiet in this family for thirty years, come back up fresh, only this time it had somewhere true to land, and it landed on him, and it was ugly for a minute, everybody talking at once, the father up outta his seat, the cousin crying mad tears, the mother saying his name over and over like she couldn't believe it even though she'd just heard it plain.

It ain't fixed. I want to say that plain too, same as I said everything else tonight. Ain't nothing tidy about it, ain't no bow on top. The middle uncle's still got thirty years he don't get back, and the oldest uncle's got to live the rest of his life as the man who let it happen and let somebody else carry it. But this family is standing on the truth now instead of standing around it, and after thirty years of the other way, I will take that.
