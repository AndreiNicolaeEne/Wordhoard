Y'all get on in here and quit showing out and sit yourselves down, 'cause I ain't fixin to tell this but the one time tonight and then it's off to bed, you hear me. Cut the lights on in here, it's fixin to get dark, and mash that button on the fan while you at it, it's coming up a cloud out yonder and this house gets close. Get you a coke outta the icebox if you thirsty, don't matter what kind, and then set still.

This here's the story of how we come to be right here in Memphis, the way my granny told it to me settin' in that same kitchen, and her mama told it to her before that, and so on back further than anybody rightly remembers now.

We wadn't always here. Before all this, before this street, before this whole side of town was even built up good, our people was down in the Delta, over in Mississippi, tenant farming on land that wadn't never gon' be theirs no matter how many years they put a plow to it. I ain't gon' dwell on it much, 'cause it wadn't nothing but hard, chopping cotton from can't-see to can't-see, owing the man at the store more come fall than they'd made all summer. That's just how it was set up down there, and everybody knowed it, and can't nobody fix that kind of wrong by talking about it now.

What finally pushed 'em off that land was the boll weevil got in the cotton two years runnin', et it down to nothing, and then the river come up over the levee on top of that, like it wadn't bad enough already. Folks say the water stood in the fields near on a month. Wadn't a crop left to speak of, wadn't nothing left to owe on neither, and the man that owned the land, bless his heart, wadn't fixin to carry nobody through a season with no crop in it. So something had to give.

It was my great-great uncle went first, didn't nobody send him, he just up and went on his own, 'cause somebody had to, and he was the one with the nerve for it and the trade in his hands besides. He'd always been the one could fix anything, mend a busted wagon wheel, set a door back on its hinges, whatever needed doing around that place he could do it. When he left he didn't carry much, just what he could tie up, and he carried his own hammer wrapped down in a flour sack 'cause that hammer was his and wadn't nobody taking it off him.

The traveling itself was a hard thing, and don't let nobody tell you different. Walked a right good piece of it, caught a ride where he could, went hungry more than he et, and it was summer heat the whole way, the kind that'll wear a grown man down to nothing. He liketa died out there more than once, my granny always said it just like that, liketa died, and we'd laugh a little even though it wadn't funny, 'cause that's just the phrase that carried it down to us.

He come into Memphis with plumb near nothing. What he had fit in that same flour sack, hammer and all. No people here waiting on him, no place ready, nothing.

The grandmother: He didn't have a roof of his own for the longest while. Stayed up under some kinfolk of kinfolk, folks that had come up a season or two before him and had a little room to spare, and he slept on their floor and was grateful for it and didn't complain, not once, 'cause complaining wadn't gon' put a roof over nobody's head.

Me: Well how long was that, Granny?

The grandmother: Long enough, baby. Yes, long enough. Wadn't no steady work for the longest while. He'd pick up a day here, a day yonder, load a wagon, help a man build a fence, whatever come up, and he'd bring home what little he made and give near all of it over for his keep, and some weeks that wadn't but a few coins and a mess of greens somebody sent home with him.

It was the hammer that finally turned it for him. A man building up around here got to talking to him, seen what he could do with his hands, and put him on steady, and from there he never looked back. He took up carpentry proper after that and it's what he done the rest of his working life, framing houses, hanging doors, whatever needed building, and he was known for it, known as a man whose work you didn't have to go behind and check.

Once he had steady work and a little put by, he commenced to sending for the rest. Wadn't quick about it, though. Took him the better part of two years, saving up what he could out of what wadn't much to start with, before he had enough to bring the rest of 'em up, his wife, his mama an' them, the young'uns and all. Two years is a long stretch to be split up like that, not knowing day to day how the other was faring, just letters when somebody could write one and somebody else could carry it up the road.

And that's where it gets to the part don't nobody talk on much. There was a cousin come along on that second trip, bringing the rest of the family up, that didn't make it. Took sick on the road and didn't recover, and they had to bury that child along the way, in a place none of us ever went back to see. My great-aunt was the only one ever spoke of it at all, and even she wouldn't give up a name.

The great-aunt: That's not a story I tell for the telling of it. That child's between us and the Lord, and that's where it's gon' stay. I'll say this much and no more, we lost one on that road, and it liketa broke your great-great-grandmother, and if you push me on it any further I'll get madder than a wet hen, so don't.

And that was that. Didn't nobody push her on it, 'cause you don't push kinfolk on a thing like that, and don't you push me on it neither.

When the family did finally get here, together at last, they didn't have much more than what they'd carried, but one thing come with 'em out of that old place besides the hammer, and it's still in this family right now, a quilt my great-great-grandmother pieced together herself out of whatever scraps of cloth wadn't good for nothing else, old work shirts and flour sacks and the like. She used to could piece a whole quilt top in under a week, or so the story goes, blind near, just by feel. She carried that one rolled up under her arm the whole way up. It's thin as paper now, patched over patches till the pattern runs plumb cattywampus, but it's kept, and it'll stay kept, God willing and the creek don't rise, long after I'm gone from here too.

They found 'em a little place to live within them first few years, over close to where the work was, nothing fancy, just four rooms and a porch, but it was theirs to rent regular instead of borrowed off somebody, and that made all the difference in the world to people who'd been beholden to somebody or other their whole lives up to then.

And it was right there, in that same little house, that the children started coming, my great-grandmother among 'em. That's the part that finally made it home, if you want to know the truth of it. A place ain't home just 'cause you're staying there. It's home once you got young'uns being born in it, once there's a whole generation that don't know no other place to call the old place, that never knowed Mississippi at all except in the telling.

So that's how we come to be right here, and that's how it was told to me, settin' right where y'all are sitting now, with the sweet tea sweating on the table and supper not long done and somebody's mama an' them fixin to walk in the door directly. My granny might could tell it better than me, she had a way with it I ain't never had, but she's gone on now, so it falls to me to carry it, close as I can. I still get my groceries near about the same place she did, push a buggy up and down them same old aisles at the Piggly Wiggly her own young'uns used to run around in, and I think on all this near every time I'm in there.

I know y'all fixin to pitch a fit here directly about going to bed, but this story's done now, so don't be ugly about it. Tell it straight when it comes your turn, hard parts and all, and give the ones that carried you here their due. Now gimme some sugar and get on to bed, all y'all, it's plumb late.
