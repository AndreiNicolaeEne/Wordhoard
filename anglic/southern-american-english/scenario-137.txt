The storm had come up out of nowhere the night before, sky turned the color of a bruise, everybody saying it was coming up a cloud sure as anything, and it took the power clean out till near about four in the morning 'fore the lights ever got cut back on. Come noon the next day the uncle went out to check the deep freezer for the fish fry and found the generator cut off and that whole mess of catfish mama an' them had pushed two buggies full of home from the Piggly Wiggly laid out soft and grey, ruint plumb through. He come round the shed, door hanging cattywampus off its hinge since the wind got it, and he was right hot already, madder than a wet hen, and he pointed straight at the sister-in-law standing there with all the kinfolk drinking their cokes.

The uncle: You the one was s'posed to be watching that generator last night. You let it cut off and now all that fish is spoilt.

The sister-in-law: Wasn't me.

The uncle: I done told you, ain't nobody else was back there but you. You might could just own up to it.

The sister-in-law: I swear before God I didn't touch that generator.

She didn't say it again. Stood there with her arms folded, face set plain and even, not giving him one bit of what he was fixin' to get out of her. Off yonder past the shed the young'uns that had been showing out all evening, mashing that little red button on the generator like it was a toy, didn't say a word, just studied something in the grass like it was the most interesting thing they'd ever seen.

The mother, off by the ice chest, said low, don't be ugly now, and he hushed some but didn't take a word of it back. Directly the sister-in-law picked her purse up off the tailgate and walked on off toward the house. I reckon didn't nobody call her back, and didn't nobody say sorry, and somebody said they'd still do the fry Sunday, God willing and the creek don't rise, like that settled anything. That denial just sat there in the yard same as she'd left it.
