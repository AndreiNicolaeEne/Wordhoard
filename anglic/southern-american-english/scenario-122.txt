The young one: Tell me the one about the thing that calls out after dark, the way you always tell it.

Me: Baby, that one comes up the same way it's always come up, near about every summer evening when the porch light draws the moths and somebody young gets to askin' why mama an' them still won't let the young'uns walk the bottom road past dark. It ain't a story I tell for the thrill of it. It's a story I tell because kinfolk before me told it to me the same way, and I reckon it's mine to pass on plain, whether you believe a lick of it or not.

Now here's the thing about it, and I want you to hear this part clear: can't nobody rightly tell you what it looks like, on account of don't nobody claim to have seen it and lived to draw the picture for you. What you're told is what you'll feel before you see one blessed thing, a kind of weight settlin' in on you, like the air's gone thick and plumb wrong. And then, before it's got any shape to it at all, it's got a voice. That's the part folks agree on every time, no matter who's doing the telling. Voice before form. Always has been.

And what it does with that voice is the ugly part of it. It don't growl, it don't moan like you'd expect a haint to do. It calls out to you soundin' exactly, and I mean exactly, like somebody you love. Your mama. Your granddaddy. Whoever it is you'd come runnin' for without stoppin' to think twice. It's fixin' to draw you off the road and away from anybody who could hep you, and that's the whole of its business, drawin' you off to where you're by yourself.

It only comes after midnight, mind you, and only on a night with no moon at all showin'. Cloudy night, new-moon night, don't matter which, long as there ain't a lick of light to see by. Comes up on a clear moonlit night, you won't hear a thing. That part I have never once heard anybody argue with.

Round here, the place it's tied to is the bend down at the bottom, where the bayou road curves back on itself right before the old trestle. I ain't sayin' it's anywhere but there. I ain't sayin' it walks the whole county or the whole state or nothin' like that. It's that bend, and near about that bend only, and that's plenty far enough for anybody with sense. Right quiet down there even in the daytime, if you want to know the truth of it.

I heard it myself the first time when I wasn't but seven or eight, settin' at my granddaddy's feet on a hot night with the fan goin' and the lightnin' bugs comin' up out the grass. He told it plain as you please, no smile on him at all, tellin' it the way you'd tell a body how to can tomatoes or set a fence post, just a fact of the place same as the heat. It was my grandmother told it different. She'd laugh when she got to the callin' part and say, bless your heart, don't you go frettin' over an old tale, and pour you some more sweet tea right quick, but I noticed even as a young'un that laugh of hers never did make it all the way up to her eyes.

First proof anybody ever hands you, and it's the one you can go look at your own self to this day, is that patch of ground right there at the bend. Ain't but a little spot, maybe big as a wash pot laid on its side, and nothin' has ever grown on it. Not weeds, not Johnson grass, not one single thing, and Lord knows Johnson grass will grow on a rock if you give it half a chance. Folks have tried plantin' it out of spite more than once. Directly it just goes bare again.

Second proof ain't nothin' you can walk out and look at, it's just what gets told, the way these things always get told, secondhand, from somebody who heard it from somebody else who heard it further back than that. Word goes round that a trucker, or a fella runnin' the river at night, or somebody's cousin's husband, pulled over at that bend one dark night to check a tire and heard his own child's name called out clear as a bell from off yonder in the trees, and his child was home asleep in the bed the whole while. Can't nobody put a face to who that was no more. It's just what gets said.

But the one I'm fixin' to tell you now, the one told in full every single time, is what happened to my own kin, years back, before you was even born. It was my auntie, my mama's baby sister, walkin' home from her workmate's house late, cuttin' through by the bend on account of it savin' her near a mile. No moon that night, she always said, black as the inside of a boot. And she heard her name, her own name, called out from off yonder in a voice she'd have known anywhere, sweet and familiar and dear to her, and she'd gone three, maybe four steps toward it, off the road and into the dark, before somethin' stopped her cold and turned her right back around.

To this day she swears it the same way every time she tells it, word for word, and the family says it right along with her now we've all heard it so many times: "It called me sweet as anything, and I liketa answered it, and then I thought, that voice is comin' from where wasn't nobody standin'." That's what stopped her. Not fear exactly, just that one thought, plain and cold, right in the middle of steppin' toward it.

That's the detail that'll make even the biggest doubter in the room go right quiet for a second, that thought of hers, comin' from where wasn't nobody standin'. There's just somethin' about it sets a person back on their heels. But I'll give the doubters their openin' too, honest, on account of I ain't never minded bein' honest about it: my auntie also swears the voice called her by her whole given name, first and middle both, and won't a soul in this family, livin' or otherwise, ever call anybody by their whole given name unless they're fixin' to fuss at 'em. That part always gets a laugh, and I reckon it's earned one.

Now I'll tell you plain, I done heard this story told more ways than I could rightly count, and it changes some dependin' on whose mouth it's comin' out of. My granddaddy told it hard and flat, no room in it for doubt. My grandmother told it soft, with that laugh that didn't reach her eyes, like she was half hopin' you'd laugh along and forget it. My auntie tells her own part of it different most every time too, sometimes it was three steps, sometimes four, sometimes she says she smelled somethin' like a cloud comin' up before it ever started callin'. I might could tell it my own way too, and I expect I have, but I have never once minded the difference. A story kept exactly the same word for word every time is a story somebody's stopped believin' in enough to still be shapin'.

But here's what's true regardless whose version you got. Round here, folks still won't answer if their name gets called from off in the dark, not till they've laid their own two eyes on who's doin' the callin'. Don't matter if it sounds just like mama, just like your own workmate, just like whoever you'd trust most in this world. You hold, and you look, and then you answer.

And I'll be honest with you, since you asked me plain and you listened the whole way through without breakin' in once, which I do appreciate. I can't say I believe it, not all the way, not in the full-grown part of my mind that knows better. But I have walked past that bend more nights than I could ever count in my whole life, and I have never one time, not once, answered a call from the dark without checkin' first. Not one time. And I don't reckon I ever will.
