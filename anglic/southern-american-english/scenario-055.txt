Girl, since you missed the whole thing I reckon I better tell it right, start to finish, because it was a day and a half packed into one Saturday.

We had it planned down to the minute. Up at six, truck loaded by nine, first load at the new place by ten, and supper in our own kitchen that night. Well. The Lord had other plans for us, God willing and the creek don't rise, because none of that came off like it was drawn up.

Didn't nobody wake up on time to start with. My husband had set two alarms and I still laid there half asleep thinking I was fixin' to get up in one more minute, and that one minute turned into an hour and some change, and by the time we drug ourselves up it was near about eight-thirty and the truck wasn't even hooked up yet. Then, I mean before we were even out the good Lord's door, the eldest went to shove the screen door open with a stack of boxes in her arms and the whole thing come off its top hinge and like to knocked her flat on the porch. Boxes everywhere, and her hollering.

The eldest: I didn't even touch it hard, it just fell off in my hands!

My husband: That hinge has been cattywampus for two years, I told you that.

So we're not five minutes into the day and already patching a door with a butter knife for a screwdriver because nobody could find the real one, which, if you want to know, was packed. In a box. Marked "kitchen misc," which tells you nothing.

Now here's a blessing in the middle of it. We hadn't called a soul, hadn't asked anybody for help because everybody's got their own life, and here comes the neighbor from two doors down, walking up the driveway with his own dolly, hadn't even had his coffee good yet.

The neighbor: Y'all look like you could use another set of hands.

Bless him, he stayed near about all day. Didn't ask for nothing, just showed up and started grabbing boxes like it was his own move. That man is a saint, I don't care what anybody says.

Now along about eleven, mama and them come by to see how it was going, and that's when it happened. My brother-in-law was carrying mama's crystal punch bowl, the one that's been in the family since before I was born, wrapped up in one bath towel because we run out of paper, and he set it down on the tailgate to shift his grip and it rolled. Just rolled right off and hit the driveway and went to pieces.

The mother: LORD HAVE MERCY, that was my mother's bowl!

Everybody just froze. My brother-in-law's face went white as a sheet.

The brother-in-law: Ma'am, I am so sorry, it just rolled, I swear before God I set it down easy...

The mother: I know it, I know it, it ain't your fault, it's just...

And she didn't finish, just stood there looking at them pieces in the driveway a good minute before she said bless your heart, honey, it's just a bowl, but we all knew good and well it wasn't just a bowl to her. We swept it up and that was that. You can't unbreak crystal no matter how bad you want to.

Somewhere in amongst all that a box went missing and I still don't know to this day where it got off to. It had my winter coats in it and a good bit of the young'uns' school pictures, the framed ones off the hallway wall. We looked in that truck, looked in both houses, looked in the neighbor's truck bed, looked everywhere a body could think to look, and it never did turn up. Just gone. I reckon it's setting in somebody's garage right now and they don't even know whose it is.

Then come the china cabinet, and Lord, that thing about ended the marriage. It was the single heaviest piece we owned, must have weighed four hundred pound with the glass still in it, and my husband and the brother-in-law could not agree on how to carry it to save their life.

My husband: You got to keep your end down, it's back heavy.

The brother-in-law: It ain't back heavy, it's even, you just don't know how to lift.

My husband: I have moved more furniture than you have had hot suppers, don't tell me how to lift.

They liketa dropped it twice arguing about it, and I finally hollered don't be ugly, both of y'all, just walk it slow and hush. We got it to the new house fine, but then that cabinet would not go through that stairwell door for love nor money. Turned it sideways, turned it up on end, tipped it back, tipped it forward, took the door off the hinges same as the screen door that morning, and it still would not clear that turn in the stairs. We must have stood in that stairwell a solid forty-five minutes, four grown people sweating and cussing under our breath, before somebody thought to take the legs off it, and it slid through like butter after that. Should have thought of it an hour before.

About the time we got that settled the sky commenced to coming up a cloud, dark clean across from the west, and my mama said we better mash it because it was fixin' to open up on us. Sure enough, we got about two-thirds of a load covered and the bottom fell out. Rain coming down in sheets, everybody running for tarps, the young'uns squealing and getting soaked anyway trying to help. We lost a good hour just standing under the carport waiting on it to let up.

By one o'clock, one-thirty maybe, everybody was plumb worn slap out and hadn't a one of us eaten a bite. That's when the neighbor's wife come walking up with a cooler, didn't even ask, just set up a spread on the tailgate. Fried chicken, a mess of greens, cornbread, and about a gallon of sweet tea in a jug sweating ice down the side of it. We didn't even sit down, just stood right there in the driveway among all them boxes eating off paper plates balanced on our knees, and I am telling you that was the best meal I believe I ever ate in my life. It saved the whole afternoon. Gave everybody enough in the tank to finish.

The mother: Y'all better eat, ain't no telling when you'll get another chance.

The neighbor's wife: Coke's in the cooler too if anybody wants one.

We ate, we drank about a whole jug of tea between us, and we got back at it.

Now the last three or four loads, I do not know where anybody found the strength for it. My arms felt like they belonged to somebody else. The eldest was dragging so bad by five o'clock she liketa fell asleep standing up leaning on the truck. My husband didn't say two words the last hour, just kept moving, one foot in front of the other, and I reckon that's how you know it's bad, when a talking man goes quiet.

Before we locked up for good I walked back through the old place one more time by myself. Every room stripped bare, nothing left but them marks on the carpet where the furniture used to set and them little nail holes in the wall where the pictures hung. Our voices echoed different in there with nothing to soak up the sound. I stood in what used to be the young'uns' room a minute and just looked at it, and I won't lie to you, I got a little something in my eye that had no business being there. Then I cut the lights off, all through the house, one room at a time, and locked the door behind me for the last time.

We didn't pull up to the new place till well after dark, headlights the only light we had to unload the last of it by. Mama and them had done gone on home, the neighbor and his wife too, after we like to loved them to death and made them promise to come get some sugar and let us feed them proper once we got settled. It was just us and the young'uns hauling them last few boxes in by flashlight, and I stood in that kitchen looking around at half our stuff still setting in the truck because we run plumb out of daylight and out of legs both.

So that's the day. Started late, went sideways before we cleared the front door, and ended with a lot less than what we started out with that morning, a bowl, a box, near about all our strength, and I reckon a little bit of that old house too. But we made it in, and ain't that about how it always goes.
