Y'all want to hear the whole of it, I reckon I better start at the front, because it's a long tale and it didn't go the way we drawed it up on the back of a receipt at the kitchen table that first night.

We'd been living in that little house going on nine years, and it fit us just fine back when it was just me and my husband and the one baby. But then the second one come, and then my husband's mama took to living with us after her hip give out, and directly the house wasn't fitting nobody. Four of us and mama an' them's dog all packed into two bedrooms and one bathroom, and the young'uns fighting over who slept where, and mama plumb done with climbing over boxes in the hallway just to get to the kitchen. I told my husband, I said we cannot keep doing this, and he agreed, and that's how the whole mess got started.

We sat down and figured we were fixin' to need about four months and maybe three men helping, my husband and his brother and whoever else we could talk into it on a Saturday. Lord, we were wrong about that. Four months turned into eleven, and three men turned into six different folks coming through that yard over the better part of a year, some for a weekend, some near every week for months.

First thing that happened, before we hardly got the old back porch tore off, was the wrong turn. My husband and his brother-in-law had it in their heads they could set the new footings without stringing a proper line first, just eyeball it, and I told them, I said y'all are fixin' to regret that, and sure enough two weeks in we come to find the whole back wall was cattywampus, off by near four inches over the run of it. Wasn't nothing to do but tear it up and start again, and that cost us near two full weeks of work we'd already put in the ground, no way around it.

Mother-in-law: I told you boys to measure twice.

My husband: Yes ma'am, we know that now.

After that we went slow and we went right, and along about the middle of the second month, on a Wednesday when it was just me and my husband out there, a truck pulled up we weren't expecting and it was two of the men from church, and they didn't ask could they help, they just come on around back and started unloading their own tools out the bed like they'd been invited. One of em hollered out, said we heard y'all needed some backs out here, and that was that, they worked clean through till supper and wouldn't take a dime for it, just some sweet tea and a plate.

Neighbor from church: Y'all don't worry about paying us nothing, just cut the lights on when it gets dark and we'll keep going.

Right about the time we got the walls framed up on one side, the weather turned on us something awful. Coming up a cloud one afternoon and it didn't let up hardly at all for the better part of a week, rain near every day, the ground turned to soup, and there wasn't a thing to be done but wait it out and watch the tarps we'd thrown over everything to keep it from ruining. My husband like to lost his mind sitting in that house doing nothing while the days ticked off the calendar.

Somewhere in there too we run plumb out of the lumber we'd figured for, ran short about two-thirds through framing the second bedroom, and the supply yard was backed up three weeks on the order. So we made do, used what boards we had left over from the old porch tear-down and some my husband's daddy had stacked up in his shed for I don't know how many years, wasn't a perfect match but it held, and you cannot tell the difference now unless you know right where to look.

Then come the fall. My husband was up on the ladder setting a header, must have been ten, eleven foot up, and his foot slipped off the rung and he come down hard, landed half on the scaffold boards and half on the ground, and my heart liketa stopped right there in my chest. He laid there a second not moving and I hollered his name and thought sure we were headed to the hospital, but he sat up cussing and holding his shoulder and it turned out he wasn't hurt nothing worse than bruised up plumb black and blue and scared white as a sheet. Scared the rest of us worse than it hurt him, I believe.

Me: You liketa gave me a heart attack laying there like that.

My husband: I'm fine, I'm fine, quit hollering.

Me: Don't you tell me to quit hollering, you liketa killed yourself.

There was a good argument too, over the roof line on the addition, whether to tie it into the old roof or run it separate with its own pitch. My husband wanted it tied in clean, and his brother swore up and down it ought to run separate or we'd have water problems inside two years. They went back and forth on it two whole evenings, both of em madder than a wet hen, neither one budging.

Brother: You do it your way, don't come crying to me when it leaks.

My husband: It ain't gonn leak. I know what I'm doing.

They settled it finally by calling a man who does roofing for a living and paying him for twenty minutes of his time to look at it, and he sided with the brother, and my husband didn't love hearing it but he took the fix.

Worst of it all, though, was the side wall in the addition. We'd had it up two months, sided and near ready for windows, when the same roofer come back through for something else and pointed out the whole thing was out of plumb, bowed near an inch over eight foot, on account of green lumber that had twisted up on us after the fact. Wasn't a thing to argue about that time. We had to tear that wall down to the studs and build it again from scratch, materials and all, right when we thought we were nearly done with that side of the house.

But I'll tell you, the day the frame finally stood up whole, all of it, front to back, and we walked through where the rooms were gonna be without nothing but sky over our heads yet, that was a good day. Mama an' them come by with a mess of greens and some fried chicken and we ate off the tailgate of the truck right there in the yard, and my husband's mama cried a little, and I did too if I'm being honest, because it was the first day it looked like a house and not just a wound in the yard.

Last stretch of it, the final four nights before we finished, we worked on past dark every one of em, running extension cords out to work lights, mashing the buttons on them battery drills till the batteries wouldn't hold a charge no more, just trying to get doors hung and trim up and the last of the paint on before we lost our minds over it. My husband's brother-in-law come every one of them nights even though he'd worked his own job all day, and I don't guess I ever thanked him proper for that, so I reckon I'm thanking him now.

And then it was done. Eleven months to the day near enough, six different folks who'd put hands to it one time or another, counting my husband and me and his brother and the brother-in-law and the two men from church, and God willing and the creek don't rise we won't ever have to do it again, though I expect we will, houses being what they are.

Standing in it now, in them extra rooms, mama with a place of her own to shut the door on, the young'uns not fighting near as much as they used to, I get to thinking on all of it, the wall we tore down twice, the rain, the fall that scared us all half to death, and it don't feel like a house so much as it feels like everybody who helped put it up is still a little bit in the walls of it. Bless my husband's heart, he liketa worked himself to death over it, but he'd tell you the same as me: it was worth every bit of it.
