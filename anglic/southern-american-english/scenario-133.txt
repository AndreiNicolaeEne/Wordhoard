Alright, let me tell y'all about this garden this year, cause it was a whole year, I mean a whole entire year of ups and downs and I ain't even playing.

Come late February I'm already studying the seed catalogs at the kitchen table, mama an' them calling me crazy for even thinking about it that early, but I had my mind set. I do a plot down at the community garden, been doing it going on six years now, and every year round about then I sit down and figure what's worth putting in the ground and what ain't worth the trouble. This year I settled on tomatoes, two kinds of squash, okra cause you basically can't kill okra in Memphis if you tried, some greens, purple hull peas, and a little corner of sweet corn just cause the young'uns like to see it grow tall. I ordered my seed, started some of it indoor under a light I rigged up, and then I waited on the weather like everybody does.

We had one more little cold snap in early April that had everybody nervous, but soon as that passed I gave it about two more weeks just to be safe, my granddaddy always said don't trust April till it's damn near over, and then I put everything in the ground. And I swear, the Lord blessed that stretch of weather right after, warm in the day, cool enough at night, rain about every third day like clockwork. Everything come up strong, the squash vines was just running, the tomato plants doubled in about ten days, I ain't never had a start like that before. I was out there every evening after work just standing there grinning at it like a fool.

Didn't take long for the trouble to find it though. First thing that showed up was these squash bugs, all over the underside of the leaves, little bronze-colored things laying eggs in them neat little rows, and I knew right off what I was dealing with cause my auntie used to fight the same thing every summer. I tried the soap spray first, dish soap and water in a bottle, sprayed it on directly I seen the eggs, and it helped some, it really did knock a good bit of them back, but it wasn't no cure. They kept coming back every week or so and I was out there hand-picking bugs off leaves like it was my second job. Never did get shed of them all the way, just kept them down enough the squash could still produce.

Then along about the middle of summer it just quit raining. Plumb quit. Three, four weeks with hardly a drop, and the ground got hard as a brick. I was hauling water by hand every single day after work, jugs and buckets from the spigot down at the end of the row, back and forth, back and forth, my arms was sore for a solid month. You do that every day for weeks you start to understand real quick what farming used to cost people, I ain't even gonna lie.

And that's when the mess with the water started. There's a fellow got the plot right next to mine, been there about as long as I have, and there's really only the one good spigot close by, so we sort of took turns, unspoken like, whoever got there first in the morning. Well one week he started coming earlier and earlier, and I mean he'd run that hose a good forty minutes some mornings, and by the time I got there of an evening wasn't hardly no pressure left and my stuff was wilting bad. I let it go a few days but I wasn't fixin' to just keep letting my garden die of thirst being polite about it.

So I caught him out there one evening and I said my piece.

Me: I ain't trying to start nothing, but you been running that water near on a hour every morning and by evening I got nothing left for mine. We got to work something out.

The neighbor: I didn't even realize I was running it that long, I'm just trying to keep my peppers alive out here same as you.

Me: I hear you, I do, but it's got to where I'm coming out here and my tomatoes look like they fixin' to give up on me.

The neighbor: Alright, alright, don't be ugly about it now. Tell you what, I'll take mornings before seven, you take evenings after six, and Sundays we just split it fifty-fifty, that fair?

Me: That's fair. I appreciate you hearing me on it.

And that settled it clean, we ain't had a cross word about it since, matter of fact he give me some peppers off his own plot later on just to show there wasn't no hard feelings, and I gave him some sugar back, told him bring his kinfolk by anytime they want tomatoes.

Now the tomatoes, Lord, the tomatoes was the star of the whole show this year. I mean a clear win, no argument about it. I had more tomatoes than I knew what to do with, canning them, giving them away, eating them for supper about every night with just a little salt, my neighbors down the row was probably tired of seeing me walk up with another bag. That right there was the best crop I done had in the six years I been out there.

But while the tomatoes was showing out, my squash got to rotting on me. Blossom end rot first, and then just general soft spots where they'd lay on the wet ground, on top of them bugs still working on it too. I lost a good third of what I was expecting off them vines. Tried to rotate what was touching the dirt, put some straw down under them, helped a little but it was late in coming.

Then right in the thick of all that, middle of August, we had ourselves a storm blow through. Sky went that color like coming up a cloud all afternoon, wind picking up, and by the time it hit it laid my corn just about flat, whole rows of it cattywampus, leaning every which way, and knocked my pea trellis clean over too. I stood out there the next morning just about ready to pitch a fit looking at it, but wasn't nothing to do but get to work. Spent that whole Saturday staking corn back up one stalk at a time, tying it with twine, propping the trellis back and re-stringing it. Lost some to the storm for good, snapped clean at the base, but I got most of it standing again and it went on and finished out the season.

Come the fall I finally sat down and tallied it all up, and honestly it come out better than I figured it would after a year like that. Tomatoes way ahead, more greens than I could eat, a real good haul of peas, okra just kept giving like it always does, corn came back some after the storm, squash was the disappointment but it wasn't a total loss. I traded off a good bit of my extra tomatoes and some greens to a fellow two plots down who raises chickens, traded straight up for eggs all through the fall, worked out good for both of us.

Then I put the plot to bed, pulled the dead vines, turned the soil, put down some cover crop and a layer of leaves over the top to let it rest till spring.

So looking back on the whole year of it, I reckon I'd call it a good year, not a perfect one. Bugs I never did whip, a storm that liketa took my corn out, water trouble that could've turned real ugly between neighbors but didn't, thanks to us talking it out like grown folks. But that tomato harvest alone was worth every bit of hauling water I did, and God willing and the creek don't rise, I'll be back out there come next spring doing it all over again.
