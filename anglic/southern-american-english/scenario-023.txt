Okay my friend, sit down somewhere, cause I am fixin' to tell you every single bit of this, start to finish, since you couldn't be there and I know that's been eatin' at you. I done told half of it to my mama already but you get the whole thing.

I woke up at like four-thirty in the mornin', wide open, heart just goin', and I laid there tryin' to talk myself back to sleep and it wasn't happenin', not one bit. Finally around six I just got up and made coffee and sat at the counter and I could not eat a thing, my friend, not a thing. My mother kept mashin' toast in front of me like that was gonna do somethin' and I got maybe three bites down the whole mornin'. She fussed at me, said "you gotta eat somethin' or you gonna be swayin' at that altar," and I told her I'd eat at supper, don't you worry about me.

Then bout forty-five minutes before we had to be out the door I like to had a stroke cause I could not find the garter nowhere. I mean I turned that whole house cattywampus lookin' for it, drawers hangin' open, my little cousin diggin' under the bed hollerin' she found it and it wasn't it, it was a sock. My aunt finally found it in the pocket of the dress bag where I'd stuck it my own self a week before like a fool. Lord. I liketa died right there in the hallway in my slip hollerin' for everybody to hush so I could think.

Then we get to the church and this is the part I really wanted you to hear, both families, all together, first time ever in one room. His mama an' them come in from over east Tennessee and my whole side, all my kinfolk, packed in there, and I'm gonna tell you it coulda gone either way but it went good, it went real good. His grandmother took my mother's hand right off and said,

The groom's grandmother: "Well ain't you just as pretty as he said, come here and gimme some sugar,"

and pulled her right in, and my mother, my mother who does not cry, ever, not at nothin', she cried first, right then, before I'd even come down the aisle. I heard about it after cause I wasn't out there yet, but my aunt said it set off half the room like dominoes.

When I did come down the aisle he was already cryin', which nobody warned me about, and that about did me in too.

Now here's the part that still makes me laugh. I get up there and go to say my vows and I flat lost my place. I had 'em memorized, I swear I did, and I get to the line about better or worse and my mind just went plumb blank, empty as a Piggly Wiggly buggy. I stood there and everybody's just waitin' and waitin' and I finally just looked at him and said,

Me: "Hang on, hang on, I had this,"

and he grinned so big and just waited on me, didn't rush me one bit, and it come back to me and I got through it and the whole church about lost it laughin' with me, not at me, with me, and honestly that's my favorite part of the whole day now that I think back on it.

Then come the rings, and that's it, that's the moment, you say the words and you put it on and somethin' just clicks over in you, I can't explain it no better than that, it's just done, sealed, ain't no undoin' that.

After that we had to go sign all the papers, and they had my daddy stand up as witness for my side and his uncle stood up for his, both of 'em signin' their name lookin' so serious like they were signin' somethin' at the bank.

Then came the photographs, Lord have mercy, the photographs. That poor photographer spent I bet a solid hour and a half tryin' to arrange kinfolk into little clusters, "aunts and uncles over here, now just the cousins, now step back, ma'am, just a little, no ma'am the other way," and half my family was hot and thirsty and somebody had done brought a cooler of Cokes out to the parkin' lot just so people wouldn't pitch a fit waitin' around in the sun.

At the reception there was a seatin' mix-up that I am still hearin' about, they put my husband's aunt right next to my great-uncle who cannot stand her since some fallin' out twenty years back nobody even remembers the start of, and the two of 'em sat there stiff as boards the whole meal not sayin' two words to each other. My mother was madder than a wet hen when she found out the seatin' chart got switched on her.

Food-wise, the mess of greens my aunt brought was gone in about ten minutes flat, people were fightin' over that pot, and then there was this fancy little dish the caterer put out, some kind of chilled soup thing, and I don't think one single soul touched it all night. Just sat there. Bless its heart.

The toast, my husband's brother stood up to give it and could not get through it, kept stoppin', wipin' his face, startin' back over, and by the end half the room was cryin' right along with him, includin' me, and I had already cried plumb out that mornin' so I didn't think I had any left, but I did.

My little cousin, the one who found the sock earlier, would not sit down the entire night. Every single song, up dancin', by herself if she had to be, showin' out somethin' fierce, and bless her heart she near 'bout wore everybody else out just watchin' her.

And of course somebody had to drag out the story about my granddaddy and the mule and the church revival, you know the one, told it to the whole table like nobody there had heard it forty times, and everybody hollered laughin' like it was brand new anyway.

Right at the end, once the last of everybody had gone on home, it come up cloudy out, lookin' like it might rain on the drive, and it was just me and him standin' there in that hall with cake smashed in the carpet and chairs every whichaway and it got so quiet, my friend, quieter than I expected, just the two of us and all that mess, and we didn't even clean nothin' up, just stood there a minute holdin' onto each other before we finally headed on out.

Anyway. That's the whole day, start to finish, and I wish more than anything you coulda been standin' in it with us. Next time, God willing and the creek don't rise, you better be there.
