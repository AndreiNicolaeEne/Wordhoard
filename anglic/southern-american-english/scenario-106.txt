I don't tell this one much, and when I do I try to tell it just like it was, no bigger and no smaller, on account of stretching it either way feels like lying about something that don't need lying about.

We'd gone down to see mama's people, our kinfolk on the other side, the ones that stayed put after everybody else scattered on up this way. Mama an' them had cooked us up a mess of greens the night before and wanted their sugar off every one of us before we ever got in the car. It wasn't going down that give me any trouble, neither. Going, there's one man in a booth waves you on through, don't hardly slow the car down. It's coming back that's the leg where they want to know is everybody in the car exactly who they say they are, on account of it's a whole lot easier to leave a place than it is to come back into one, and that's why the checkpoint sits right where it sits, same spot it's sat my whole life and mama's before me. Mama's daddy used to say back in his day you used to could roll on through with nothing but a wave, said one time the man liketa waved him on through with a chicken loose in the back seat and never asked him a thing. I reckon it ain't like that no more, and I reckon it ain't going to be again, though mama's people still tease that I might could just cross of a Tuesday and beat the whole line, and maybe I might could, but I never have tried it.

By the time we eased on up toward the front of that line we'd already set there the better part of a half hour, longer than I ever stood waiting behind somebody's buggy at the Piggly Wiggly of a Sunday evening, engine running, windows cracked some 'cause it was hot enough to melt a fence post, and the sky off to the west had commenced coming up a cloud, which didn't do my nerves one bit of good. I had a thermos of sweet tea up front sweating worse than I was. The young'uns had been showing out something awful the whole drive down and kept on asking could they have a coke now, and I done told 'em twice already, don't be ugly and don't you go pitching no fit, not here, we'll get us one directly, just sit still and let mama handle it. I ain't never done a thing in my life worth mentioning, every paper in that car was good and true, and I was still right nervous, plumb nervous if I'm honest, 'cause something about setting in a line of cars watching brake lights and men walking up and down with dogs will get down in your stomach regardless of what you got or ain't got to hide.

There was a fellow two cars up from us, traveling by his own self, and they had him pulled clean over under that little tin roof they got off to the side, out of his car, standing with his hands where everybody could see 'em while two of the officers went on through his trunk. Took a good while. Wasn't a soul in that line said one word about it, not us, not the car ahead, not the car behind, everybody just watching out the side of their eye and not watching, the way you do when you don't want to be the reason anybody remembers your face.

When it come our turn I mashed the button to run my window on down the rest of the way and passed everything on out to him, my license and the young'uns' papers and all.

The one at the booth: Citizenship?

Me: American, sir. All of us.

The one at the booth: Where you all coming from and where you headed?

Me: We been down visiting my mama's people, sir. Headed on back up to Memphis now.

That first one I answered plain and didn't have to study on it a second. The next one he asked took more care to get right. He wanted to know, ma'am, could I show him something saying daddy knew the young'uns was out of the country and was fine with it, seeing as how daddy wasn't riding along with us. I had to think how to say it, so it wouldn't come out sounding like nothing more than what it was, just daddy had to work and couldn't get the time off. I told him yes sir, I had the letter, and then I like to plumb forgot right then where I'd put it, went hunting through the glove box, which was already cattywampus with maps and napkins and I don't know what all, before I found it stuck down behind the insurance folder where I'd tucked it safe and then gone and forgot I'd tucked it.

He took all of it and didn't say nothing for a minute, just looked it over, page by page, slow, and that little bit of quiet felt longer than the whole half hour that come before it. I kept my hands still in my lap and my voice easy when I did have to talk, on account of I did not want him hearing one shake in it, even though inside I was about as settled as a cat on a hot stove, and if that little one had pitched a fit right then I believe I might could have got madder than a wet hen, nerves or not. The one small kindness he showed us, and I think on it yet, is he looked back at the young'uns fixin' to squirm clean out their seatbelts and said, right gentle, bless their hearts, looks like they about done with this line, and let a little smile go along with it, which took some of the starch out of the moment for me even if it wasn't but a second of it.

Then he squared the papers back up together, handed 'em on through the window, and said, y'all have a safe trip on home now, and waved that hand toward the road up yonder past the booths. That wave was the whole of it. That's what let us go on.

I didn't feel a thing let go in me right then, though. I drove on regular as you please, answered the young'uns when they asked was we there yet, kept both hands set on the wheel. It wasn't till we'd gone a good twenty minutes on up the road, flags and booths well behind us and nothing but highway and that same cloud still building off to the west, that I felt my shoulders come down from up around my ears where they'd been setting the whole time without me even knowing it, and my hands got to shaking a little on the wheel where they hadn't shook once back there when it counted. I had to pull over onto the shoulder a minute, not for nothing wrong, just to let it pass on through me, and I sat there thinking, well, I reckon that's done, God willing and the creek don't rise we'll make Memphis in time for supper.

And that's why I tell it careful when I tell it at all. Wasn't nothing happened to us, not really, not the way it goes for some. But it took something out of me anyway, standing there answering plain when plain was called for and careful when careful was called for, keeping my face set and my voice level while a man went through every paper we had to our name, watching that other fellow held back longer than us and knowing it could've gone different for anybody in that line, us included, on any given day. So I don't make it bigger than that, and I won't make it smaller neither. It was a half hour in a line, two questions, one letter I like to forgot, and twenty minutes of driving before I could breathe regular again. That's the size of it, and that's the size I give it.
