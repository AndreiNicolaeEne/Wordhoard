Me: Well since you're asking, and since you're fixin' to be up in the middle of it come daylight anyway, I reckon I better walk you through it now so you ain't standing there plumb lost. It goes the same near about every single morning, like somebody wrote the whole thing down once and everybody in this house just keeps performing it over and over.

Me: My husband's up first, always, and I mean always, before the alarm, before the dog, before God's own rooster if we kept one. Man does not own a snooze button in his soul. He's up in the pitch dark and the first thing he does, every time without fail, is stand out on the back step a minute with his coffee before he ever cuts the kitchen light on, like he's fixin' to case the yard for whatever happened to it overnight, however coming-up-a-cloud or however clear it is out there. Won't nobody bother him in that one minute. Then the kitchen light goes on and the whole rest of it starts up behind him.

Me: I'm up second, and I wake to the exact same grumble every day of my married life, which is him hollering that I have once again left my whole business spread out over that one wash-room counter like I'm fixin' to open a beauty shop in there. And I holler right back that a man who takes twenty-five minutes doing nothing but standing in front of a mirror ain't got one bit of room to talk. We have never once settled it in eleven years and we ain't fixin' to settle it now. It's just the morning's opening number.

Husband: You are in there every blessed morning like you own the whole place.

Me: I do own the place. Half of it's mine, and I might could use your half too if you don't hurry it up.

Me: Then it's the young'uns, and that part runs in the same order every single morning, don't matter what. Eldest gets woke first, on account of she takes the longest to peel out from under them covers, then it's the little one right after, who pops up like toast the second you say her name. I go on down that hall and it is the same three things hollered in the same order, every morning: "Get up." Then a minute later, "Get dressed." Then, not too much after that, "Get MOVING, we ain't got all day." I have not varied that order once in ten years, and I done told you already I ain't fixin' to start.

Me: And directly behind that comes the argument, and it runs close enough to the same words that I could about recite both sides of it myself. It's over that same wash-room counter, funny enough, except now it's the two of them instead of me and my husband.

The eldest: She is in my spot. Tell her to get out my spot.

The youngest: It ain't got your name wrote on it nowhere.

The eldest: Mama, she is showing out again and it is not even seven o'clock in the morning.

Me: And I holler on down the hall, don't be ugly, both of you, and I get back two little "yes ma'ams" like clockwork, and somehow that's the magic words, 'cause they quit, more or less, for about four whole minutes before they're right back at it, one of 'em madder than a wet hen the whole time.

Me: The dog's got her own fixed part in all this too. She lays under the table the entire breakfast through, right at my husband's foot special, on account of he's the one that slips her whatever the young'uns won't eat off their plate, which some mornings is a right good mess of eggs. She don't move for nothing else going on in this house, not the radio, not the arguing, not the door slamming, but the second that skillet hits the stovetop she is up and stationed like she's punched a clock.

Me: Breakfast is the same thing every single morning, eggs and grits and whatever biscuit's left over from supper the night before, on account of we don't hardly ever finish a whole pan of them. And the little one, she has not one time in her whole life varied her order. Dry toast, no butter, cut corner to corner, and if it comes out cut straight across instead she will look at that plate like it has personally wronged her.

Me: Every single morning something goes missing, and it's always one of about three things, a shoe, the good hairbrush, or the car key, and it's hunted for in the same three spots every time: under the couch, in yesterday's coat pocket, or, Lord only knows why, behind the dog's bed, when the whole time it was over yonder on the dryer where it always ends up. Somebody's near about pitching a fit down on their knees, hollering it was RIGHT here.

Me: And you always know the whole house is finally, properly up and moving, 'cause them old pipes commence to hollering the minute the hot water comes up good for the shower. That groan runs clean through the walls. Ain't nobody sleeping through that racket, not even the young'un who could liketa sleep through a house fire.

Me: The eldest is the one running behind, every day, same few minutes, like clockwork, except the bad kind of clockwork. So there's always this last little stretch right at the door, coats snatched off the hooks, backpacks slapped into hands, lunch boxes mashed shut on the run, and somehow, every single day, nothing gets left, only 'cause it never does. We're too practiced at it by now to actually forget a thing. We just make it look cattywampus on the way out the door.

Me: Sunday's the one day the whole rhythm just stops. House goes quiet all of a sudden. Nobody's hollering get moving, my husband ain't out on that step at dark-thirty, the pipes don't holler till whenever they feel like hollering. It's the one morning that don't run on the same gears as all the rest, and I about don't know what to do with myself them mornings, tell you the truth.

Me: But every other day, whoever's headed out that door last hollers back the same four words before it shuts behind them: "Y'all be sweet now." Same words, same door, same everything, every morning, and there's always a gimme-some-sugar quick before anybody gets out of reach, even the big one who acts like she's too old for it now.

Me: And I stand there and watch that whole ridiculous parade go marching off down the walk, same order, same fussing, same everything it was the morning before, and it tickles me plumb to death every time, even now, even having seen it, what, ten thousand mornings and running. Half my own kinfolk don't believe me when I tell it. And here you are about to get the real thing, live, here directly. Bless your heart.
