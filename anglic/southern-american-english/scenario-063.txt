Me: Girl, let me tell you what all went on with that tree, since you weren't around for none of it. It's a whole thing. Sit down a minute.

So it come up a cloud somethin' fierce that Tuesday night, wind just a-whippin', and I remember layin' in bed thinkin' I hope to God that big ol' water oak don't come down on the house. Well it didn't come down on the house. It come down on the fence. I went out the back door next mornin', still in my house shoes, and there it was, laid out flat across the whole line, fence just mashed down to splinters under it, cattywampus every which a-way. Wire poppin' up outta the dirt like it was tryin' to crawl off somewheres.

And here's the thing, that tree wasn't even fully on my side or fully on theirs. It'd been growin' right up along the property line for years and years, roots probably tangled up under both yards same as the branches was tangled up over both roofs. So right off the bat you got a argument just waitin' to happen, 'cause ain't nobody can say for certain sure whose tree that even was.

I let it set a hour or two, got my coffee in me, then I walked around front and knocked on their door. And I want you to know I was tryin' to be nice about it. I wasn't fixin' to start no mess first thing in the mornin'.

Me: Mornin'. Guess you seen the fence.

The neighbor: I seen it. Lord, what a mess. Wasn't nothin' nobody could do about that, though. That's just the wind. Wind'll do what wind wants.

Me: Well, I hear you, but that tree's been leanin' on our side a good long while now. Leaned bad enough my husband liketa broke his neck lookin' up at it every time he mowed under there.

The neighbor: It's a tree. Trees lean.

Me: Not like that one was leanin'. That's a might-fall-any-minute kind of lean, and it did.

And right about then my brother, who was over helpin' me look at the damage, he pipes up.

My brother: Didn't we tell y'all about that thing two summers back? I remember standin' right yonder by the gate tellin' you it needed lookin' at before it come down on somebody.

The neighbor: I don't recall nobody sayin' nothin' like that to me.

My brother: Well I recall it plain as day, and I ain't the only one. Mama an' them was there too.

That kinda set the whole tone for it, if I'm honest. Once it got to who-said-what-and-when, wasn't nobody backin' down. I said the tree was theirs, or leastways theirs enough, and it'd been a known danger, and they ought to pay to put the fence back right. They said it was an act of God, plain and simple, wind's doin' and not a soul's fault, and why should they be out good money over weather. We went back and forth on that a good little while, right there in the yard, both of us gettin' a little hot about it, though nobody was outright ugly, just stiff. Stubborn. I wasn't fixin' to be the one who caved first and pay for the whole thing my own self when it was their tree fell on my fence, and they clearly wasn't fixin' to admit no fault neither. We're talkin' near on two weeks of this, fence just layin' there in pieces, both of us kinda avoidin' each other at the mailbox.

Then the old man from two doors down, the one that's lived on that street since before either one of us bought our houses, he come by on his own, wasn't nobody ask him to. Just come strollin' up one evenin' while we was both out there again goin' round and round about it.

The older neighbor: Now I ain't gettin' into whose fault a windstorm is. That's between y'all and the Lord. But I'll tell you what I know. That tree's roots was under both them yards, which means in the eyes of most folks it belonged to both of y'all whether anybody planted it there apurpose or not. So the way I see it, ain't neither one of you got clean hands on it, and ain't neither one of you got to feel like you lost nothin' by helpin' put it right.

He said the material, the posts and the wire and all, ought to be split down the middle, seein' as the tree was jointly owned whether we liked that word or not. But the labor, the cuttin' and the diggin' and the settin', that was gonna be a two-family job regardless, so nobody was doin' nobody a favor, everybody was just doin' their own fence. Framed it so it wasn't about who was right. Framed it so both of us could walk away without sayin' we was wrong. My neighbor said fine to that pretty quick, and I said fine to that pretty quick too, and honestly it was a relief, 'cause I don't think either one of us actually wanted to keep on like we was.

So the money got split, and then come the real work. First mornin' we was all out there together it was stiff as a board, I won't lie. Everybody sayin' "yes ma'am" and "no sir" real polite and not much else, chainsaw goin', everybody just workin' their own section, quiet. My husband and their boy haulin' the big limbs off to the curb, me and the neighbor's wife tryin' to sort out what fence posts could be saved and what was gone for good. Felt like we was strangers doin' a chore together, not neighbors.

Round about noon, though, the neighbor's wife come out with a big ol' pitcher of sweet tea and cups for everybody, didn't nobody ask her to, just brought it on out to where we was all workin' and started pourin'. And my husband, without even thinkin' on it, handed the neighbor his good posthole digger when he seen theirs had a busted handle, just handed it right over, didn't say a word about it, went back to what he was doin'. Little things like that. But it changed how the whole rest of the day went. Somebody made a joke, somebody laughed, and by the time we broke for supper we was talkin' regular again, talkin' about the young'uns and whatnot, like it hadn't ever been tense.

Took us the better part of that week, workin' evenings and the weekend, but we got that fence back up, and I'll tell you it's a sturdier fence now than the one that come down. We used better posts, set 'em deeper, and that ol' tree's gone now so ain't nothin' fixin' to fall on it again anytime soon, God willing and the creek don't rise.

And things between the two houses, they're better now than they was even before all this happened, if you can believe that. We wave, we bring each other a plate when one of us is cookin' somethin' big, her and me trade tomatoes and greens outta the garden all summer long. Bless her heart, she still brings up that fence sometimes like it was the best thing that coulda happened to us, gettin' forced to work it out together. I reckon she ain't wrong.
