The mother had cut the lights on in the kitchen before the sun was even good and up, coffee not made yet, the notepad and the calculator with the sticky seven laid out square in front of her chair. She'd been pushing the buggy round the Piggly Wiggly all week doing figures in her head instead of minding her list, and now she had it wrote down.

The daughter came in still working one arm loose from her coat sleeve and pulled out the chair across the table.

The daughter: Yes ma'am, what you got.

The mother: Sit on down a minute. I been thinking on it all week and I done made up my mind. We are going to the Smokies this year, you and me, like we always said we would, and we are doing the whole trip on a thousand dollars and not one red cent more.

The daughter: A thousand total. For both of us, everything.

The mother: For both of us, gas to the funnel cakes to the pillow we lay our head on at night. A thousand and that is it. Mash that button and let's get to figuring.

The daughter: Alright then. What's the biggest thing we got to pay for first.

The mother: Getting there. Ain't no way around that one. Your daddy's fixin' to look the car over before we go, and between that and the gas both ways, I reckon on four hundred dollars just to get us up yonder and back home again.

The daughter: Four hundred. Lord, that's near half of it right there before we've even unpacked a bag.

The mother: I know it. But if your daddy don't find nothing wrong under that hood, we might could shave some off that number. He does love showing out under a car, so let him have his fun, and I ain't forgetting what we save. Hold onto that thought.

The daughter: Alright, four hundred for now. How many days we talking.

The mother: That's what I been fixin' to ask you. I was hoping on two weeks, but with a thousand dollars, two weeks is a pipe dream and you know it.

The daughter: It surely is. What about a week.

The mother: A week feels short after all that driving. We might should just settle on something in between. What if we said ten days.

The daughter: Ten days I could live with. Ten days and we still got money left for everything else.

The mother: We're fixin' to find out. Put ten days down and let's keep going.

The daughter: Now mama, you know we ain't going all that way and not stopping at Dollywood. I don't care what it costs, that one is not getting cut off this list.

The mother: I wasn't fixin' to cut it, don't be ugly. Dollywood is staying right where it's at. Tickets run ninety apiece anymore, right expensive if you ask me, so that's a hundred and eighty dollars for the two of us.

The daughter: A hundred and eighty. That's steep.

The mother: It is, and since it's staying, something else has got to give. I liketa fell out of my chair when I seen what them cabins with the hot tub go for. A plain room at the motor lodge keeps the rain off us just the same, so that's what we're downgrading to.

The daughter: Fine by me, I ain't picky about a hot tub.

The mother: Good, cause it ain't in the budget noway. Now, four hundred for the road plus a hundred and eighty for Dollywood, that's five hundred and eighty dollars gone already.

The daughter: Five-eighty. What's the plain room run us then.

The mother: I called around. A hundred dollars for the whole stay if we book it right and don't get choosy about the floor.

The daughter: Five-eighty and a hundred is six-eighty.

The mother: Six hundred and eighty dollars. And hold on, before I forget, if your daddy gets that car squared away cheap and we come in under four hundred on the getting-there part, every dollar of that difference goes straight toward an eleventh day. I ain't giving up on that eleventh day yet.

The daughter: I like that. Alright, what's left to figure.

The mother: Food, and what we do for fun besides Dollywood, and what we set back in case something goes sideways on us, cause something always does.

The daughter: Speaking of fun, I want us to get our picture made. That old-timey place where they dress you up, that's fifty dollars for the both of us and I have wanted that since I was a young'un.

The mother: Fifty dollars for a photograph. Bless your heart, you'd have us broke by Wednesday. We could put that fifty toward the eleventh day instead and just take a picture on the phone for free.

The daughter: Mama, a phone picture is not the same and you know it. I want the real one, the kind that goes on the wall.

The mother: We'll see. Let it set a minute while we do the food.

The daughter: Alright. Food.

The mother: Twenty dollars a day feeds the both of us if we ain't eating out every meal. We'll eat our dinner out the cooler in the middle of the day and save the real money for supper of an evening, sweet tea with it, that's the house wine everywhere down there and it's free with the meal most places anyhow. Maybe a mess of greens if we come across somewhere that sells them.

The daughter: Twenty a day, ten days, that's two hundred dollars. And when we stop, grab me a coke. A Sundrop if they got it, a Mountain Dew if they don't.

The mother: I hear you. Two hundred dollars for food, add that to the six-eighty and we're at eight hundred and eighty.

The daughter: Eight-eighty. What about if the car acts up for real, or it comes up a cloud on us and we got to pull off somewhere for the night, or one of us takes sick.

The mother: That's what the extra's for. I want a hundred dollars set back that we don't touch unless something goes wrong. God willing and the creek don't rise we won't need a dime of it, but I ain't leaving home without it.

The daughter: A hundred dollars just sitting there.

The mother: Just sitting there, and if we don't use it, it comes home with us. Add that in, eight-eighty and a hundred is nine hundred and eighty dollars.

The daughter: Nine-eighty. Now what about my picture.

The mother: Well ain't that a mess. We're at nine hundred and eighty already and we ain't even put your fifty dollars in yet.

The daughter: I know it, but I want it in. I done told you twice now, I have wanted that picture since I was little.

The mother: And don't think I don't know you'll be madder than a wet hen if I cut it, so alright, it's staying. Nine hundred and eighty plus your fifty is a thousand and thirty dollars.

The daughter: A thousand and thirty. We come up thirty short then.

The mother: We surely did, and I said a thousand and not a bit more, so something's got to come down thirty dollars or better.

The daughter: We could cut the room down more.

The mother: Can't get it much cheaper than a hundred dollars for ten nights and still have a roof that don't leak. No, I reckon we trim the money set back for trouble. Bring that hundred down to fifty and pray hard nothing goes cattywampus on us.

The daughter: Mama, that makes me plumb nervous, cutting the trouble money in half.

The mother: Makes me a little nervous too, if I'm honest, but fifty dollars is still fifty dollars more than nothing, and this trip has been a dream of ours a long time. Your granny used to could squeeze a dollar so far it hollered, and I am trying my best to do her proud. Let's do it.

The daughter: Alright. Take fifty off the hundred, trouble money is fifty now instead of a hundred.

The mother: That brings us down to nine hundred and eighty dollars total, and twenty dollars left over to spare.

The daughter: Nine-eighty, twenty to spare. And if daddy gets that car right for cheap, we might yet get that eleventh day out of the getting-there money too.

The mother: Might could. We'll cross that when we get to it directly. Your daddy already asked was y'all going to need the good cooler, so I reckon he's halfway resigned to it. Maybe all y'all can go together next summer, the whole bunch of you, but this year it's just me and you, and we'll bring something back for the kinfolk if there's a nickel left, which there ain't.

The daughter: I done heard you say it's staying in, mama, quit fussing about the picture and come on.

The mother set the calculator down on top of the notepad like that settled it for good, and it did.

The mother: Come here and gimme some sugar, cause we are going to the Smokies.
