Let me tell you how that whole day went, because it wadn't just us young'uns and Mawmaw's own children out there, it was kinfolk from three different counties by the time the trucks was all parked. Word got around that the house was fixin' to be sold and everybody who'd ever set foot in it wanted one more turn through the rooms before some stranger's name went on the deed. The taxes had got up past what my aunt could carry by herself, and the roof was fixin' to go, and won't nobody in that family got the money or the want-to to fix a roof on a house that wadn't gone be lived in again noway. Mawmaw had been gone since the spring, God rest her, and the house had just sat there since, and mama an' them finally said enough is enough, we got to clear it out proper and let it go.

We started in the front room, on account of that's the room you walk into first and it seemed right to start where she'd have started, greeting folks. My aunt was the one going through the china cabinet, and she come up holding Mawmaw's old hymnal, the spine near about gone, pages soft as cloth.

My aunt: Lord, I ain't seen this thing in I don't know how long. She used to keep it right here on top, remember, and she'd hum through the whole thing of a Sunday evening whether we had company or not.

The mother: I remember. She liketa drove Daddy crazy singing "In the Garden" while he was tryin' to watch his ballgame.

Everybody laughed at that, and my aunt set it careful in the box marked KEEP, like it might crumble if she wadn't gentle with it.

From there we went on into the kitchen, which is where the second thing turned up, the kind that don't bring back a single memory to a living soul. Down in the bottom drawer, behind the good spoons, was a little tin box, rusted shut, and when the eldest finally got it open with a screwdriver there wadn't nothing in it but three foreign coins and a folded-up piece of paper with numbers on it that didn't match a phone number or nothing else anybody could figure.

The eldest: Anybody know what in the world this is?

My uncle: I don't have the first idea. Might could be from when she and Papaw went overseas that one time, but that was before I was even born.

Nobody could say for certain, and reckon we never will now, so it went in the box marked WHO KNOWS, which was really just an old shoebox somebody had wrote on with a marker.

The argument in the kitchen was over Mawmaw's cedar chest, the one at the foot of her bed that everybody had their eye on. My aunt wanted it on account of she was the one who used to hide in it playing games as a girl, but the mother said she was owed it since she was the one who'd drove out here every single week the last two years to check on her. It got a little heated, more than it needed to, but the oldest cousin present, who wadn't even blood kin but had married into the family thirty years back and everybody counted as such, put a stop to it quick.

Her workmate's husband, which is to say the cousin-in-law everybody just calls kin now: Y'all stop it right now, you're gonna make her turn over in her grave carrying on like this over a piece of furniture. Draw straws on it, or one of you take it and the other one take the quilt chest in the hall, and let's be done.

That settled it. The mother took the cedar chest and my aunt took the quilt chest, and both of 'em hugged on it like it was the last word.

Now the disagreement over whether a thing ought to be kept at all, that was the recliner in the den, the ugly gold one with the busted footrest that Papaw used to sit in every night of his life. Half the family wanted it hauled straight to the road, said it was cattywampus and smelled like fifty years of cigarettes, and the other half like to had a fit at the thought of it going to the dump.

The youngest: That chair is an eyesore, plumb worn through on the arm, ain't nobody sitting in that thing again.

The eldest: You don't touch that chair. That was Papaw's chair. I don't care if it's ugly, it stays in the family or it don't leave here at all.

They went back and forth on it a good while, mash the button on the TV just so somebody had something else to look at besides each other, until finally they agreed the eldest would take it home and reupholster it, which everybody knew good and well wadn't never gonna happen, but it kept the peace.

The hidden thing nobody expected turned up in Mawmaw's closet, taped up underneath the bottom drawer of her dresser where the wood had been pried loose. It was a coffee can, and inside was near two hundred dollars in old bills and coins, plus a little envelope of what looked like ration stamps from way back. Nobody had the first clue she'd had that stashed.

My uncle: Well I'll be. Mad money. She always did say a woman ought to keep some back that her husband don't know about.

The mother: Bless her heart, she was smarter than all of us put together.

Her bedroom was the room that took the longest by a mile, longer than the kitchen and the front room and the den combined, and I mean it wadn't even close. Every drawer had something folded up inside another something, every shoebox on the closet shelf had photographs or letters or buttons saved off of dresses forty years gone. We was in there from midmorning clean past noon, and every few minutes somebody would holler out over something they found, a lock of hair in a envelope, a report card, a corsage gone brown and flat as paper.

Somewhere in the middle of all that we broke for dinner, on account of a body can't clear out a house on an empty stomach and won't nobody trying to. Somebody had brought a mess of greens and a pan of cornbread, and there was fried chicken and two kinds of coke in the cooler and a whole gallon of sweet tea that didn't hardly last the hour. We sat out on the tailgates and the porch steps and just ate together, all of us, and it was the first time all day anybody had sat still.

The mother: Y'all get you some more of them greens before the young'uns finish 'em off.

The eldest: I done ate plenty, ma'am, I'm liable to bust.

After dinner we got on to the big stuff, and the piano was the piece nobody in the end wanted, which surprised everybody because it had sat in that front room longer than half the grandchildren had been alive. It was out of tune bad, and moving it was gonna take four grown men and a dolly and a prayer, and when it come down to it not one soul had a place to put it or the money to get it tuned right.

My aunt: I hate it worse than anything, but I ain't got room, and it needs work bad as that roof did.

Uncle: Ain't nobody gonna play it noway, all the grandbabies took up guitar instead.

So they called a fellow who does estate work and he come and hauled it off that same afternoon, and there wadn't a dry eye watching it go out the door sideways, tipped up cattywampus on its side like something dying.

The one thing everybody agreed on quick, no argument at all, was the cast iron skillet, the big one she cooked cornbread in every day of her life. Everybody just looked at each other and said the same name at the same time, the one who does most of the cooking now for the holidays, and that was that, settled in about ten seconds flat, quicker than anything else all day.

By late afternoon the sewing room stood empty, swept clean, sunlight coming through them bare windows onto a floor that hadn't seen bare boards in decades. Somebody stood in the doorway a minute just looking at it, not saying nothing, and I reckon we was all thinking the same thing without having to say it out loud.

We divided up what little was left out on the carport, odds and ends nobody had claimed outright, and it went easy enough, everybody taking a little something, a mixing bowl, a set of dominoes, a framed picture off the hallway wall. Wadn't no fussing left in anybody by then.

The last thing before we left was cutting the lights off room by room, the eldest going through and mashing every switch, checking each one twice like Mawmaw always did. We stood a minute on the porch, all of us, the whole wide circle of kin that had come, and then somebody locked the door for the last time and we all went on home, and I don't reckon that house ever felt so empty as it did with the porch light off and all of us standing out yonder in the yard not wanting to be the first one to pull out.
