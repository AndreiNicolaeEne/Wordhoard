Okay now, since you never saw a lick of it, let me start you from the beginning, 'cause if I just tell you the end you ain't gonna believe me and you sure ain't gonna get why it's still funny.

So this was a Sunday, after church, after dinner, and everybody was still around 'cause we was fixin' to have supper later too, mama had done cooked enough for three families like she does. Mama an' them had been in that kitchen since Saturday night, greens and all, a whole mess of greens on the stove, and the men was all out back on the porch 'cause it was too hot to be inside with that oven going. Kinfolk everywhere. My brother-in-law had brought the bones out, the good set, the heavy ones, and we set up right there on the card table somebody had drug out from the carport.

Now I want you to understand, it started as nothing. Just talk. I said something like, "I don't know why you even set down to play me, you know how this go," just running my mouth the way you do, and he said, "Is that right," and I said, "That's right," and it just kind of grew a life of its own from there before either one of us meant a thing by it.

My cousin was standing there with a coke in his hand, and he's the one that lit the fuse. He said:

The cousin: Y'all wanna make this interesting or naw?

Me: Shoot, I ain't scared.

The cousin: Naw I mean for real. Y'all put something on it.

And my brother-in-law, he wasn't as sure of himself as I was, I could tell, but he wasn't backing down neither on account of everybody standing around watching now. My uncle had come over off the grill with his tongs still in his hand, and he started right in too, egging it on, saying I was fixin' to get showed up and I better watch myself. Between him and the cousin and two of my nephews who'd wandered over, the pot kept climbing before a single domino had even been flipped. Somebody said fifty. Somebody said, naw, that's too much, don't be ugly about it now, let's keep it reasonable. We settled on twenty. Twenty dollars, small enough not to sting nobody, and everybody shook on it right there before we even drew our hands.

I want to tell you, that game went on a good while. Back and forth, back and forth, me up, then him up, then me again. I was playing good, I ain't gonna lie to you, I had that boneyard read like a book. But along about the middle he caught a run I did not see coming, and I mean it turned, and it turned hard. He laid down two in a row that closed off everything I had left in my hand, and right then, before the game was even technically over, everybody on that porch already knew. You could see it on their faces. My aunt hollered out, "ooh, bless his heart," and she wasn't talking to me kind, she was talking about me.

And when it was over, well and truly over, I just put my head down on that table and groaned, 'cause there wasn't a point in pretending. I said, "I done lost, y'all, ain't no use in it," and everybody commenced to hollering and carrying on like I'd lost a football game and not a domino match on somebody's back porch.

Now here's where it gets strange, and here's the part I need you to really listen to. Nobody wanted that twenty dollars. My cousin, he's the one, he stood up and said:

The cousin: Naw now, hold up. We ain't taking his money. Money's boring. We gonna make him do something.

And the whole porch just about came apart with how much they liked that idea. My uncle was already grinning around his cigarette. My nephews was jumping around like it was Christmas. And they didn't leave it vague neither, they hashed out right then and there exactly what it had to be: I had to sing the whole first verse and the whole chorus, not humming it, singing it, of a certain song everybody knows real good in this town, standing up on the tailgate of my brother-in-law's truck, hips going the whole time, in front of the entire family, at the next big cookout, and, this is the part that got me, wearing my uncle's old white jumpsuit collar he'd had hanging in his closet since the seventies for reasons nobody has ever explained to me.

I tried to talk my way out of it. I said, "Look, I'll just give y'all the twenty, we call it square, ain't no need for all that," and they laughed me right out of the yard. My brother-in-law said, "Naw sir, we done decided, ain't no bargaining now," and everybody agreed, laughing, shaking their heads, no way, no how, that bet was locked in tighter than a jar of mama's pickles.

They set the day right there, the next Saturday, and the place, which was gonna be right in that same backyard where half the family was already fixin' to be for the cookout anyway. And when that Saturday came, God willing and the creek didn't rise, it happened proper, in front of everybody who was there, and by then word had gotten around so there was even more people there than usual, kinfolk coming from clean across town just to watch.

I stood up on that tailgate in that hot old collar with sweat already running down my back, and my brother-in-law mashed the button on that little speaker somebody had brought, and I did it. I sang the whole thing, hips and all, right there yonder where the light from the porch was hitting me just right, like a spotlight, like I was somebody. Everybody was screaming laughing before I'd even got through the first line good.

But the moment, the one that still gets brought up to this day, my aunt, the one who'd said bless his heart at the domino table, she got so tickled watching me shake around up there that she stood straight up out of her lawn chair to laugh better, and she knocked the whole cooler of cokes right over, ice and all, cattywampus all over the grass, and didn't even notice, didn't even look down, just kept on hollering and clapping with coke running everywhere around her feet. That woman laughed so hard she had to hold onto the fence. The whole yard lost it. My nephew said later he liketa died. Somebody had to go cut the lights on out there 'cause it had gotten dark by then and nobody had even noticed, we was all so far gone laughing.

And that twenty dollars ain't never changed hands to this day, and honestly I don't reckon anybody even remembers whose idea the bet was in the first place. But every single time this family gets together now, somebody brings it up, somebody says "remember when he had to," and it gets told all over again, and I promise you it gets a little bigger every time. Last go-round somebody swore there was a full crowd of thirty people and I did two whole verses, when it wasn't but maybe fifteen of us and one verse and a chorus, tops. Give it another year or two and I reckon I'll have danced on the roof of the truck instead of the tailgate, and there'll be a marching band in it somewhere. That's just how it goes with this bunch. The bet's long dead and paid for. The story's the part that won't quit.
