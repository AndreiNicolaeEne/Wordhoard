Right, sit down there and I'll tell you the whole of it, because it was fierce craic for the first half of the night and an absolute disaster of a job for the second half, and I love him for every minute of it.

By the end of the night, it's banjaxed he was, no messing, not merry, not the usual grand skinful, banjaxed altogether, eyes going two different directions, head hanging like it was too heavy for the neck entirely. And do you want to know the very first sign it had gone past the usual? I asked him was he alright and he said,

My friend: I am, I'm grand, I'm actually grand, amn't I always grand.

Me: You amn't grand at all, sit down before you fall down.

And that was the tell, because the second he stood up off the stool to prove me wrong, the legs went from under him like a foal on ice, didn't even manage the one step, I'd him by the arm before he hit the deck. And he's still going, I'm grand, I'm grand, except it's coming out more like I'm gran, I'm gran, the words sliding into each other, and I knew then we were well past the point where another pint would sort him out.

Getting him out the door was its own production. He did not want to go, not a bit of it.

My friend: One more, sure one more won't kill a man.

Me: It'll kill you stone dead, get up out of that.

My friend: I'm only after sitting down.

Me: You're after sitting down since half seven, come on now.

He gave out to me something shocking, said I was some class of a tyrant, that a man can't enjoy himself with me around, that yer man behind the bar would back him up. Yer man behind the bar just looked at the two of us and shook his head like he'd seen it a hundred times, which he probably had, and told me mind him on the way, which I thought was decent of him. In the end I got him up by main force, and out we went, him roaring something about notions, that I had notions about myself thinking I knew better than him.

The walk should have been ten minutes, it's not far at all in the ordinary run of things, but with an arm around him the whole way, taking half his weight, steering him off the road and round the bins and away from a bollard he was dead set on introducing himself to, it took the best part of forty minutes. He stopped twice. First time to be sick into a hedge outside somebody's wall, and I stood there rubbing his back telling him he was grand, get it up, better out than in. Second time he just sat down on the wall itself, no warning at all, folded onto it like the battery went, and said he only needed a minute.

There was yer wan out walking a dog past us at that exact moment, and the look she gave the two of us I won't forget in a hurry. Half disgust, half pity, and she pulled the dog in closer to her like we might be contagious. I gave her the small nod that says sorry now, we're grand, nothing to see here, and she walked on faster than she'd been walking before. Fair enough, I suppose, it was a sight, him hanging off a strange wall and me standing over him like I was minding a prisoner.

We took a wrong turn as well, in fairness, because I'd had a fair few myself by then, nothing near what he'd had but enough. Brought him down the wrong road entirely, the one that only looks the same as ours in the dark, and we were a good five minutes down it before he stopped dead and said, very solemn for a man who could barely stand,

My friend: Dat's not right. Dat's not de way at all. I don't know dis road.

And he was dead right, God help us, the one flash of sense he had all night was pointing out I'd made a right hames of the directions. Turned around, doubled back, the pair of us laughing though neither of us really found it funny, only the laughing you do when you're knackered and it's late and the whole thing is well past sense, and half hoping no squad car of guards would come round the corner and find the two of us in that state stumbling about the road.

Getting water into him after was its own war entirely. I kept at him the whole way, you need water, drink the water, and he kept waving me off, the notions of him, said he was grand, water was for people who were actually sick and he wasn't sick, he was only relaxed. I'm after telling him fifty times he'd thank me for it in the morning and he wasn't having any of it. It was only near the door that he declared, sudden as you like, that he was parched, dying for a drop, like the idea was his own all along, and drank half the bottle standing in the front garden with his head back.

Somewhere between the wall and the water he came out with the one line that stopped me for a second. Grabbed my arm, went very serious, very sincere, the eyes glassy and full, and said,

My friend: D'you know something, you're the best friend a man ever had going. I mean dat. I'd do anything for you, anything at all, d'you know dat.

Me: Ah stop, you're only codding.

Me: I know you would. Now will you keep walking.

And he near cried over it. Pure maudlin, soft as you like, the kind of thing wouldn't come out of him sober in a hundred years, he'd sooner die than say a word like that to me stone cold, but three sheets gone he's a poet altogether.

The door was the last battle of the night. Fumbling for the key, dropping it, him trying to help and only making it worse, leaning his whole weight on the door so it wouldn't budge even with the key turned, the two of us cursing under our breath so as not to wake the house. Got it open eventually, near fell into the hall, and I walked him, carried him more if I'm honest, into the front room and got him down onto the couch before he went down on his own terms, which would have been a lot rougher.

Once he was down he grabbed my sleeve again, dead serious, and made his declaration for the night.

My friend: Dat's it now. I'm done. I'm never drinking like dat again as long as I live. I mean it dis time.

Me: You will so. Sure you say that every single time.

He went to argue the point but he was asleep before he got the sentence out, so that settled the matter for us both.

Rang him the next morning, more out of duty than anything, to check was he alive at all. He answered like a man who'd been kilt, the voice like gravel dragged over stone, and I said how's yourself, and he said desperate, absolutely desperate, his head like a drum going, and could he ever act the maggot like that again as long as he lived. I asked what he remembered and it was gas, honestly, bits and pieces of it, the football, the first few pints, something about a hedge he wasn't sure was real or only a dream of one. Nothing after that though, the walk was gone entirely, the wall was gone, yer wan with the dog was gone, and the big sincere speech about being the best friend a man ever had going was gone as well, thank God, though I told him about that one anyway because it was too good to let lie. Filled the rest in for him bit by bit and he was mortified the whole way through it, saying feck's sake, feck's sake, over and over, asking was he really that bad, and I said worse, and he believed me, the eejit.

He's grand now anyway. Sore for a few days after, swore up and down all over again that that was the last of it, and sure we'll see how long that stretch of good behaviour lasts. But that's the whole of it, and I wouldn't have missed a minute of it for the world, not even the bit in the hedge.
