By half one the house had thinned right down to the five of them, the good delph washed and put away back in the press, the everyday cups out on the table, and the immersion left on above in the hot press though there wasn't a sinner left to want a bath off it. The mother had the pot going again. Nobody had asked her to.

The eldest was the one started it off, not meaning anything by it, just looking at the window over the sink.

The eldest: D'yiz remember he used to stand there of a morning with the tea in his hand, watching out for the bin men like it was the match itself.

The sister: Twas Tuesdays only, love. That's collection day. He wasn't standing there every morning of his life.

The eldest: Ah here, you're right, I always have that one wrong. I do. Every time I tell it I have him standing there seven days a week and sure the man had better things to be doing than watching a bin lorry on a Sunday.

That got a small laugh going round, and the youngest let one out too loud altogether, a proper bark of a laugh that surprised even herself, and put a hand up over her mouth after it. Nobody minded. The neighbour waved a hand at her, sure isn't it grand to hear a laugh in this house tonight of all nights.

The neighbour: D'ye know what he used to say to me, and I can hear it as clear as if he was after saying it this minute. He'd take one bite of the fry and go, isn't dat only gorgeous, dat is. Every single Sunday of his life. Didn't matter if the eggs were like rubber, isn't dat only gorgeous, dat is.

The mother said it back, exact, the same stretch on it and everything, the same little pause before "gorgeous," and for a second it was like the man himself was in it with them.

The mother: Isn't dat only gorgeous, dat is. God, I can hear him.

Quiet for a second after that, the good kind, and then the neighbour went on, quieter now, like she wasn't sure should she say it.

The neighbour: Do yiz know he used to go up to the church of a Wednesday evening on his own. Not for Mass or nothing, just sat above in the porch a while. I only know cause I seen him one time collecting the youngfella from the football and asked was he right, and he said he does be up there most Wednesdays, praying for a fella he worked with years back that had no one after him when he went. Never told a soul that I know of.

The sister: Never.

The eldest: He never said that to me in his life.

Quiet again, proper quiet this time, the kind where you can hear the immersion tank ticking above in the hot press, until the mother stood up and got the plate of sandwiches going round again, an excuse as much as anything.

The mother: Go on, eat something, the lot of yiz, there's plenty there and it'll only go stale.

The sister took one and looked at the eldest over the top of it, and something passed between them that had nothing to do with tonight at all.

The sister: You still owe me for the loan of the good tablecloth in nineteen ninety whatever it was, you know that.

The eldest: Would you leave that, I gave that back to you years ago.

The sister: You did in your eye.

The eldest: I'm not having this out tonight.

The sister: Nor me either. Forget I said it.

And that was the end of that, the two of them going back to their tea like nothing was said, because nothing was going to be sorted at half one in the morning with the man only two days gone.

It was the youngest brought up the stubbornness, laughing before she even got the story out properly.

The youngest: Do yiz remember the immersion switch. He wouldn't have an electrician near the house, wouldn't hear of it, said any fella could rewire a switch and he wasn't paying good money for a job he could do himself. Fused the whole downstairs three times. Three times. And still wouldn't call the man.

The eldest: And herself giving out to him the whole time.

The mother: I was hoarse from giving out to him. He'd stand there in the dark with the torch going, mutter something and go back at it again. Stubborn as a mule over a switch that cost a fiver in the shop.

The neighbour: That was him all over though.

The mother: That was him all over.

Nobody said it straight out, but it settled in the room anyway, that if you were asked to put your finger on the one thing, that was it, the sheer bloody stubbornness of him, that once he'd decided a thing he'd go to the wall over it, and how it used to drive the whole house demented and how they'd give anything now to see him standing in the dark with that torch one more time.

It was somewhere in the middle of the stubbornness talk that the youngest went quiet, and when the sister looked over she saw the tears going down her, not making a sound about it, just sitting there letting them come while the rest kept talking, nobody stopping to make a fuss of her, only the mother reaching a hand over without looking and leaving it on her arm a minute, and the talk went on gentle around her the way water goes round a stone.

After a while it turned to the practical, the way it does.

The sister: Who's ringing the undertaker in the morning about the removal time.

The eldest: I will so, first thing.

The mother: And someone'll need to do the messages, we're near out of milk and there'll be crowds in and out all day tomorrow.

The neighbour: I'll do the messages, don't you worry about that, you've enough on you.

The eldest: And the flowers, are we right for the flowers.

The sister: I'll see to the flowers.

The mother: Grand so. That's that settled anyway.

It was the neighbour still awake when the rest had started nodding, the last of the whiskey going round in small ones, no ceremony to it, just the bottle passed hand to hand, and she stood up with her glass like she'd only just thought of it.

The neighbour: Here's to himself so. Wherever he is, and knowing him he's above giving out to someone about wiring.

The mother: To himself.

The eldest: To himself.

And they drank it standing, the four of them and the youngest still wiping her face, and the house settled quiet around them, the kettle gone cold, the immersion still humming away above in the hot press for no one at all.
