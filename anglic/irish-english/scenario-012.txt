I called round on the Tuesday like I always do, and we were sat at the table with the tea going stone cold between us before I said a word of it. Himself was above in the front room with the match on, so it was only the two of us, and I knew rightly that if I let this evening go by without saying it I never would, I'd carry it another twenty-five years and die with it in me like an eejit.

Me: How's yourself.

Mother: Grand, grand, sit down there, I've the kettle only boiled. Himself won't stir now till the final whistle, don't mind him.

I sat down and I could feel the scar under the table, the way you'd feel a stone in your shoe, though sure I've felt it every day of my life and never thought twice about it most of the time. It's only when she'd ask that it ever came up at all, and she has asked, over the years, more times than I could count.

Mother: You're very quiet on me. Would you turn off the immersion below before you go, I left it on since this morning and it does be costing a fortune.

Me: I will, in a minute. Ma, I need to say something to you and I don't want you raging at me over it, sure it's far too late in the day for raging now.

Mother: What is it. You're after going very serious on me altogether.

Me: The leg. The scar. It wasn't the bike at all. It was never the bike.

Mother: What do you mean it wasn't the bike, sure I brought you to the doctor myself that evening, I remember the whole of it, you came in the door white as a sheet and the leg opened up something desperate.

Me: I know you remember it, that's the very thing I'm ashamed of, that you've a whole memory of a thing that never happened the way you think. It's the tree it was, down at our usual spot, the big one over the river past the field. We were dared into it, the four of us, and I'd backed out of something like it once before, the summer before that, and they never let me forget it, not for one single day. So this time when it come round again I wasn't backing out, and I went higher than any of us had ever gone, way higher than I meant to go, up past the branch we always stopped at.

Mother: Jesus, Mary and Joseph.

Me: There was a grand stretch in the evening that time of year, do you remember, we'd be out till all hours and nobody minding where we were. And I'm standing up on this branch, and below me the whole gang looking up going on so, go on, and one of them, the eldest of us, roaring up at me.

The eldest: Go on so, if you're not a big babby. You said that the last time as well.

Me: I'm not a babby.

The eldest: Prove it so.

Me: And I did. I went. And the second I was in the air I knew I'd got it wrong, I'd misjudged the whole thing, the water wasn't where I thought it'd be, and I hit it flat and it knocked every bit of the wind clean out of me, I thought I was kilt, honest to God, I couldn't get a breath in me at all for what felt like an age. And under the water there was something, a bit of an old fence post or a shopping trolley or God knows what, we never went back to look, and it opened my leg the whole way up the side, and I never even felt it happen, I only felt the cold and the not being able to breathe, and then the blood coming up through the water round me.

Mother: Oh, love.

Me: They pulled me out between them, the whole shower of them panicking as bad as I was, one of them roaring.

Yer man: Feck, feck, look at the state of him, he's after cutting the leg off himself.

Me: And we're standing there soaked to the skin, the blood running down into my shoe, and it's the eldest of us again, thinking quicker than the rest of us put together.

The eldest: Say you fell off the bike. Say it. We all say the same thing or we're all banjaxed.

Me: And we agreed it there and then, standing in the field, still dripping, the whole gang of us swore it between us like something sacred, and I went home and I told you I'd come off the bike on the gravel below the shop, and you believed me, and the leg got seen to and stitched under that story, and I've told the very same false thing to every soul that's ever asked me about that scar in twenty-five years since, doctors, the wife, the children, everyone. It's not the bike I fell off, Ma. It's a tree I jumped from on a dare, and I opened my leg on something under the water that none of us ever saw, and four children stood there and made up a lie between them because we were more afraid of being in trouble for the tree and the river than we were of anything else, and it worked, it worked so well I nearly started believing it myself after a while.

Mother: Twenty-five years. You let me believe the bike for twenty-five years.

Me: I did. And I'm sorry for it, I am, it wasn't even that I meant to keep lying, it's that once the lie was told once it was easier to keep saying the same thing than to ever go back and say I made a hames of the whole story that first night and never fixed it after.

Mother: Was I ever going to hear this at all, or were you going to let me go to my grave thinking it was a bike.

Me: I don't know, Ma, honestly. I think it's only now, being older myself, having my own crowd running round doing the same class of mad thing, that I look at them and I think, if one of them came in soaked and bleeding and swore blind it was off a bike, I'd want the truth of it, I'd want to know what really happened to my child, and I couldn't keep it any longer knowing that and having done the very same to you.

Mother: Were you not terrified telling me now.

Me: I am. I was shaking coming up the road to you, if I'm honest with you.

Mother: Are you sorry.

Me: I am. I'm sorry for the lie itself and I'm sorry for how long it went on, and I'm sorry you were worrying about the wrong things all this time, giving out to me over gravel that was never even there.

Mother: I always said you had notions of yourself, going that high. I said it to himself after, I said that child has notions, thinking he's able for anything.

Me: I did have notions, that's exactly it, I had fierce notions that evening and they nearly did for me. And I know we were only acting the maggot down there, the whole lot of us, eejits the four of us, but it could have been worse than a scar, it could have been very bad, and I think that's the part that stayed with me longest, not the cut itself but knowing how easy it would have been for one of them to be standing there with no one to pull me out at all.

Mother: You're an awful eejit, do you know that. A grand, big, grown eejit, sitting there after twenty-five years telling me you jumped off a tree instead of a bike.

Me: I am so.

Mother: Come here to me.

Me: I will.

Mother: I'm not vexed with you, love, don't be thinking that. I'm only sorry you carried it that long on your own, the whole of it, that's an awful weight for a child to carry and never say a word.

Me: It wasn't so bad. Sure it was only a story, in the end, and now it isn't one any more.

Mother: Turn off that immersion now before you go, and don't be leaving it another twenty-five years to tell me the next thing.

Me: I won't. I promise you that much, I won't.
