Right, I'll tell you the whole of it start to finish, sit down there, it's not a short one.

It started because we were bursting out of the place. Three of them in the one room, the eldest sleeping over the youngest like something out of a hostel, and the kitchen so small you couldn't swing a cat in it without giving out to somebody for standing where you wanted to be. Himself and me were at the table one night with the good pen and the back of an envelope and we reckoned, sure, if we did the guts of it ourselves with a bit of help off the family, it'd take six months, maybe seven at the outside, and three of us on it steady. Six months. I nearly laugh saying it now. It was eleven months in the end, door to door, and there wasn't a fortnight went by that some new face wasn't up a ladder or down in a trench for us. Six different people helped over the length of it, and that's not counting anyone who only came for an afternoon with a wheelbarrow.

We started well enough, foundations first, and that's where we made the hames of it. Himself was sure he had the levels right off the plans and didn't bother getting them checked, and about ten days in the brother-in-law came round, took one look down the trench and said the whole thing was out.

The brother-in-law: That's not level at all, that's after sloping the wrong way entirely.

Himself: It is not, I done that with the laser.

The brother-in-law: You done it with your eye and a prayer, more like. Look at it.

And he was right, it was out by enough that it would've shown in the floor for the rest of our days, so it all had to come up and go back in properly. Two weeks near enough, gone, digging out what we'd only just put down. I wasn't talking to himself for a good bit of that fortnight, I'll say that much.

Then there was the day the extra hands showed up and nobody had asked them. We were only after getting the block-work going again when a van pulled up and out gets the brother-in-law with two of his own crowd from the job he does be on, fellas I'd maybe met once at a christening.

Me: What's all this, we didn't ask for a gang.

The brother-in-law: Sure yous were only crawling along, I told these two we'd a day's blocklaying going and they'd nothing on. Are yous able for it?

The lads: We are.

And they were savage, honestly, the wall went up in a day that would've taken himself and me the guts of a week on our own. I gave them the dinner after and a few cans and that was that, no fuss, no bill, that's just how it does be done round our way when there's a job on.

The weather nearly finished us off around the middle of it. We were after getting the roof timbers up and were waiting on the sheeting when it started, and it rained for the better part of a week solid, none of your soft Irish drizzle either, proper stair-rods, the site a bog, the trench full, everything under a tarp that kept lifting off in the wind. You couldn't do a thing only stand at the back door looking out at it and giving out to the sky.

Herself, the eldest: Is it ever going to stop?

Me: It's to stop sometime, love, sure it always does.

It stopped, eventually, and we lost the best part of that week only mopping out and drying things before we could go near it again.

Materials ran short around then too, timber mostly, we'd ordered for what we thought was the job and it wasn't near enough once the plans changed a small bit on the layout. The yard was slow getting the rest to us so for a stretch we were making do, using shorter lengths off an old shed that was coming down anyway, doubling up bits that weren't meant to be doubled, himself getting very creative with a saw. It wasn't pretty in places but it held, and sure most of it's behind plaster now, nobody's the wiser.

I'll tell you about the fall as well, because it put the heart crossways in me at the time even though nothing came of it. Himself was up on the scaffold doing the flashing round the chimney and his foot went from under him on a plank that hadn't been pinned right, and he came down, not the whole height thank God, only off the lower lift onto the platform below, but I heard the bang from the kitchen and my stomach went through the floor.

Me: Are you alright, are you alright, don't move.

Himself: I'm grand, I'm grand, would you stop roaring.

He wasn't grand, he was white as a sheet and shook for the rest of the day, but nothing broken, only bruised down one side and mortified with himself for it. Kilt with the fright is what I was, more than he was.

There was a row too, a proper one, over the stairs, of all things. Himself wanted them going up straight along the one wall and the brother-in-law, who'd done a bit of carpentry in his time, said it was a desperate waste of the space and they should turn halfway with a landing.

Himself: I'm not putting a turn in it, it's more work and less headroom into the bargain.

The brother-in-law: You'll regret it the day you're carrying anything bigger than yourself up it. It's a hames you're after making of the whole layout if you go straight.

Himself: Would you ever stop telling me my own house.

They didn't speak for near a week over it, the pair of them like two eejits, and in the end we went with the turn, and himself never once admits the brother-in-law was right, though he does be very quiet about it whenever anyone compliments the stairs.

The gable wall was the worst of it though, worse than the foundations even. We were three courses up on the block-work before the fella doing our windows came to measure and told us the wall was out of square, not level, plumb, whichever word you want, it was wrong every way a wall can be wrong. There was nothing for it only knock the whole lot back down and start again from the footing up, a full week of work gone in an afternoon with a bolster and a lump hammer, and the neighbour next door leaning over the fence the whole time enjoying himself no end.

The neighbour: Yous are getting great practice at building that wall anyway.

Me: Would you ever go in and do your messages and leave us alone.

But it went up right the second time, and it stood, and that was that.

The day the frame finally stood up straight and true, roof timbers all in place against the sky, that was the first day the whole thing felt like it was ours rather than a hole in the ground with our savings in it. We stood out the back with the youngest and a few cans between the adults, looking up at it.

Himself: Would you look at that now.

Me: It's like a house, so it is.

The eldest: It's deadly.

Small enough of a thing to stop and look at a few bits of timber, but it was the first day I believed it'd ever be finished.

The last stretch was four nights running working on past dark with the work lamps rigged off an extension lead, plastering and first-fix electrics and himself up a ladder past eleven at night swearing under his breath, everyone knackered, everyone codding on they weren't. We were banjaxed the whole lot of us by the fourth night, but it was finished, floors down, doors hung, and we walked round it the next morning in the quiet before anyone else was up, just the two of us, not saying much.

We tallied it up after, sitting at the table the same as we'd started, eleven months from the first sod to the last coat of paint, and six different people who'd given us a hand along the way besides ourselves, not one of them paid a cent for it, only fed and thanked and remembered. Standing in it now, of an evening, the whole lot of us fitting round the one table with room to spare, I do think back on the fortnight we lost at the start, the wall we knocked down twice as good as, the fright of the fall, the row over the stairs that turned out grand in the end. It was a long aul stretch of a job, but it's ours, every crooked bit of it that only we know about, and it's a grand house to be standing in of an evening now that it's done.
