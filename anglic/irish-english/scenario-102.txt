This is the hall. Narrow as anything, you couldn't swing a cat in it, and it always smelled of whatever was after being cooked below, fry mostly, and of the good coats hanging there that never got worn only to Mass and funerals. The phone used sit on the little table there when we got one at all, and that was the coldest spot in the whole house, standing there in your socks having a conversation with your granny while the draught came in under the door. You'd know the second you came in the door what kind of a house it was, whether there was a row on or whether it was grand, you'd feel it off the walls near enough.

Down there now is the good room, we called it the good room and God help you if you were caught in it any day that wasn't Christmas or a wake. Sure it had the good three-piece suite in it, the plastic still half on the arms of it for years after we got it, and the good delph in the cabinet that never came out. That room was for visitors and priests and nothing else, we did our actual living in the back with the telly. The mother would say,

The mother: Don't be sitting in there, that room's not for sitting in.

Me: Sure what's it for so, if it's not for sitting in.

The mother: It's for good.

And that was the end of that conversation, there was no arguing it.

The kitchen is out the back and that's the heart of the whole place, always was. Smelled of tea and toast of a morning and of the dinner the rest of the day, and there was one thing that always happened in there without fail, the radio going the whole day, whatever station the mother had it on, and her doing the messages list on the back of an envelope while she was at it. The kettle was never off the boil. Himself would come in from work and the whole conversation would be had over a cup of tea before a word was said about anything that mattered. You'd be sent to the shop for the messages and told not to be codding around on the way back or there'd be trouble.

Upstairs now, three of us shared the back room for years, myself and the two below me, top and tail in the one bed when we were small and then bunks after, and you'd hear every creak the other one made all night. The eldest had the box room to herself when she got old enough that nobody could argue her out of it, and we were raging over that for a good while, I can tell you. The parents' room was down the landing, and that was a country of its own, you wouldn't go in there unless you were sick or it was Christmas morning.

Now the good armchair by the fire below, that was himself's chair, nobody sat in that, not even when he wasn't in it. You'd nearly get a clip for going near it. That chair had a shape worn into it that fit no one else. And herself, the granny, when she stayed with us, had the little stool by the window that was hers and hers alone, she'd be sitting there of an evening with her tea watching the street like it was the pictures.

My own spot, the one that was mine, was under the stairs where the coats and the hoover were kept, there was a gap behind the ironing board just big enough for me to get into with a torch and a comic, and nobody thought to look for me there for years. I'm after spending half my childhood in that cupboard, if the truth be told.

Out the back was the yard, concrete, with the clothesline strung across it and the coal shed in the corner that always had spiders the size of your fist in it. That's where you'd be sent to get the coal in, cursing the whole way. The wall at the end of it was low enough that you could see over into next door, and the neighbour there, a lovely woman, would be out hanging her own washing the same time every day near enough, and there'd be a chat over the wall about nothing in particular.

The street itself made its own noises, same time every evening, you'd hear the Angelus bell going off in the distance and everyone's dinner would start around then, like the whole road ran on it. And every single day, same time, an old fella would go by with his dog, out for the constitutional, rain, hail or shine, and you could nearly set your watch by the two of them.

The one thing that was always broken, or if not broken then always being given out about, was the immersion. There was murder in that house over the immersion, someone always leaving it on too long or not long enough.

Father: Who left the immersion on again, there won't be a hot drop left for anyone.

The eldest: I amn't after touching it.

Father: Someone's after touching it, it doesn't turn itself on.

It went on like that for years, that immersion caused more rows than anything else in the whole house.

The place changed with the seasons more than you'd think for somewhere so small. Summer the door stood open the whole day and the street was full of us, kerbs and rounders and whatever else, half the road out playing till it was near dark and someone's mother roaring out the door for them to come in. Winter it went the other way, everyone in, fire lit, condensation running down the windows, and it felt twice as small and twice as warm at the same time. There'd be a bonfire down the green come the autumn and the whole street out for it, adults included, standing round with tea in their hands acting like they weren't freezing.

The last clear memory I have of the place is standing in that same hall with everything packed into boxes, the good room empty for the first time in its life, and the mother crying over nothing you could put your finger on, just the leaving of it. Someone said something to try and lighten it and someone else told them to stop acting the maggot, this wasn't the time, and we all had a small laugh over that in the middle of it, which was probably the point.

It's still standing, the house, I drove past it a while back. New door on it, new windows, they've done something to the front that I couldn't tell you the sense of, and the wall out the back is higher than it was, they've built up the yard into something else entirely. You wouldn't know it from the outside now, not really, though I'd know that step in the hall blindfolded if they ever let me in the door again.
