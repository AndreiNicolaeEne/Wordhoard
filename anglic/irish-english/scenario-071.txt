God, love, sit down there a minute because I'm banjaxed altogether and I have to get this out of me before I can even think about my bed.

You know the laneway out the back of us, the one runs behind all the gardens the length of the road. State of it for years. Brambles up over your head, an oul shed roof after collapsing in on itself since the storms back in the spring, rats in it the size of cats you'd swear. The corpo were meant to come out and clear it two years ago now, and yiz can imagine how that went. So it just sat there rotting away, and every one of us giving out about it over the wall to each other of an evening and doing nothing only that.

Then last night the neighbour above, the one with the greyhound, he's out at his gate roaring up and down that he's had enough of it, that he's ordering a skip for Saturday morning whether a soul helps him or not, and would we all come out and get the job done between us for once. And I'm telling you, word went up and down that road faster than you'd believe. He wasn't even back in his own door before yer wan across from him was out banging on three more, and by bedtime half the street had it that there was a skip coming and everyone was to be out at nine sharp.

We thought maybe eight of us'd turn out, the usual crowd. There was over twenty standing there at nine, and more again dribbling in after, kilt with the cold half of them, standing round saying how's yourself to each other and rubbing their hands. People you'd never see out, yer man from the far end that keeps to himself, oul ones you wouldn't think could lift the messages never mind a sheet of corrugated iron. It was mad altogether, the turnout of it.

So the neighbour above starts dividing us, some at the brambles with the shears, some loading the skip, a crowd of the men getting stuck into the shed itself, because that was the real job of it, that was the heavy end. And it was himself from three doors down took that on. Does be doing the marathon every year, that fella, and it showed, he was in under that roof half the morning hauling the sheets out near enough on his own, sweating like a pig, wouldn't let anyone near him with it.

"Are you right under there?" says someone.

"I am," says he, and back in again.

Then about eleven we had the disaster. One of the sheets he's hauling out catches on something and the whole lot comes down sideways, and doesn't it take the top clean off the wall between two of the gardens. Shout out of the whole lane, I nearly died. Nobody hurt, thank God, only the wall in bits and himself standing there covered in rust and cobwebs looking like he'd seen a ghost. It's fright we got, I'll tell you that for nothing.

And that's when the row started, because the two neighbours whose gardens meet at that wall have never agreed on whose wall it is anyway, and now one of them wants it built back up in blocks and the other's having none of it.

The one whose side it fell on: We'll get it in blocks, sure you can't get that oul brick anymore anyway.

The other one: We will not. It goes back in the old brick or it doesn't go back at all.

The one whose side it fell on: Ah would you stop, you're only a culchie for wanting to match brick that's a hundred years old.

The other one: And you're a right jackeen gobshite that'd block over anything to save himself the bother.

The two of them going at it like Christmas, and everyone else standing round with shovels not knowing where to look. It was the neighbour above sorted it in the end, told the pair of them they were only making a hames of a grand morning's work over a wall that stood there since before either of them was born, and wouldn't they shake on it and split the cost of the blocks between them and leave the moaning for another day. And they did, so. Grand as anything after that, the two of them sharing a can within the hour.

By one the food started coming out. Yer wan across the road had a table up at her gate with tea in a flask the size of a bin, straight out of the press it was, she said, been saving it for a day like this, and sandwiches for the whole road. Then herself two doors down comes out with a tray of sausages off a barbecue nobody knew she even owned, tongs and apron and all, and somebody muttered she was getting notions about herself, but sure everyone ate them fast enough. Cans going too for whoever wanted one once the shovels were down a minute. Nobody went hungry, I'll tell you that.

The children were put doing the small bits, gathering the bramble cuttings into piles, carrying the smaller stones over for the wall, delighted with themselves they were, thinking it was the real work same as the men. One of them went too near the skip and got a right giving-out for it from the mother, the bold thing, grinning the whole time she was at him. And the oul fella from the end house, must be near ninety, wasn't having it sitting on his chair watching either. Up he was directing the whole operation, telling everyone where things were meant to go, and when nobody minded him fast enough didn't he get himself a sweeping brush and start on the footpath, and you couldn't get him to stop for love nor money.

"You'll do yourself an injury," says herself to him.

"I'm after doing harder days than dis one," says he, "so go on away and let me at it."

Amn't I well used to him by now, and he was right, he outlasted half of us.

The one thing had the whole lane in stitches was the dog. Acting the maggot the whole morning he was, and then he gets himself into the skip after something and ends up stuck to the middle in an oul mattress that was in there, legs going like mad and getting nowhere, roaring his head off, and there was men had been working solid four hours flat on the ground laughing at him. Even the two that had the row over the wall were laughing. That dog done more for morale than the lot of us put together.

We thought we'd be done by two. We were nowhere near it. Gone five it was, three hours later than anyone had in their head, before the last of it was finished, the skip filled twice over near enough, the wall back up between the two gardens, the lane you could walk down without a machete, the shed gone entirely bar the base of it. When the last of the iron went in there was a cheer went up the whole length of the road, I'm not codding you, and car horns going and someone's youngfella letting off a party popper he'd been saving since Christmas.

And the street was different that evening, love. Sure it'll go back to itself inside a week, everyone behind their own door same as always. But for that evening, with the chairs still out and the last of the cans going round in a grand stretch in the evening, it didn't feel like that at all. Felt like somewhere you'd want to be.
