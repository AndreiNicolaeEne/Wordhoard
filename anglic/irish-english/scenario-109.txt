The key wasn't even fully turned in the door when I heard the telly go off inside, and then the hall light came on over me, and there she was standing in the kitchen doorway with the tea towel still in her hand, looking at me like I was after landing from the moon.

Mother: Oh my God. Oh my God, is that you? IS THAT YOU?

Me: It's me, Ma.

Mother: JESUS, MARY AND JOSEPH AND ALL THE SAINTS...

And she was across the hall before she'd the towel down, and the arms were round me and she wasn't letting go, not for a good while, not for ages, actually, she just stood there holding on with her face into my shoulder and I could feel her going, so I held on an' all, and over her head I could see the father coming out of the front room in his socks, and the eldest coming down the stairs two at a time roaring back into the sitting room.

The eldest: HE'S HERE, HE'S ACTUALLY HERE, GET UP OUTTA THAT...

Father: Would you let the man in the door before ye smother him, for the love a God.

Mother: I will not let go of him, I'm after waiting eight years for this, you can wait your turn.

Father: Fair enough so. Come here to me anyway.

And the father had a hold of me then too, the two of them near enough at the same time, and he was thumping me on the back the way he does, and saying nothing much, just "good man, good man" over and over, which is as much as you'd get out of him even if the house was on fire, but his eyes were bright enough that I knew well he was fighting something back and not quite winning.

The eldest: Would you look at the state of him. Would you LOOK at him.

Mother: You've gone thin. Have you not been eating over there at all?

Me: I have been eating, Ma, don't start.

Mother: You have not, look at the face on you, you're gaunt.

The eldest: He's not gaunt, he's just not fat off your dinners anymore, that's all that is.

Father: He's after growing an inch as well I'd say, would you not?

Me: I don't think you grow at my age, Da.

Father: Ah you never know. Stranger things.

The aunt: Is that a bit of grey in it? IN THE HAIR? Would you look, there's a bit of grey coming in above the ear.

Me: Don't be putting years on me the second I'm in the door.

The aunt: I'm only saying it, sure it suits you.

And then it all came at once, the whole lot of it, everyone talking over everyone, so that I couldn't rightly answer one thing before the next one landed on top of it.

The eldest: Did you hear about the cousin, the one out in...

Mother: Would you let him get his coat off first...

The eldest:, she's after getting engaged, over Christmas, massive fuss about it...

Father: And yer man from down the road, you'd remember him, he's after selling the house and going to Australia of all places...

Mother: And do you remember the wan that used be minding you when you were small, she's after having her third now, a little girl...

The eldest: And the young fella next door, the one that used be only a babby, he's about six foot now, you wouldn't know him...

Mother:, himself above in the house, the uncle, he's not been well at all, we'll tell you about that in a minute, not now...

Father: There's a fierce amount to catch you up on, that's for certain.

Me: Stop, stop, one at a time, I can't take it all in, I'm only in the door!

Mother: Sure that's what happens, we've eight years of it saved up for you.

Over near the stairs, half behind the banister, the youngest was hanging back, not saying a word, just peering out from round the wall the way a small one does when there's too much noise in a room for their liking.

Me: Well, would you look who it is. Come here to me, are you not going to say hello at all?


Mother: Go on, don't be shy now, sure isn't that your own...

The eldest: He barely knows you, sure the last time he saw you he was only walking, weren't you, love, you don't remember a bit of it.

Me: That's alright, we'll get to know each other again so.

And the youngest gave a small smile at that and ducked back a bit further, delighted with the attention and mortified by it in the same breath, the way small ones are.

It was only then I noticed it, standing there in the hall with the coats and the smell of the house all around me, the exact same smell it always was, and under it something cooking.

Me: Is that a stew on? I'd know that smell anywhere.

Mother: It is, I put it on the second I heard you were landed today, sure I wasn't going to have you walking in to nothing in the pot.

Me: God, that takes me right back, that does.

Father: Nothing changes in this house, sure the smell of that stew has been the same for forty years.

Me: The place hasn't changed much either, do you know that. The wallpaper in the hall's the same, the good room's the exact same, even the creak on that third stair is still there.

The eldest: Some things they never fix.

Father: Why would we fix it, sure it tells us when yous are sneaking in late.

Me: But the road out there's after changing altogether, all them new places gone up where the old shop used be, I hardly knew where I was walking.

Mother: Ah sure the whole area's gone mad building everything, you'd want to see the price of a house round here now, it'd put years on you.

Father: Desperate, so it is. Anyway. How long are you stopping this time? Are you back for good or is it only the flying visit?

Me: I'm here a good few weeks anyway, we'll see after that.

Mother: A few weeks! Sure that's nothing, is it good enough.

Me: It's what I have, Ma.

Mother: Well we'll make the most of it so. I'm ringing the whole family the day, we'll have everyone over Sunday, a proper dinner, the works, I'm not having you back in the country and no gathering for it.

The aunt: Do the messages tomorrow so, I'll come with you, we'll get everything in, nothing left to the last minute.

Father: And don't be forgetting the drink, sure half of them will only come for that anyway.

The eldest: Do you remember, before you went, the state you made of that barbecue, the year you nearly burnt the shed down?

Me: I made a fine hames of that, in fairness, I'll give you that one.

The eldest: A fine hames is right, you were only codding us it was under control, and the whole fence nearly went up.

Me: Ah stop, I was young and foolish.

The eldest: You're not much better now, are you.

Father: He's grand, leave the man alone, he's only in the door.

The eldest: I'm only saying, sure it's tired you must be after all that travelling.

Me: I am, actually, I'm banjaxed, if I'm honest, but I wasn't going to say it with the head that's on this house right now.

Mother: Sure you can rest after the tea, there's no rush at all, you're home now.

And for a second, just the one second, the whole hall went quiet, the lot of them just standing there looking at me like they still couldn't credit it, the bags still sitting where I'd dropped them by the door, forgotten altogether, and nobody saying a word, just looking, the mother with her hand still resting on my arm like she was afraid I'd vanish again if she let go.

Father: Well. How's yourself, anyway. Properly, I mean.

Me: I'm grand, Da. I'm delighted to be back, so I am.

Father: You are so.

And then the kettle went on, and the youngest crept out a small bit further from behind the banister, and the noise started up again, softer this time, everyone settling in around the table the way they always did, like I'd never been away at all.
