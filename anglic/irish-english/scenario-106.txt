Sure I don't tell this one often, and when I do I try not to make a big deal of it, because it wasn't a big deal, not really, not compared to what some had of it. But you asked me now so I'll tell it to you straight, the whole of it, and you can just sit there and take it in, you don't have to say anything back.

It was up on the border road, the checkpoint, and it was there because that stretch was the crossing point for the whole of that side, everyone funnelled through the one spot whether they liked it or not, cars and the bus both, and there was no way round it that anyone sensible would try. So you just joined the queue like everyone else and you waited your turn same as the rest of them.

And there was a good stretch of a queue by the time I got up to it, a good half an hour I'd say, standing there in the one spot, inching up bit by bit, the car in front of me and the car in front of that, and nothing to do only stand there with your own thoughts for company, which isn't always the best company to have. I won't lie to you, I was in bits going up to it. Not shaking exactly, but there was a hop in my stomach the whole way, and I kept going over in my head what I was going to say before anyone even asked me anything, which is a fright you give yourself for nothing half the time, but sure you can't help it. It's nervous I was, plain and simple, and I'm not ashamed to say it now.

When I got up to the front there was a young fella there, a soldier, and he put his hand out for the papers without much of a hello, and I handed them over, no bother, my hand steady enough though God knows what was going on underneath.

He looked at the papers a second and then he asked me the first thing.

The soldier: Where are you travelling to.

Me: Home. Down south.

Plain as that, and true as that, so it came out easy enough, no trouble in it at all.

Then he asked me the second thing, and this one I had to mind how I answered, because it wasn't as simple a thing to say straight out, there was a bit more to it and you'd want to get the shape of it right or it'd sound like something it wasn't.

The soldier: And what were you doing up this end.

Me: Visiting family. My aunt's not been well, I was up seeing to her a few days.

And that was true too, every word of it, but you'd want to say it careful, not rushed, not like you were reciting it, because a rushed answer sounds worse than a slow one even when there's nothing behind it only the truth.

Then he went quiet on me, and just stood there going through the papers, turning them over, checking whatever it is they check, and that silence, I'm telling you, that silence does be the longest few seconds of the whole thing. You'd swear the man was reading a whole book off two pages. I just stood there and said nothing, looked straight ahead, didn't fidget, though my two feet wanted to.

And then he asked me had I anything on me I'd want to declare, and I said I hadn't, and then he asked was I the only one travelling in the car, and I said I was, and I near enough forgot to say where I was actually going to be dropped that evening, the exact address of it, and he had to ask me twice before it came back to me, because I'd had it in my head the whole queue and then it went clean out of it the second he asked, the way these things do, and I had to stand there a second fishing for it before it came back to me and I got it out.

There was a fella ahead of me, in the car before mine, held back a good while longer than the rest of us, pulled in to the side while they went through something with him, and nobody said a word about it, not the soldier, not the ones behind me in the queue, but you could feel everyone's eyes going that way all the same, watching out the corner of them, not staring exactly, just aware of it, the way you'd be aware of anything happening a few feet from you that isn't your business. I felt for yer man, whoever he was, standing there with his own nerves no doubt worse than mine, but you'd not say a word, you'd not even look too long, you'd just keep your own eyes to yourself and hope it wasn't going to be your turn for that kind of hold-up next.

I kept my face as steady as I could the whole time, and my voice too, because you don't want either of them giving anything away, even when there's nothing to give away, sure that's the whole trick of it, keeping steady when there's nothing wrong at all, only your own nerves working against you.

He did give me one small thing though, the soldier, when he handed the papers back, he said it'll not be long now, you're grand, and he said it decent enough, not like he had to, and I was glad of it, because a small word like that goes a long way when you're standing there with your stomach in a knot.

Then he handed the papers back to me and gave a nod, just the one nod, and waved me on with his hand, and that was it, that was the whole of it, I was through.

And I drove on, and I kept driving, and it wasn't till I'd a good twenty minutes down the road behind me, well past it now, that the whole thing let go of me at once, and I had to pull in for a minute because my hands were shaking on the wheel and they hadn't been shaking a bit of it back at the checkpoint, isn't that a funny thing, it waits till you're safe to hit you. I was banjaxed after it, if I'm honest, wrung out altogether, and I hadn't even done anything only stand in a queue and answer a few questions plain and true.

That's why I tell it careful when I tell it at all. I won't make it bigger than it was, because it wasn't a great drama, nobody laid a hand on me, nobody raised a voice to me, I was through inside ten minutes of reaching the front of it. But I won't make it smaller than it was either, because sure I felt it, every bit of it, the queue and the questions and the silence and the twenty minutes after where my own hands wouldn't behave themselves. It was what it was, no more, no less, and that's the only way I know how to tell it to you now.
