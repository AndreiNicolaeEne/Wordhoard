Me: Jaysus. Is that yourself?

The old friend: It is, how's yourself, ya langer, I nearly walked past you there.

Me: Ah stop, would you look at the state of me, I'm after putting on about two stone since we last laid eyes on each other.

The old friend: Sure aren't we all, I'm after doing the messages there and me feet are killing me, I do be in bits after a Saturday in that shop.

Me: How's the family keeping anyway?

The old friend: Ah grand, grand, himself is above in the house doing nothing as usual, and yourself, are you still below in that job?

Me: I am, I am, can't complain, or I can but sure who'd listen.

The old friend: God, it's mad seeing you, do you remember we hadn't seen each other since school.

Me: Don't be talking. It's years, it is. I was thinking about school there the other day funnily enough, I don't know what put it in me head.

The old friend: Was it the master.

Me: It was, actually, how did you know.

The old friend: Sure it's always the master, isn't it, nobody thinks of the good ones, it's always him you think of.

Me: God, the fear of him. Do you remember the rule, if you so much as let a word of English out of you in that room you were banjaxed. Not a syllable. He had us in immersion above in that classroom and there was no getting out of it.

The old friend: I remember. And if you got caught, it wasn't a clip round the ear you got, it was the line.

Me: The line! I haven't thought about the line in twenty years and now I can feel me hand cramping just saying it.

The old friend: A hundred times. Every single time, no matter what it was for, talking, chewing, looking out the window, it was a hundred lines, the same line, over and over.

Me: "I must not talk while the master is talking." I could still write it out for you now with me eyes shut.

The old friend: Do you remember what he used to say to the whole class, when he was building up to one of his rages?

Me: Go on.

The old friend: "I have eyes in the back of me head and ears in the walls, and the both of them are working overtime on this shower." He said that to us every second day, near enough word for word.

Me: He did, and we believed him an' all, sure we were terrified he actually could see us.

The old friend: D'you remember the time he had yer man up out the back writing it on the board, in front of everyone, a hundred times, his arm going like the clappers, and the master just standing there with his arms folded not saying a word, just watching him sweat.

Me: I do, I was sat two rows back and I remember thinking please God don't let it be me next.

The old friend: Were you ever called on for the tables?

Me: I was, and I still go red thinking of it. He called on me one day for the seven times and me mind just went, blank, not a number in it, and I stood there like an eejit with me mouth open and nothing coming out.

The old friend: I know that feeling well. I used to freeze the second he looked in my direction, even if I knew the answer, the fear of him would take the words clean out of me.

Me: D'you remember the one time yer man talked back to him, and got away with it. I still can't believe that happened.

The old friend: God, I do, I think about that to this day. The master said something about his homework being a disgrace, and yer man just looked up and said, "Sure you never checked it properly anyway," bold as brass, in front of the whole class.

Me: And we all just froze, waiting for the roof to come down on him.

The old friend: And nothing happened! The master went a bit red and just said, "Get on with your work," and that was it. We were in shock. Weeks we were talking about it.

Me: To this day I don't know how he got away with that. Any of the rest of us would have been kilt.

The old friend: Do you remember his little habit, the thing he used to do with his knuckles before he wrote anything on the board, cracked every one of them one after the other, and we used to do it behind his back, the whole class of us cracking our knuckles when he wasn't looking.

Me: I'd forgotten that! God, we were savage little things, weren't we, mocking the poor man.

The old friend: We were. But do you remember the day he was actually kind to someone, it threw the whole class, nobody knew what to do with it.

Me: I do, that was the day yer wan's granny died and she came in and she was in bits, and he just went over to her, dead quiet, and told her to sit near the window and take her time, and he didn't call on her once the whole day.

The old friend: It near broke us all, we didn't know he had it in him.

Me: Have you heard anything of him since, is he still about at all?

The old friend: I heard he retired years back, someone said he was above in Wexford, but I couldn't say for certain. Someone else told me he's in a home now, but sure I don't know is that true or not, you'd hear anything.

Me: I heard the Wexford thing an' all, but nobody knows for sure. It's mad, isn't it, a man who ran your whole world for a few years and you can't even say for certain what became of him.

The old friend: It is mad. Looking back on it now though, it wasn't really fear we had of him at all, or it started as fear, but it's more like respect when you look back on it.

Me: It is, do you know. The man taught us proper, whatever about the shouting.

The old friend: He did. And God, the state of us being scared of an oul fella with chalk on his sleeve.

Me: (laughing) The two of us grown adults and I nearly jumped when you said his name there.

The old friend: I know! I still feel it, a little flicker of it, even now, saying it out loud on the street.

Me: Same here, I swear to God, a small little jolt of it, like I've done something wrong.

The old friend: (laughing) We will so, we'll be like that till we're above in the nursing home ourselves.

Me: Anyway, how's things with you generally, are you still in the same job?

The old friend: I am, I'm still there, it's grand, can't complain. And yourself, are you still with the same crowd?

Me: I am, moved up a bit within it, it's alright, the money's decent, the commute's a nightmare though.

The old friend: Tell me about it, I do be exhausted getting into town of a morning. And how's the rest of the family, is your mother keeping well?

Me: She is, she's grand, doing the messages every day of the week same as ever, giving out to me over nothing.

The old friend: Ah sure that's mothers for you. And are you still knocking about with any of the old crowd?

Me: A few of them, we do meet up now and again for a pint, it's good craic when we do, though half of us are knackered by nine these days.

The old friend: God, we're getting old.

Me: We are, in fairness. Look, we'll have to sort out a proper catch-up, get a few of the old crowd together some evening.

The old friend: We will so, that'd be deadly, I'd love that.

Me: Grand, I'll get your number off you there so, and we'll sort something.

The old friend: Do, yeah, it was fierce good seeing you.

Me: You too, mind yourself now.

The old friend: I will, you too, safe home.
