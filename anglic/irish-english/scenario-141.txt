I got them all into the good room, which should have told them something on its own, because we don't sit in there only for a death or a christening, and I saw the mother's face go a small bit careful when I said it, sit down there now, the whole of ye, I've a thing to say and I want ye all to hear it together and not off each other after.

Nobody took a turn. They just sat, the eldest by the window with the arms folded, the youngest down the far end near the door like a bolt for it might be needed, the aunt in the good chair she always takes because nobody ever tells her different, and the mother with her hands in her lap gone very still, which for her is saying plenty.

Me: There's something I'm after keeping back a long time now, years, and it's well past time I said it.

I let that sit a second. Amn't I entitled to take my time over it, after twenty-odd years of not saying it at all.

Me: Yous all remember the night the pub went up. I'm not going back over the whole of it for the sake of it, I'm going back over it because what yous were all told about that night wasn't the truth, and I've known that the whole time, and I said nothing.

The mother's hands moved then, just a small bit.

Me: Yous all remember how it was blamed on him. That he was last to lock the place and he must have left something on above in the stockroom, the immersion or the ring on the cooker, nobody was ever sure which, only that it was down to him being careless, and that was that, decided the same week, before the guards had even finished with it. And the anger that came down on him after, real anger, the kind that doesn't go away just because everyone stops shouting about it out loud. Da never let it go, God rest him, he was giving out about it to his grave, and the rest of yous went along with it because sure what else was there to go on. And he's carried that ever since. Twenty years of being the one who burnt the place down. Every family thing since, it's there in the room even when nobody says it.

The eldest didn't move, only the jaw went tight.

Me: It wasn't him.

Nobody said a word. The quiet in that room you could have cut.

Me: It was never him. I know exactly who left that pan going that night, because I saw them go out the back gate myself, and years after that I was told the whole of it straight, to my face, in confidence, and I've carried that confidence a lot longer than I'd any right to. It was her.

I looked at the youngest when I said it. Didn't soften it, didn't dress it up. She went white.

The mother: You're codding me. You're only codding.

Me: I'm not. I'm dead serious. I'm as sure of it as I'm sure of me own name.

The aunt shifted in the chair, and I could see the whole shape of what she was about to say before she said it, the same as always, that sure she'd only ever believed what she was told, that it wasn't her fault for giving out to him the way she did all them years, that nobody could blame her for it.

Me: And before you say it, I know well you only believed what you were told, the same as the rest of us. I'm not standing here handing out blame for that. I'm only saying the blame yiz gave him was never his to carry, and it's time that stopped.

I told them then how I came to know it. That I was only a young one myself that night, up for a glass of water when I shouldn't have been out of the bed at all, and I seen her going out the back gate in a hurry with the smell of something burning already coming up faint from below, and I didn't understand what I was looking at, not properly, not for years. And it was only long after, the two of us alone one night, that she told me the whole of it herself, straight out, the pan she'd left going, the panic, the lock she'd let herself back in with after he'd already gone home, and she made me swear I'd say nothing, and God help me I swore it and I kept it.

Me: And I'll tell yous honestly why I kept it, because it's owed to yous now same as the truth is. I was afraid of what it would do, bringing that anger back up again after it had finally gone quiet in the house, that anger nearly wrecked this family the first time round and I didn't want to be the one that set it off a second time. And I wanted to protect her, because she was young and frightened and it was done, it couldn't be undone by me saying it. And if I'm honest as well, I didn't want to be the one that caused more damage by opening my mouth. I thought staying quiet was the safer thing. I know now it wasn't safe at all, it was only quiet.

Me: There was a time, years back, at his own wedding of all things, I nearly said it to him that night. Had it up in my throat and everything. And I looked at him standing there delighted with himself and I thought, not today, not this, and I let it go again. I've thought about that night more than I'd like to say.

Me: And it's taken a toll on me, carrying it, watching him go round under a name that was never his, watching yous all still a small bit cool with him even now without ever knowing rightly why yourselves, and me the only one who knew the why of it the whole time.

I looked over then at the eldest, because I owed him that much, to look at him when I said it.

The eldest: Say it again.

Me: It wasn't you. It was never you. I'm certain of it.

His face did about four things at once. There was relief in it plain as anything, the shoulders coming down a small bit like a weight lifting that he'd been holding so long he'd forgot it was there. And under that, fast behind it, there was a fierce anger rising, and fair enough, because twenty years is twenty years, and no one hands those back to you.

The eldest: Twenty years. Twenty years of it, of yer man at work giving me the look, of being the fella who burnt his own family out of house and home, and yous all knew, some of yous, and let me carry it.

The youngest was crying by then, not loud, just sitting there with the tears going and her hands over her face.

The youngest: I never meant for any of it. I only wanted it out of the house before anyone knew I'd even been down there at all. I never thought it'd go the way it went. And then it was too late to say anything, everyone had already decided, and I was a coward, I was, I let it stand.

The anger that had been sitting quiet in that room for twenty years found its right home then, and it wasn't quiet anymore. The eldest turned it straight on her, and fair play to him for that much restraint at least, that it stayed words.

The eldest: You let me take that. You let Da go to his grave still giving out about me over it, and you sat there every Christmas since knowing well it was you.

Me: That's mine to answer for as much as hers. I'm the one kept it this long. I should have said it years ago and I didn't, and I'm sorry for that, properly sorry, not as an excuse, because there is none, only because it's owed to say it.

Nobody hugged it better in that room, and nobody tidied it up. The mother sat with her hand over her mouth, saying nothing at all for the longest time. But it was said now, the whole of it, out where it couldn't be put back, and whatever this family is going to be after tonight, at least it'll be standing on what actually happened instead of the story that got made up to cover the hole where the truth should have been all along.
