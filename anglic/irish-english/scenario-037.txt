I was down for the week helping out with the spuds, and I'll tell you now, I amn't built for it, but sure you can't be sitting on your hole above in Dublin while they're all above in the field before the sun's even thought about coming up.

Himself had us up before light, banging on the door like the house was on fire.

Himself: Are yous getting up or what? The digger'll be here at half seven and I'm not having it standing there with nobody to follow it.

The aunt had the kettle on before any of us had our eyes open, and the first job of the day went to me, if you don't mind, on account of I was the only one awake enough to be trusted with it, go out and let the hens out and check nothing had got at them in the night. Grand oul job for a Dublin fella, standing in his wellies in the dark talking to hens.

The youngest got put on the easy end of it, gathering up the small spuds that'd rattled loose behind the digger, into a bucket, nothing that'd break her. She wasn't happy about it.

The youngest: Sure that's only a young one's job.

The aunt: You are a young one, love, now go on.

The eldest got the heavy end, and there was no argument about that either, he's built like the side of a shed and it fell to him to be lifting the full bags up onto the trailer all day, one after another, his back must have been kilt off him by dinner time though he never said it.

We'd the first section along the top cleared by mid-morning and himself had us all shift down to the lower field then, different job again, less digging and more bagging and hauling, and that's when I noticed the middle one was falling well behind on her row, not saying a word about it, just going slower and slower with her head down. Nobody made a thing of it. The eldest just drifted over and started working her row along with his own, no fuss, no comment, just did it, and she said nothing either only kept going at her own pace with the weight off her.

Round about one the aunt came out across the field with a basket and a flask and we all downed tools and sat into the ditch for the sandwiches. Fierce good they were an all, ham and mustard and the tea black as the road.

The uncle: How's yourself getting on above, are we working you too hard for a Dublin man?

Me: I'm grand, I'm only after finding out what my back's for.

The uncle: Sure that's what it's for alright.

There was a bit of slagging over the tea about the neighbour's way of doing the rows, he'd come to help for the day and he does everything backways according to the uncle.

The uncle: Would you look at yer man, going against the drill like that, you'll have the whole lot in a hames before dinner.

The neighbour: I do it fine every year, don't be minding him.

The uncle: You do it wrong every year and all, that's your trouble.

Great craic altogether over it, the neighbour giving as good as he got, calling the uncle an aul eejit and the uncle roaring at him to feck off back to his own row so.

We weren't long back at it after the dinner when the sky went the colour of a bruise over towards the west and himself was up and looking at it with the face on him.

Himself: That's coming in fast now. We'll not get the far field done if that hits, we'll shift and get the near section cleared first while it's dry, leave the far one for after if it clears.

So the whole plan changed there and then, everyone moved down to the near section and worked like the hammers of hell for the guts of an hour, racing the cloud, and didn't it hold off after all, went round us more or less, only a few spits of it, and once we could see it clearing off towards the mountains himself called us back to the far field to push on with the afternoon's work.

That's when the digger caught a big stone in the ground and something in it went with an awful bang and it stopped dead, black smoke and all out of it.

The uncle: Ah for feck's sake, that's all we need.

Yer man that owned the digger was down under it for near forty minutes with a spanner and the rest of us had to fall back on the forks for that stretch, digging by hand along the row it should have done, hard aul go of it but it kept the day moving instead of everyone standing round watching him curse at an engine.

Yer man with the digger: Right, she'll do, don't be asking her to do it twice now.

And off it went again, wobbly but working, and we got the last of that section by hand and machine together.

By half four or so the light was starting to go that soft grey it gets, and himself came round shouting at everyone to pick it up.

Himself: Come on now, we're losing the light, get them bags up and don't be standing round admiring your own work.

Everyone moved faster after that, even the youngest was made hurry along with her bucket, and the last section, the awkward corner up by the gate that nobody wanted, that's the bit everyone ended up converging on together at the finish, every last one of us in around it, the neighbour and the eldest and the middle one and myself and the youngest all working the same few rows shoulder to shoulder to get it done before the dark had it beat, and there was something deadly about that, the whole crowd of us in the one spot for the last of it.

We got it. Every bit of it in.

The uncle stood counting the trailers going up to the shed, three full loads and a bit of a fourth, and he was fierce pleased with himself over it.

The uncle: That's a savage crop this year, best we've had in a good while. Not bad going for a shower that includes a jackeen.

Me: I'll take that as a compliment so.

The uncle: You can take it any way you like, culchie or no culchie we got it in before dark and that's the main thing.

We were all banjaxed after it, proper knackered, the whole crowd of us collapsed into the kitchen with our boots half off and not a word out of anyone for a good ten minutes, just sitting there staring at nothing, too destroyed to even reach for the tea the aunt had going.

And just when I thought we were let sit, the aunt turned round to the youngest before anyone was let near a chair properly.

The aunt: Go on out and shut in the hens before you sit down, I'm not having them caught out again with the fox about.

The youngest: Ah sure I only just sat down.

The aunt: You'll sit down after, go on now.

Out she went again, grumbling the whole way, and the rest of us just laughed at her, too wrecked to move ourselves, and that was the day of it, start to finish, a grand stretch in the evening after it all the same, sitting there with the light gone and the work done and not a bother on any of us bar the sore backs.
