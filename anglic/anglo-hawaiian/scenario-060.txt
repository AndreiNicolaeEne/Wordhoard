Eh let me tell you how the day wen go, cuz you nevah stay there and everybody kept asking me after, how was, how was.

So the house, the plantation one, Grandpa's house, get since he wen pass back in spring the family wen decide no can hang on top the place no more. Taxes going up, the roof stay leaking by the carport side, and Grandma cannot climb one step no more so she stay at the care home already three months. Nobody like say it out loud but everybody knew, this the last time the house going be full of people. So not just us grandkids, was the whole extended side, second cousins, the neighbor titah from next door who wen grow up with our mother, Uncle's old fishing buddy who used to stay here every weekend back in the day. Choke people in that little yard by seven in the morning, more cars than the driveway could hold.

We wen start with the front room cause that's where everybody stay looking at anyway when they walk in. Boxes going up fast, old newspaper, the doilies on the couch arms, all that. Was in there my auntie wen find Grandpa's ukulele up on top the china cabinet, all dusty, one string busted. She wen just stand there holding um.

Auntie: Eh, remember this. He used to play um every night after dinner, sitting right there by the screen door, singing to us till we go sleep. I stay hearing his voice right now, I swear.

She wen tear up little bit and everybody kind of went quiet for one minute, chicken skin all over, then the second uncle wen come put his hand on her shoulder and say leave um, we go clean um up nice, no throw.

Was also in that front room where we wen find the thing nobody could figure out, one small tin box, rusted at the corner, had inside one medal, some kind military-looking thing, and a folded paper all in one language nobody could read, maybe Filipino, maybe Ilocano, could be Japanese even, nobody sure.

The eldest: You know what this is? Whose this stay?
Second uncle: No idea, brah. Nevah seen this in my life and I wen grow up in this house.
The youngest cousin: Maybe was Great Grandpa's, from before, from the plantation days when he wen come over on the boat.
The eldest: Lolo, how you going know that, you wasn't even born.

Nobody could settle um so they just wen wrap um up careful and put um in the box marked "ask Grandma," even though everybody knew she probably not going remember either.

Next was the bedroom, Grandpa and Grandma's room, and that's where the argument wen start over the koa dresser. Two of the aunties wanted um bad, one saying she the oldest daughter so she should get, the other saying she the one who wen take care Grandma every day this past year so she deserve um more. Went back and forth good little while, raised voices even, until Uncle wen step in.

Uncle: Ho, enough already, we not going fight over one dresser in front the whole family like this. Whoever going take um, take um and give the other something else, we get plenty here, no need make this big kine thing.

They wen settle on the one who took care Grandma getting the dresser, and the other one getting the mirror set instead, and that was that, pau, no more fuss.

Was in that same room where the disagreement wen come up about the old rice cooker sitting in the closet, one ancient thing, cord all frayed, dent on the side.

Cousin's wife: Just throw this, no can even plug this in safe, going shock somebody.
The mother: No, no throw that, that's the one Grandma wen cook rice in every single day for forty years, that thing stay history.
Cousin's wife: History or not, still one fire hazard.
The mother: I no like argue about um, just put um in my car, I go keep um on the shelf, no need plug in.

Nobody wen win exactly but the rice cooker wen go with the mother anyway, and life went on.

The closet in that room was where we found the thing nobody wen expect, behind a loose board at the back, one manila envelope, thick, full of old black and white photos and letters, all tied with string, and underneath that, cash, old bills, folded so old they wen go soft like cloth. Everybody wen go quiet again, real quiet, looking at each other like, this for real?

The eldest: Eh, this Grandpa's handwriting on top. Look the date on this letter, this from before he even wen marry Grandma.
Auntie: Get chicken skin now for real. All this time, hiding in the wall like that.

They wen decide leave the money for Grandma's care fund, no question, nobody even wen discuss um, just everybody nodded and that was settled, the one thing all day nobody wen argue about.

The room that wen take forever though was the back storage room, the one used to be Grandpa's workshop turned catch-all. Was stack to the ceiling, fishing nets, old tools, boxes from three different moves nobody ever wen unpack, Christmas decorations from decades back, one whole shelf of canning jars. Took us from mid-morning clear to almost two in the afternoon just that one room, everybody sweating, cussing little bit under the breath, "who wen keep all this junk," but laughing too, cause every third box had something make everybody stop and talk story for ten minutes.

Round one o'clock, stomach stay growling on everybody, so we wen take the break, spread the grindz out on the tailgate and one folding table under the mango tree, rice, the auntie's chicken long rice, somebody wen bring poke, laulau somebody's husband wen make, and the leftover manapua from the box in the truck. Everybody sitting on coolers, on the porch steps, plates on the lap.

Second uncle: Eh this long rice broke da mout, who wen make this.
Auntie: Me, the same recipe Grandma wen teach me.
Second uncle: Garans ah, taste just like hers.

After grindz, energy wen come back a little, enough to tackle the big stuff. The old chest freezer in the carport, nobody wanted um, too heavy, too old, compressor probably dead anyway. Took four guys to wrestle um onto the truck for the dump run, everybody complaining their back going hurt tomorrow, but nobody wen fight over that one, just, get um out.

The one thing everybody wen agree on quick, no argument at all, was the family Bible sitting on the shelf in the hallway, all the names written inside the front cover going back generations. Soon as somebody wen pull um down, the whole room went, oh, that go to the eldest, no question, and the eldest just wen nod and hold um close, done, simple as that, yeah?

By late afternoon the workshop room finally wen stand empty, swept even, and that's when it wen really hit, the whole house was empty now, every room, echoing when you talk, our footsteps loud on the floor.

We wen spread the leftover stuff, small kine things, extra dishes, tools, the leftover linens, out on the lawn and everybody wen just take turns picking what they wanted, no drama by that point, everybody tired, everybody kind of soft from the whole day already.

Last thing, before the sun wen go down, we all wen walk through one more time, room to room, nobody saying much, just looking. Somebody wen turn off the water heater, somebody wen check all the windows stay locked. The eldest wen lock the front door, stood there with the key in the hand for one long minute before putting um in the pocket.

The eldest: Well. That's that then.

And everybody just wen stand in the yard little while longer before we all wen go home, nobody really wanting to be the first one to drive off.
