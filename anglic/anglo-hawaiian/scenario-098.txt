Okay so, dis da story my grandfather wen tell my mother, and my mother wen tell me, and now I telling you, so lissen up.

Way back, da family stay from one small village, Ilocos side, up in da mountain part where get plenny rock and no plenny water. My great-grandfather side, dey had one lil piece land, but nevah enough fo feed everybody, yeah? Get too many mouth, not enough rice. And den one year da rain nevah come right, crops wen die, and da landlord guy still wen want his share anyway, no care if you starving or what. So dat was da push, plain and simple, no food, no future, nothing fo da young ones going inherit but debt.

Get one recruiter guy wen come around da village dat time, telling everybody, ho, Hawaii get work, plantation work, good pay, come sign up. My great-grandfather, he was da one wen go first. Alone. Left da wife and da two kids behind, told dem he go send fo dem soon as he stay set up. His own mother, my great-great-grandmother, she wen cry plenny dat day, garans, dey say she nevah stop crying till da ship wen disappear.

Da journey, ho, dat was da hard part everybody talk about most. Weeks and weeks on top one ship, packed in dea like da cargo, seasick da whole time, bad water, bad food, no room fo move. My great-grandfather nevah talk too much about da boat part, but da auntie, she da one always say, "Get one cousin wen go on dat same boat, and he nevah made um." She nevah say da name though. Every time somebody ask, she just shake her head. "No, no need know da name. He stay gone, dat's all. Da ocean wen take him." Chicken skin, every time she tell dat part, even now.

My great-grandfather wen land here with basically nothing. One small wooden box, some clothes, and one lil carved wood cross his mother wen give him before he left, dat da one thing he wen carry from da old place, and dat same cross still stay in da house today, up on top da shelf by da altar. Nobody touch um. It's old, da paint mostly gone, but everybody know what um is.

First stretch here was rough, no joke. No steady work waiting fo him like da recruiter guy wen promise. He wen stay with one family from da same province, already here couple years, dey wen let him sleep on top da floor in dea lil plantation shack, borrowed space, not his own, and he wen help dem out however he could just fo pay um back, chop wood, fix da roof, watch da kids. Dat family, ho, dey wen save him, garans. Without dem he probably would've been sleeping outside.

Eventually he wen find work, but not steady kine, one day here cutting cane, one day dea loading da wagons, whatevahs dey needed extra hands for. He would tell my mother later, "First few months I no can even count how many different job I wen do. Whatevahs dey give me, I take um." Slow slow he wen save up little money, penny by penny, living cheap, no buying nothing he no need.

Once he had enough saved, he wen send word back home, get money fo da ticket now, come. But dat kind of thing, back den, take time, yeah? Paperwork, waiting fo da next ship with space, saving little extra just in case. So was a little over two years before his wife and da two kids finally wen arrive. Two years him alone here, dem alone dea, only letters going back and forth, when dey could even get letters through.

Da day da ship wen come in with his family on top, dey say he wen run down to da dock and just stand dea shaking, no can even talk. His wife wen see him and start crying right dea on da gangway. Da kids, dey ain't even reckanize him at first, he wen change so much from da work, skinny, dark from da sun. But once dey stay all together again, dat was da first time in two years he wen feel like one whole person again, not just half of one family.

After dat, tings wen start coming together little by little. He wen take up carpentry work on top da plantation, turns out he had da hands fo um, and once people wen see he do good work, steady work wen come his way. He wen stay carpenter da rest his life, fixing camp houses, building fo da plantation store, whatevahs dey needed built or fixed. Dat wen become his trade fo good, passed down even, his boy wen learn from him too.

Within da first few years dey wen find one house fo settle into, one of da plantation camp houses, small kine, but theirs to live in steady, not borrowed like before. And dat's where da rest da kids wen come, my grandmother was born right dea in dat camp, and her braddahs and sistahs after her. Dat, my mother always say, dat's when da place wen really become home. Not when dey landed, not when dey wen find work, but when da first baby wen born right dea on dat land. Cause once you get one keiki born someplace, dat place yours now, no matter where you came from before.

I remember sitting on top da porch as one small kid, listening to my grandmother talk story about all dis, how her fadda wen come alone, how her mother wen wait two years not knowing fo sure if he even made um okay. She would tell um slow, taking her time, and everybody just sit quiet and listen, no interrupting. Sometimes my grandfather would chime in little bit too, "Yeah, and da old man nevah complain one time about all dat, garans, tough kine guy." And den we go grindz after, whole family, choke food on top da table, cousins running around, brah and titah teasing each other, and somebody always bring up da old stories again like dat, every single time we all together.

Dat's how I stay telling um to you now, da same way, sitting down, taking time, no rushing um. Cause dat's how you supposed to pass um down, slow, careful, so nothing get lost. My kids goin hear da same story from me one day, and I hope dey tell um just as careful when it's dea turn.
