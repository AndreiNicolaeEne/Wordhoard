Arre don't even ask me about that day, yaar. You missed it, lucky you, because I am still telling people the full story like a filmy serial, episode by episode, and still it is not finishing.

See, plan was we will start packing van by seven, latest seven thirty, because after nine the sun comes straight on that lane and then no auto wala will even stop for us. But my husband, whole night he is saying do this, do that, arrange this cable, that cable, and by the time we are actually sleeping it is two thirty. So morning, seven o'clock alarm is going off on and off, off and on, and both of us just switching it off and sleeping again like two small children. When I finally sit up it is already twenty past eight. I am shouting, get up, get up, we are so late already, what to do now.

And then, first thing, before we are even out of the door, the lock. Main door lock, from inside it is jamming, key is going in but not turning, na. My husband is doing this way, that way, I am standing there with two bags in each hand, tiffin box balanced on top, and the key is just not listening. Ten minutes we lost there itself, just at our own front door, before even starting anything. I am telling him, see this is a sign only, whole day is going to be like this, and he is telling me, don't be senti, it is just a lock, chalo, push and try.

By the time the lock problem is solved and tempo fellow is calling third time, where you people are, sir, I am standing since half an hour only, meter is not running but my time is going, we are already one and a half hours behind. That tempo bhaiya, I tell you, patient man, otherwise he would have left long back.

Now this is the part that still I cannot believe. My neighbour aunty from the next building, nobody called her, nobody even told her properly we are shifting today, but she is just standing there at seven forty five with a flask of tea and two of her boys. She is saying, I heard you people talking two days back only, so I thought let me come, one more pair of hands is always useful, no? That woman, I am telling you, she did more work than half my own relatives who I actually called and who gave some excuse or other, uncle is not well, this is not there, that is not there.

Then, the breaking. Oh god, don't remind me. My mother's old showpiece, glass one, elephant family, small one carrying the big one on the back, she got it from her wedding time itself, forty something years it survived every shifting before this. One of the boys, aunty's son only, good boy, meaning well, he is carrying box marked FRAGILE. I wrote it myself in red marker, big letters, but he is stacking one more box on top, thinking it is nothing, and the whole stack is toppling on the stairs. Such a crash, yaar, whole building must have heard. I am just screaming, no no no, that's it, that's it, all gone. My mother's face, I cannot describe. Everybody just froze on the staircase for two minutes, nobody saying anything, only that sound still ringing in the ear.

Mother: What was that, what fell?

Me: Nothing, nothing, small thing only.

Mother: Don't lie to me, I heard glass.

And then I had to tell her, and she just went quiet, quiet the whole rest of the day, which was somehow worse than if she had shouted at us.

On top of all this, one box, small one, I had written KITCHEN MISC on it, spoons, some old vessels, my daughter's school certificates also I think were in there, that box we never found again. Simply vanished. Maybe left behind in old flat, maybe fell off the tempo somewhere on the road, who knows. We searched everywhere in new place, under the sofa, behind the fridge, everywhere, bas, gone. Till today it is not there. What to do, some things you just have to let go.

Then came the fridge. Double door, heavy heavy thing, and this became a whole lafda between my husband and the tempo bhaiya's helper. My husband is saying, tilt it this side, hold the bottom, and the helper is saying, no sir, other side, you will damage the compressor, and both are shouting instructions over each other, nobody listening to the other, aunty's son trying to referee in between and getting shouted at by both. Fifteen minutes just standing in the lane arguing how to lift one fridge, and it hadn't even moved one inch.

And then, the stairwell. Old building, narrow, that turn at the second floor landing, ninety degree turn, and this fridge simply refusing to go. This way, that way, upside down almost, tilted like this, tilted like that, somebody's hand is getting caught in between, somebody is saying careful careful, and still it is not going. Twenty minutes on that one landing, sweating, cursing, and finally the watchman uncle comes and says, take off that side railing bracket, sir, it is loose only, and just like that, five minute job, fridge is through. All that fighting for nothing, could have called him one hour before itself.

Meanwhile sky is also turning. Was clear morning, but by afternoon clouds came from nowhere, that heavy grey Mumbai monsoon type cloud, and just as we are carrying the mattress down, out of nowhere, big rain. Everybody running for cover, mattress half wet, boxes we are throwing tarpaulin over, someone's shirt completely soaked. My husband standing in that rain looking like he wants to cry, or maybe that was just rain on his face, who knows.

Then lunchtime came, and honestly that saved the whole afternoon. Aunty had got vada pav for everybody, and someone ran and got cutting chai from the corner, and we all just stood there among the boxes, no chairs, no table, eating standing up, chutney dripping on somebody's hand, laughing little bit finally after all that tension. That five, ten minutes, standing there with hot chai and vada pav in the rain-cooled air, that was the only proper nice part of the whole day, I am telling you.

After that it was just loading, loading, loading. Last three, four trips, my legs were just not moving, arms like jelly, everybody quiet now, nobody even talking, just doing. That is when exhaustion really hits, not in the morning when you are fresh and excited, but those last loads when body is just saying no more, no more, and you are still saying two more boxes, two more boxes.

Before locking up I walked through the old rooms one last time. Empty now, echoing, our voices sounding strange in there. Kitchen where I cooked eighteen years, that mark on the wall where my daughter used to measure her height, small small pencil lines going up every year, all still there, we forgot to even take a photo of that. Stood there two minutes, nobody rushing me, then just locked the door and gave key to watchman uncle and came down.

We reached new place well after nine at night, dark long back already, streetlights on, and we had far less with us than we started that morning thinking we will have. No showpiece, no that one box, half the day gone in fighting over lock and fridge and stairwell. But we were there, finally, sitting on the floor among half opened boxes, too tired even to talk, and my husband just looked at me and said, chalo, at least we made it, no?

I said, haan, we made it. What to do, this is how it goes only.
