Arre where do I even start, this whole house thing took my whole one year, I'm telling you. Eleven months exactly, and six people helping-helping along the way, some for two days only, some stuck with me till the end. Let me tell the whole saga properly, from the start, because everybody just sees the finished house now and says wah, so nice, but nobody knows what went inside, no.

See the thing is we had outgrown the old place completely. Two rooms only, and by then it was myself, the wife, the two kids, and my mother also shifted in after my father passed. Five people in two rooms is not a joke, yaar. Kids doing homework on the bed, cooking happening basically in the passage, at night we are like sardines only. My wife used to say, this is not living, this is timepass, we are just adjusting-adjusting every day. So we decided, bas, enough, we will build up, add one floor, make it proper.

First thing, sitting with the mistri, we did the reckoning. He is looking at the plot, scratching his head, saying, "Six, seven months, madam, easy job only, don't worry." I knew already that is faltu talk, contractor timeline is never real timeline, so I told the wife, add three months minimum to whatever he says, then we will be close. And see, I was right, six months became eleven in the end. He said four-five people will be enough, one mason, two labour, one electrician coming and going, one plumber at the end. Turned out six different hands touched that house before it was done, some overlapping, some coming fresh when the earlier ones had other sites also going, everybody juggling two-three jobs at one time, that is how it is here only.

We had barely started, two weeks in, and already the first setback came. The mason had marked the foundation line little bit off, angle was not matching with the boundary wall, and nobody caught it till the plinth was half done. My uncle who used to be in PWD, he came to see, walked around once, and just stood there shaking his head. "This is not straight," he said. "You break and redo, otherwise whole first floor will be crooked, then what to do." Imagine, almost two weeks work, gone, just like that, redoing from scratch. I was so senti that day I couldn't even eat properly. The mason kept saying sorry sorry, chindi mistake he called it, but chindi or not, it cost us.

Then, this one I still laugh about, one Sunday morning, no warning, no phone call, my wife's cousin turns up with two of his building's watchman-type friends, all three ready to work, gloves and everything. Nobody had asked them, they just heard from someone that our work was going on and came, saying "we are free today only, use us na." That whole day flew, we finished the boundary side wall which was supposed to take three-four days alone. That is jugaad for you, when help just comes standing at your gate.

Monsoon came and just sat on us for almost one full week. Rain rain rain, no let up, cement work is impossible in that weather, sand all wet, everything covered in tarpaulin, everybody sitting under the shed drinking cutting chai and doing complete timepass. My mother used to look out at the half-built structure every morning and just sigh, "This will never finish, only God knows." I used to tell her, bas, patience, what to do, weather is not in our hands.

Somewhere in the middle we ran short of cement, don't ask how, calculation went wrong somewhere, or maybe wastage was more, I don't know. The regular supplier's stock was also not there that week, some shortage going on in the area itself. So we made do, mixed in whatever was lying at two other small shops nearby, different bags, slightly different grade, mason was not too happy but he adjusted, said "chalo, will manage, no option anyway." Small small compromises like that, all through the middle months.

One evening, second floor slab work going on, one of the labour boys slipped off the bamboo scaffolding, came down almost one storey height. My heart stopped completely, everybody ran, I thought worst has happened. But he landed on the sand heap below, thank god, nothing broken, just shock and one twisted ankle, rested two days and came back saying "boss, I am like that only, nine lives," laughing it off. But that whole evening nobody could work properly, hands were shaking, we just sat.

Then came the big argument, this one dragged for almost one whole week. The kitchen platform, my wife wanted it one height, standard modern height she had seen in her cousin's flat, but the mason kept insisting the old way is better, lower height, "your mother will find it easier, madam, trust me." Every evening this same discussion, back and forth, my mother sitting quietly in the middle not taking sides but clearly having an opinion. Finally we called one more opinion, a relative who does interior work, and settled on something in between. Everybody grumbling little bit, but work continued.

Worst blow came when the plumber, checking the bathroom wall for the pipe line, found the whole wall was not plumb, tilting slightly, must have been from some early mistake nobody caught. That wall had to come down completely and go up fresh, no shortcuts this time, my uncle stood there the whole day supervising personally, checking with the level again and again, saying "this time no mistake, I am watching only."

But I still remember the day the frame finally stood, all four sides up, columns tied, looking like an actual house instead of some sad half-thing. Small win, but that evening we all sat on the unfinished floor, ate vada pav that the mason's boy got from the corner stall, and just looked up at it. Somebody said "ek number, boss, finally looking like something," and honestly that vada pav tasted like the best food I ever had, paisa vasool for all the tension of the past months.

Last stretch, final four nights, we worked well past dark, borrowed lights hanging off the scaffolding, everybody tired, my wife bringing tea out at ten, eleven at night, mosquitoes biting like anything, but nobody wanted to stop so close to done. Neighbours used to look out from their society balcony and ask, "still going on, ya? light has gone in your area also or what," seeing the borrowed bulbs burning so late.

And then, one ordinary Tuesday, it just stood finished. No big function, no fanfare, just the last coat of paint drying and everybody standing back looking at it quietly. My mother touched the main door frame and just stood there, not saying anything, very senti moment for all of us.

After, sitting one evening with tea, I did the full tally in my head. Eleven months start to end. Six different hands, mason, two labour boys who changed midway, one electrician, one plumber, and that cousin with his two friends who came that one Sunday, if I count them separate that is more, but core six I say. Two weeks lost to the wrong foundation, one week lost to rain, one scare that turned out fine, one wall rebuilt completely, endless small small adjustments in between.

Now when I stand inside, upstairs room where the kids study, proper kitchen where my mother can cook standing comfortable, I don't think about all that tension so much, only sometimes, when I touch some corner and remember, arre this is where that argument happened, or this is the wall we broke twice. But mostly now it is just home, only, and that is enough.
