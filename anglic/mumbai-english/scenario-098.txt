Our people are originally from one small gaon in the Konkan, right on the coast, where my ajoba's family had only one strip of land, paddy in the monsoon and nothing much rest of the year. Whole wadi was doing the same thing only, coconut and rice and some fishing on the side, and land was getting divided every generation, smaller smaller, until there was not enough left to feed so many brothers. Then one bad monsoon came, crop failed completely, and after that there was no question of staying back and just managing. What to do, na, when the land itself is not there for you.

It was my ajoba's own father who went first, this is going back three generations now, alone, leaving the wife and small children in the wadi with the promise he will send for them once something is settled. Whole family saw him off at the jetty, and my aaji used to say her mother-in-law told her the old man did not even look back once, because if he looked back he will not go.

That crossing itself was the hard part, not the leaving. There was no train reaching all the way in those days, only the launch from the coast to Bombay port, two three days in the monsoon swell, everybody sick, packed in like sacks, one bundle of clothes and some dry bhakri each and that is it. My aaji used to tell, very softly, that there was one cousin-brother travelling in that same launch, younger boy, and he did not survive the crossing, some fever caught him mid-sea and by the time the launch reached there was nothing to be done. Nobody in the family says his name even today, only "that one we lost on the boat," spoken by the one relation who was actually there, and only when the conversation goes that deep, otherwise it is not brought up at all.

My ajoba's father landed in Bombay with almost nothing. Few annas in the pocket, one small bundle, and that is all. First many weeks he had no proper work at all, was doing this that, loading at the docks whichever day there was call for labour, standing since morning at the naka hoping somebody will pick him. Some days work was there, some days it was not there, and on the days it was not there he was just walking around the port area, timepass only, waiting for tomorrow.

He did not have his own room or anything, he was staying with another family from the same wadi who had come earlier and taken one corner of a chawl room near the mill area. Whole family, and him on top, sleeping in shifts almost, borrowed floor space, borrowed vessel to eat in. That family used to say "stay here only, what is the hurry, once you get your own place you go" and that is how he managed those first months, eating whatever was cooked for everybody, contributing what little he had.

Then slowly the mill work started. First it was only day rate, loading raw cotton bales, no fixed naukri, going every morning not knowing if there is work or not. Then one jobber from the same area, hearing he is a steady sort of man, took him on properly in the spinning department, and that became his trade for good. After that it was regular pagaar every month, small small amount but coming regular, and that is what changed everything for him.

Once he had saved enough, and that took its own time, he sent word back to the wadi that tickets are being arranged. Family was still there, wife and children waiting, hearing news only through whoever was travelling back and forth. It was more than two years, my aaji says, two years and some months, before the rest of them actually came across and the family was whole again in one place. Two years he was here alone, sending money when he could, and they were there, doing whatever they could with the land that was left.

When the wife finally came, she carried one thing from the wadi that is still in our house today, a small brass samai, the oil lamp, wrapped in an old sari so it would not knock about on the journey. Even now every Diwali that same lamp is lit first, before any of the newer ones, and everybody in the house knows it came all the way from the gaon on that crossing, nobody has to say it out loud even, it is just known.

Within the first few years of the family being together he had found a proper room to settle in, one room in a chawl near the mill, and the family stayed in that one room for years and years, whatever came. My father was born right there, and his sisters too, all in that same room, and that is really when the place became home, my aaji used to say, not before. Before that it was just somewhere you were surviving in, waiting to go back one day, but once children are born there, born and going to the municipal school down the lane, playing on the same galli, that is when a place stops being temporary and becomes yours.

I am telling this now the way I heard it as a child, sitting near my aaji in the evening, she on the katta outside, all of us small ones around her, and she would tell it slow, taking her time, sometimes going senti in the middle when she came to the part about the cousin, sometimes laughing when she told how her mother-in-law used to scold the old man for eating too fast at that borrowed family's table. We were not even understanding half the geography of it then, what Konkan was, what the launch journey even meant, we were just sitting there because it was evening and there was nothing else to do, and somehow the story stayed.

Now the mills are closed since long, that whole area is different, society buildings coming up where the mill used to be, but the room is still there, still in the family, my uncle is there even today. When I take my own children there, get down at the same signal, walk the same galli, I tell them the same story, more or less the same words even, the cousin who is never named, the lamp that is still lit first, the two years the family was apart before it was whole. My children ask "then what happened, then what happened," same as I used to ask, and I tell them, this is how we came to be here, this room, this lane, this city, all from one man who did not look back at the jetty because if he looked back he would not have gone.
