Well before the sun, when the sky was still no colour at all, the mother was already up, banging the steel dabba, calling everyone by turns.

The mother: Get up now, get up, tea is getting cold, whole field is waiting only.

By the time I splashed water on my face at the well outside, the uncle was standing in the yard with the sickles laid out on a cloth, counting them one by one, ten of them for ten hands.

The uncle: Youngest one, go and check the bund side, water is there or not, and count how much rope bundle we are having. Go fast, don't waste time.

That job went to the cousin, eighth standard only she is studying, bunked her Sunday tuition special for today. She went off and found the motor for the pump left on since last night itself, had to off it fast before more water came sitting into the field. Easy work otherwise, running here and there, tying small small bundles, carrying the water pot up and down, but somebody has to keep the count, the uncle said, otherwise by evening nobody will know what got cut and what is still standing.

The heaviest section, the one near the far bund where last week's wind had knocked the paddy flat, that went straight to the workman who comes every season, the one everybody just calls bhaiya though he must be forty plus by now.

The uncle: Bhaiya, that lodged part is for you only, you are having the strength for it. We will manage the easy rows.

We started in the grey light, sickles going swish swish through the wet stalks, feet cold in the mud right up to the ankle. By seven the near section, close to the road, was cleared, and then the shift happened, whoever was cutting went to bundling, whoever was bundling went to carrying the sheaves up on the head to the threshing yard.

The uncle: Chalo, change side now, don't stand there.

By mid morning one aunty from next door, not even blood relation but everybody calls her aunty only, started falling behind on her row. Her back has been giving trouble since two years now, everybody knows this, but nobody said one word about it. The didi from up the lane just came quietly and took half her row, cutting alongside like it was nothing at all.

The aunty: Arre no no, I will manage, you go do your own side.

The didi: Bas, you do the tying only, I will cut, what is there in that.

The tiffin came out around eleven, the mother and another aunty carrying it down balanced on the head, tins stacked one on top of the other, bhaat, one usal, one dry bhaji, pickle, and cutting chai in a kettle with vada pav packed from the morning itself. Everyone sat under the one tree at the edge of the field, banana leaf for plate, and the talk turned senti filmy for two minutes about last year's harvest before it went back to joking about who is cutting how fast.

The father: This one is doing full timepass only, look at his row, half of everybody else's.

The bhaiya: Food was mast today, na, that's why slow. Arre uncle, quality over quantity, no? Better ek number cutting than chindi work like some people.

There was some faltu argument argument then, one side saying cut low right at the root, other side saying leave some stem for the buffalo fodder later, small fight, nothing serious, everybody laughing by the end, haan haan.

Just after the meal the sky went filmy, proper drama, dark clouds rolling in from the sea side, wind changing direction all of a sudden. Everybody knows what that means in this season.

The uncle: Forget the far side for now. Prepone the low field, that one will spoil first if water sits. Everybody come, fast fast.

The whole plan changed just like that, no discussion needed, everybody moving together to the low field, cutting fast, eyes going up to the sky every two minutes. Somebody's phone was being pakau in between, ringing and ringing, one missed call only from a cousin brother in the city asking how much got done, nobody even picking it up, time is not there for all this now.

But the rain only threatened, didn't actually come down, and by two the sky cleared a little and the sun came back out hot, gamchha on the head, water bottle going round and round.

Then the lafda happened. One old sickle, edge gone thin from too much sharpening, snapped clean at the handle while the cousin was trying to help cut a small patch near the workman. In the same confusion a small bundle got trampled into the mud, whole little patch spoiled.

The bhaiya: Kya yaar, this sickle also had to go now, of all times.

The cousin: Sorry, sorry, I didn't mean it, it just...

The uncle: Arre nothing, it happens, don't be senti about it. Go get the spare from the shed. This spoiled patch, we'll just gather whatever paisa vasool we can from it, no use crying, what to do.

The bhaiya just laughed it off, bindaas as always, some jugaad with a cloth wound tight held the handle together anyway till the spare one arrived, and work carried on, nobody scolding anybody.

By four thirty the light started going soft and gold and slanting low, and the word came down the line.

The uncle: Fast now, fast, one hour good light only left, after that it is useless working in the dark.

Everybody picked up pace, less talking now, just the sickles and the stalks falling.

The cousin: Didi, come na, help me tie this last one.

The last section, the far corner everybody had been avoiding because of the thorny bush at the edge, that one got finished with literally everybody converging on it together, no separate rows left, eight nine people standing shoulder to shoulder cutting inward like closing a circle, one small shout going up when the last bundle went onto the stack.

The tally happened right there in the fading light, the uncle and the father counting the stacked bundles out loud together.

The father: Achha, forty, forty one, forty two, forty six total. Jhakaas, more than last year.

The mother: Thank god, good harvest this time, no complaints.

Then the collapse. Everybody just sat down wherever they were standing, backs against the bundles, nobody wanting to take even one more step. The bhaiya lay flat on his back right there in the stubble. The didi was fanning herself with her own dupatta. The cousin had gone quiet, half asleep sitting up against a bundle. The water bottle went round one more time, someone lit a beedi, and just then the current went, light has gone in whole area, current gone with the last of the storm cloud passing over, but nobody even minded, there was enough evening left in the sky still.

Before anybody was properly allowed to rest, though, the mother turned to the cousin one last time.

The mother: Do one thing, go and check the goats are tied properly for the night, then only you can sit, otherwise in the morning we'll be searching whole village for them.

The cousin groaned, got up on legs that had nothing left in them, and went off into the near dark to finish that one last chore, while everybody else finally, finally let their shoulders drop.
