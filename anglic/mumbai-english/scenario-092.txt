By two, two-thirty, the whole house had thinned out to just us five, sitting near the kitchen on whatever chair or step was free, the good chairs all folded back and stacked because so many had come earlier. The big tube light was off and only the small one near the fridge was on, so everybody's face was half in shadow, half not. Tea had gone cold in two cups nobody had picked up again. Outside, one auto passed, then nothing, that Mumbai three a.m. quiet which is never fully quiet, some dog barking somewhere near the society gate.

The eldest was first to say anything after a long time of nobody saying anything.

The eldest: You know what I keep thinking of, only. That thing he used to do, check the door two times before sleeping. Every night, na. Lock it, walk to the bedroom, come back again, check.

The mother: Three times it was, not two. You always get that part wrong.

The eldest: Is it three? Arre yaar, all these years and I'm still getting it wrong.

The eldest laughed then, properly laughed, the kind that surprises even the one doing it, and put a hand over the mouth like sorry sorry, but nobody minded, in fact the aunty also let out one short laugh she clearly hadn't planned, then looked a little embarrassed about it and nobody said anything about that either. That is how it goes in this kind of night, laughing comes and goes on its own time, nobody can off it or on it like a switch.

The uncle was quiet for a bit, turning his cup in his hand, then he said the thing everybody in that room had been waiting, without knowing it, for somebody to say.

The uncle: What he used to say, remember, every single time somebody left the house. Not "bye" or anything like that. "Khana kha ke gaya na?" Every time. Didn't matter if you were going next door or going to Pune.

And then the uncle said it again, exact same words, exact same tone, dragging the "na" a little at the end the way the dead man used to drag it, and for one second it was like the voice was back in the room itself, and nobody said anything, everyone just sat with it.

The mother broke that one first, wiping her eye with the end of the pallu, half laughing again.

The mother: Even the day he went to hospital that time, only, he asked the driver if he'd eaten.

Then the aunty, who had been quiet mostly, said something that made even the mother go still.

The aunty: You all know he was paying that boy's college fees. The watchman's son. Three years he was doing it, quietly, nobody was to tell anybody.

The mother: What? Since when, this?

The aunty: Since the boy failed twice in eleventh standard and the father was going to pull him out. He told me only, don't tell your mother, she'll say we don't have that kind of money ourselves.

The mother: Arre! I didn't know this at all, na!

For a moment after that nobody said one word. Just the fridge humming and somewhere far off a train, the last local of the night probably, going wherever the last local goes.

It was the eldest who broke it, getting up to fetch the tin of biscuits from the kitchen shelf and putting it down in the middle again, pushing it toward everyone though nobody had asked.

The eldest: Eat na, all of you. Sitting like this the whole night, at least eat something, don't do drama.

The uncle took one biscuit, more to have something in the hand than to eat it, and passed the tin along.

Somewhere in the passing of the tin, an old thing came up between the eldest and the aunty, some flat in Thane that was supposed to have gone to whom, a fight from maybe fifteen years back that had nothing to do with tonight at all.

The aunty: You still remember that, ha. That was your father's decision only, I never even asked for it.

The eldest: I'm not saying anything, I'm just saying it was said that way.

The mother: Bas, bas, leave all that. Not tonight, this discussion.

And they left it, both of them, just like that, no more push from either side, the old thing sinking back down under the water again the way it does.

Then the uncle, shaking his head with a small smile that had real warmth in it now, no edge left at all, told the one everyone half-knew already but wanted to hear again.

The uncle: You remember the fan story. Ceiling fan in the hall was making that sound for, what, four years? Everybody telling him, replace it na, it's making that grinding sound the whole night, you can't even sleep properly. He wouldn't. Every time, "this fan is a jhakaas fan, don't touch it, it's giving full service still." Four years, that sound. In the end it just stopped on its own, completely dead, and even then he was saying, must be some small wire issue only, get the electrician, don't buy new one.

The mother: Stubborn like nobody I've seen. On small small things especially. Big things he'd listen, adjust, but on this fan, that particular steel almirah, his one pen he used for everything, no moving him.

Everyone laughed a little at that, easy laughing now, not the surprised kind from before, and somewhere in all of it, without anybody putting it in so many words, the room had settled on it: that was the thing, that stubbornness, half-annoying and half the most him thing about him, that is what they would all keep telling for years whenever his name came up.

It was around then that the younger one, who had said almost nothing the whole night, started crying, quietly, no sound to it really, just sitting there with tears going down and not wiping them, not making any show of it. Nobody stopped talking for that. The uncle put a hand on the shoulder for a second, kept it there, and kept talking at the same time, and the talk just moved around the crying gently, like water going around a stone, not stopping for it and not ignoring it either.

Then, the way these nights do, it turned practical, without any ceremony about turning practical.

The eldest: Tomorrow morning I'll call the pandit, fix the time for everything.

The mother: I'll manage the food side, tell the society people how many will be coming.

The uncle: I'll handle the papers, bank and all, don't worry about that side at all. Aunty, you do one thing, you call up all the relatives who couldn't come tonight, let them know properly, not just a missed call and forget.

The aunty: Haan, haan, I'll do that first thing.

The mother: What to do, all this has to be done only, no getting around it.

Nobody argued about who was doing what, it just fell into place the way it needed to, plain talk, nothing dressed up in it.

By then it was properly late, even the dog outside had gone quiet, and it was the uncle, last one really still fully awake, eyes red but awake, who lifted his cup of by-now stone cold tea and said, not like he'd planned it at all, just what came:

The uncle: Chalo. To him then. Whatever he was, stubborn old fellow that he was, he was one number.

And they all lifted whatever was in their hand, cup or glass or just an empty hand, and drank to that, and sat on a little longer in the almost-quiet before the house, finally, let itself go to sleep.
