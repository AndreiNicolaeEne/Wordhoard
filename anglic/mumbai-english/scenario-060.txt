So basically the building is going for redevelopment, that is the whole reason, nothing more dramatic than that. After grandfather passed only, the flat was locked for two years, papers this side that side, society AGM, developer NOC, and finally the developer people said vacate by month end, demolition men are standing outside only with their trucks. So whole extended lot had to come, not just us, my mother's side, my father's side, whoever has some claim or some memory sitting in that house. Everybody was told nine o'clock sharp, but knowing this family nobody came before ten-thirty only. Chalo, that is also normal, no?

We started from the hall because that is where everybody dumps their bags first anyway. The eldest took charge immediately, standing in the middle with a marker pen and cardboard, writing KEEP, GIVE, THROW on three boxes like he is running some exam hall. That cupboard in the hall, the minute we opened it the old radio-cum-cassette player came out, the one grandfather used to keep on the window ledge for cricket commentary.

The uncle: Arre, this one, this one is having a story only. Every match, this fellow is sitting with this radio on his lap, one hand on the volume, shouting at Gavaskar like he can hear him.

He went completely senti holding it, turning it over and over, and for two full minutes nobody said anything, just let him have his moment, no?

Then from the same cupboard a small tin box came out, rusted, tied with a red thread, and inside, one key. Just one key, old style, big teeth, no lock anywhere in the house it matches. Everybody passed it hand to hand.

The middle uncle: This is for what only, nobody is knowing?

The aunty settled in Pune now: Maybe it is for the old trunk we already gave to the kabadiwala last year na.

The eldest: Or it is for some almirah at your side, who knows. Keep it in the KEEP box only, no use throwing, later somebody will remember.

Nobody could place it. Small small mystery like that, every old house is having one, isn't it.

From hall we moved to the dining side, and there the first real lafda started, over the dining table. Solid wood, grandmother's dowry piece, six chairs, scratched all over from forty years of homework and dinner and Ludo.

My mother: This table is coming to our house only, I am telling you now itself, no argument.

The middle one: Why your house only, my flat is also having space, I can adjust.

My mother: Your flat is having space where, you yourself were saying last month itself no space for even one more chair.

Bas, ten minutes of this, then the eldest did one thing, he said whoever is doing the puja for grandmother's shraadh every year, that person takes the table, matter closed. Everybody agreed, chalo fine, no more discussion, table settled.

Kitchen was quick actually, mostly steel and Tupperware, tiffin boxes small small ones stacked one inside other, veg masala dabba still smelling of turmeric after all this time. But the old fridge standing there, single door, running since I don't know how many years, that one caused the real disagreement, whether it should even be kept at all.

The youngest cousin: Throw it na, it is not even cooling properly since last two years, what is the use keeping such faltu thing.

The aunty: Throw how you can say, your grandmother bought this with her own first salary only, this is not just fridge.

The youngest cousin: Sentiment sentiment, we cannot fill house with sentiment, we are having so much already, what to do with one more dead fridge.

That one went back and forth whole morning, on-off, on-off, nobody backing down, so the eldest parked it as pending and moved everybody to the bedroom before it became a whole scene.

Bedroom is where the hidden thing turned up, nobody was expecting it. Behind the old almirah, there was a loose wooden panel at the back, and inside, a bundle of letters tied with string, and under that, some old currency notes, the pre-decimal type, and two gold bangles wrapped in a handkerchief. Complete silence for one full minute, then everybody started talking together.

The uncle: See, this is why we are clearing room by room properly, otherwise this would have gone straight in the throw pile.

The mother: Whose letters even, kya likha hai, read one na.

Nobody read them fully there and then, too much emotion, decided to keep for later, home mein baithke padhenge.

That bedroom itself took forever after that, far longer than any other room in the whole flat, every single drawer had something, old photographs, school report cards with marks written in red pen, an eighth standard result card somebody had kept for forty years for no reason, bangles, buttons, medicine strips gone expired since who knows when. What should have been one hour became almost three hours, everybody sitting on the floor going through things, getting distracted, going senti, coming back to work, going senti again.

By then it was well past one, stomach was making noise, so the eldest called it, bas, break time, everybody eat first, work later. Somebody had got vada pav from the corner stall downstairs, somebody else brought a big tiffin of veg pulao from home, and one cutting chai order went to the tapri boy who was told come up in twenty minutes. All of us sitting on the bare floor of the empty hall, plates balanced on knees, no table because that one was already wrapped for shifting. Good half hour of just eating, laughing about the fridge fight, the youngest cousin still saying paisa vasool the way that vada pav was, everybody forgetting for a bit that the house is closing forever.

After lunch the big decision, grandfather's old wooden almirah, huge thing, three doors, mirror gone cloudy in the middle one. Nobody in the end wanted it. Too big for any flat, too heavy to shift, mirror anyway broken feeling from inside. Even the aunty who fought for the fridge said leave this one, too much lafda to carry down four floors with no lift working since last month. So that one went, no argument at all this time, straight tag for the scrap bhaiya who was already waiting downstairs with his cart since two hours, calling up "aunty ho gaya kya" every ten minutes.

Grandmother's old sewing machine, on the other hand, everybody agreed on immediately, no fight, no discussion, it goes to the eldest's daughter, the one who is actually stitching things on it every weekend, learned from watching grandmother only as a child. Not one word against it from anybody, straight, done, into her bag it went.

By evening only the small back room was left, the one used as store, and that emptied out fast once we got to it, mostly old newspapers and bags of bags, and then it was standing there completely bare, sun coming in slanting through the grill, dust floating in the light, and for a second nobody moved, just stood looking at four empty walls that used to be a whole house full of people.

What was left, some crockery, a few show-pieces, old vessels, the diyas, that got divided there itself, quick quick, everybody taking one-one thing, no big ceremony, somebody's workmate had sent an auto already downstairs to help carry.

Last thing, the eldest did the round of every room one more time, checked each switch off, water off, and then locked the main door, tried it twice to be sure, stood there for a second with his hand still on the lock. Then he turned around, said chalo everybody, done, and we all went down together, nobody looking back up at the building, though I think everybody wanted to only.
