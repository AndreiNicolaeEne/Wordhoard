Richt, I'll tell ye the hale o't, fae stert tae feenish, cause fowk keep speirin fit like it wis and I've telt it that mony times noo I could dae it in ma sleep.

It wis the sky that gied it awa afore onythin else did. Aboot fower in the efternoon it went a queer colour, a kind o yalla-green ye dinna see in a normal day, and the hale place gaed affa still. Nae a breath. The washin on the line wisna even movin, and I mind sayin tae the eldest, there's somethin nae richt aboot this. Aabody could feel it, even the dog widna settle, jist paced aboot the kitchen fleer greetin tae itsel.

The eldest: Fit's wrang wi the dog?
Me: Naethin's wrang wi the dog, it's the wather. Get yer boots on, we've work tae dae afore this comes in.

And it didna tak lang. Within the hoor the wind wis up and I wis oot in the gairden like a mad wifie, chasin the bin afore it gaed doon the road, tyin the trampoline doon wi rope tae the fence posts, bringin in the flooer pots and the bairns' bikes and onythin else that could be lifted. The mannie fae next door wis oot an aa, we wis baith at it, shoutin ower the fence.

The neighbour: Ye gettin battened doon?
Me: Tryin. This yin's nae messin aboot.
The neighbour: Aye, they said on the wireless it's tae be the warst in years.

By the time I got back in, the wind wis really risin, that low roarin soond that gets intae yer chest afore ye even hear it wi yer ears. I got aabody thegither in the front room, the bairns wrapped in a blanket on the sofa, the mother-in-law doon fae upstairs an aa cause I widna hae her up there her lane wi the wind gettin like that. We sat close, aa thegither, and I mind sayin tae them, dinna fash yersels, it's jist wind, it'll pass. I wisna near as sure o that as I soondit.

The pooer gaed oot nae lang efter. Aa the lichts jist deed at yince and the hoose gaed black, and somewye that made it aa feel a lot mair real. The wee een startit greetin.

The youngest: I dinna like it.
Me: I ken, quinie, I ken. Come in aside me.
The eldest: Is the hoose gonnae fall doon?
Me: Naw it's nae. Haud on tae yer sister.

And then it got tae the pynt whaur naebody wis sayin onythin ava, cause ye couldna. The wind wis that loud ye'd hae had tae shout richt intae somebody's lug for them tae hear ye, so we jist sat, aa haudin on tae each ither, listenin tae it batterin at the windaes and the slates gaan on the roof like somebody up there kickin them aff yin by yin. I heard a tree come doon, nae far fae the hoose, a crack like a gunshot and then this lang draggin thud that ye felt in the fleer as much as heard, and I mind grabbin the bairns in closer and thinkin, richt, that's somebody's tree deen, please God nae oors. There wis a moment, richt eftir that crack, whaur I wis mair feart than I've been in a lang while, and I didna say it tae naebody at the time, I jist sat there wi ma airms roond the twa loons and the quinie, sayin nithin, prayin the roof held.

It didna aa haud. We heard it gaan, a rippin, tearin soond fae the back o the hoose, and I kent fine that wis slates or mair than slates comin aff. Cudna dae a thing aboot it, jist had tae sit and listen tae yer ain hoose bein pulled at. And the windae in the back bedroom, we fund oot efter, got a crack richt across it fae somethin flein intae it, a big yin, but it held, thank God, it didna gang in, cause if that had gaed in wi the wind ahint it I dinna like tae think.

The mother-in-law: I've nivver kent it like this.
Me: Naw, me neither.
The mother-in-law: Are we aa richt here?
Me: Aye. We're aa richt. Jist bide close.

It felt like hoors, though I doot it wisna. And then, aa at yince, it jist stapped. Nae a windin doon, jist stapped, like somebody had switched it aff, and the quiet efter wis its ain kind o frichtenin, cause yer lugs wis still ringin fae aa the roarin and noo there wis naethin, jist a drip somewye and the odd creak.

The eldest: Is it feenished?
Me: I think it's feenished, aye.

We sat a meenit langer afore onybody moved, jist tae be sure, and then I got up and opened the door tae hae a look.

The gairden wis nae the gairden onymair. The tree fae twa doors doon wis richt across the road, blockin the road hale, we widna be gettin the caur oot till somebody wi a saw cam and cleared it. Half the slates aff the back o the roof, lyin smashed in the gairden or jist gone athegither, and a great blue tarp wis gonnae be needed afore the neist rain cam in, nivver mind fixed richt, that wid tak weeks. The crack in the bedroom windae catchin the licht, still haudin, still there, and I stood and looked at it a lang while, thinkin on foo close that had come.

And then I looked ower the fence and near laached, cause the neighbour's fence wis flat, the hale length o it, doon like a row o dominoes, and he wis staunin there wi his hands on his heid.

The neighbour: Wid ye look at that.
Me: Aye, but that's jist a fence, mannie. We've a tree in the road and half oor roof away.
The neighbour: True eneuch. Coont yersels lucky it wisna waur.

And he wis richt, though it didna feel affa lucky at the time, staunin there in the wind-wrack wi the pooer still aff and a tree tae shift afore we could gang onywye.

The days efter wis aa cleanup, chainsaws gaan up and doon the street fae mornin tae nicht, fowk oot in their gairdens pickin up slates and branches and ither fowk's bins that had blawn intae their hedges, tarps gaan up ower roofs aa alang the road, and it wis a fortnicht afore we got a mannie oot tae look at oors richt and langer than that afore it wis dane.

But I'll tell ye, even noo, tellin it, I still get tae the pynt whaur the tree cam doon and I hear masel sayin, and the crack o it, ye nivver heard the like! And when I get tae the windae I'm awa again, and it held, it actually held, I couldna believe it! Fowk that's nae been through it jist naidna understaun foo it grups ye, the wye ye still reach for the words even months efter, cause naethin else quite says it richt.
