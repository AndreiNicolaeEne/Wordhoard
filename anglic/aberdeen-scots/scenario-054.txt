I'll spik ye through fit happened wi the hoose, since ye werena there for nane o it, stert tae feenish, and it wis a richt saga an aa.

We'd bidden in the auld place gey near fifteen year, and it wis fine eneuch when it wis jist the twa o us and the eldest a bairn in a cot. But by the time the youngest cam alang we were five in the hoose, forby my mither-in-law bidin ben the scullery hersel, and there wisna room tae turn aroon in, never mind fower growin bairns aa needin space for skweel books and beets and aathing else forby. The loons were sleepin heid tae fit in the box room, the quinie had a bed in fit used tae be the press, and aabody wis fair scunnert wi it. There wis nae end o claik aboot it fae the neighbours forby, aabody speirin fit we were gyaun tae dae. Een nicht, efter the bairns were doon, the wife says tae me, we canna gyang on like this, we'll hae tae dae something. And that wis hoo it stertit.

I sat doon wi a pad o paper, like a gype, and reckoned it aa oot afore we'd liftit a spade. I telt the wife it wid tak aboot fower month, maybe five, and I thocht I'd manage it mysel wi jist my brither gien's a han at the weekends. Weel. Fower month cam and gaed, and we were still diggin. By the time it wis feenished it had teen eleven month tae the wik, and sax folk aa in had a han in it at een point or anither - my brither, my brither-in-law, the neighbour and his loon, a joiner chiel fae doon the road, and a mannie my faither kent fae the yard fa cam and did the electrics for's. So I wisna far wrang on the folk, but I wis affa wrang on the time.

The stert o it wis affa near a disaster, mind. We were oot markin the trenches for the steens, and I'd read the plan vrang - had the hale thing set oot a fit and a hauf ower far intae the gairden, and we didna catch it till the trenches were dug and the mixer wis on its wye. My brither took een look and says, that's nae richt, and we checked it against the drawing, and richt eneuch, I'd got it aa vrang. Felt a richt vratch aboot it. That cost's near a fortnicht, diggin it aa oot again and settin it richt, and I mind bein sae scunnert wi mysel, thinkin, we've nae even stertit richt and we've lost a fortnicht already.

Aboot a wik intae the diggin, we got a richt surprise. I wis oot my leen on the Setterday mornin, jist me and a spade, and up comes the neighbour wi his loon, baith o them in their auld claes.

The neighbour: Fit like. We've seen ye oot here yer leen every day this wik, thocht we'd gie ye a han. Fit are we deein?

I hidna asked him, hidna even hintit at it, but he'd been watchin ower the fence and jist turned up wi his loon and a barra, and atween the twa o them we got the trenches feenished in a day fit wid hae teen's a wik my leen. That wis a gweed day.

Syne we hit a stretch o wither that stopped aathing deid. It rained for near a wik solid, the kind that gets intae yer beets and stays there, and the site turnt tae glaur, glaur aawye ye lookit. Ye couldna pour concrete in that, couldna dae naething but stand and watch the days gyang by, and I mind bein fair depressed wi it aa.

Then partwye through, wi the blockwork gyaun up, we ran short o material. The merchant had said the blocks wid be in the morn and they werena, some mix up wi the order, and we were sittin there wi a hauf-biggit wall and nae mair blocks tae feenish the coorse. So we made dae wi fit we hud - dug oot the odd steens fae the auld shed that were left ower fae years back, mixed the sizes in a bit that widna show, jist tae keep the coorse level and keep gyaun till the richt anes cam.

There wis a scare an aa, near the middle o the job. My brither-in-law wis up on the scaffold daein the heid o the gable, and he lost his fittin - I dinna richt ken foo, a plank shiftit or somethin - and doon he cam, no far, mebbe eicht fit, but far eneuch tae gie's aa a fricht. He landed on the pile o sand we hud there, thank the Lord, and got up wi jist his pride hurt and a sair back for a day or twa. We were aa feart he'd broken something, but naething wis, nae worse than the scare, and the wife cam runnin oot fae the hoose white as a sheet when she heard's aa shoutin.

There wis a richt argument an aa, ower the roof. The joiner chiel wintit tae dae it his wye, wi felt and battens like he'd aye deen, and my brither wis convinced we should pit in a breather membrane instead, said the felt wid rot in ten year and aabody wis deein it the ither wye noo.

The joiner: I've been roofin thirty year, I ken fit warks.

My brither: Aye, and the wye ye've aye deen it isna the wye they dae it noo, is it.

We ended up gyaun wi the membrane, and I'll say nae mair aboot it than that, but there wis a coorseness in the air for a day or twa efter, and the wife tellt them baith tae dinna fash her wi it and jist sort it atween them.

The warst o it though wis the back wall. We'd biggit it up near tae fu heicht afore the joiner cam ower wi his level and telt's it wisna plumb, oot by near an inch ower the heicht o it. I couldna believe it wi my ain een. There wis naethin for it but tae cae it aa doon and stert again fae the fittins up. My brither-in-law jist stood there sayin, dinna fash yersel, better tae ken noo than efter the roof's on. He wis richt, but it wis a sair een tae swallow, aa that wark gone in an efternoon.

There wis a gweed day tho, I mind it weel - the day the timber frame finally gaed up and stood there, the hale shape o the extension there for aabody tae see for the first time, no jist lines on the grund. The wife cam oot wi tea for aabody and we jist stood and lookit at it a fair while, naebody sayin muckle.

It wis near the hairst afore we got tae the end o it, fower nichts in a raw wirkin on past dark, my brither and me and the joiner up ladders wi torches and the car heidlichts turnt on the gable, tryin tae get the last o the slates on afore the wither turnt again, near eneuch wirkin under the meen. My hands were that cauld I couldna feel the nails.

And syne een day, it wis jist feenished. Naebody said muckle, we jist stood back and lookit at it, the hale thing biggit and slated and the windaes in. The wife gied's a bosie richt there in the glaur and grat a bit, and I didna say naething because I kent I wid an aa if I opened my mooth. Efter, she'd made a pot o tatties and neeps for aabody fa'd helpit, and we sat roon the table fair worn oot and gled o it.

We teen a tally o it aa efter - eleven month fae stert tae feenish, sax folk aa in helpin at een time or anither, and mair concrete, mair timber, mair sair backs than ony o's had thocht at the stert. But standin in it noo, hame at last, wi the bairns aa got their ain rooms and the quinie nae mair sleepin in a press, I widna change a meenit o it, near-fa's and arguments and aa.
