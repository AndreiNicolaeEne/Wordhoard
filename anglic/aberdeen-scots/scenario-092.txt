The hoose had thinned oot tae the closest four or five o them, aabody else awa hame lang since, and the last o the tea gone caul in the pot and naebody fashed tae mak a fresh ane yet.

The eldest: Mind fit he wis like wi the porridge, ilka mornin. Semolina intae it, nae maitter fit onybody said tae him.

The mother: It wisna semolina, quine, it wis oatmeal ye're mindin. Ye're aye gettin that bit vrang.

The eldest: Aye, I ken, I ken fine I aye get that bit vrang, I dinna ken fit wye. Semolina, oatmeal, it's aa the same tae me noo.

The youngest: (a laugh gets oot afore he can haud it in) Sorry - I didna mean tae, it's jist - the twa o ye arguin ower porridge, and him nae even here tae tell ye which ane it wis.

The aunt: Naebody minds, chiel, laugh awa. It's fine tae laugh the nicht.

The neighbour: He'd hae laughed himsel, onywye.

The eldest: He wid that.

The neighbour: D'ye mind fit he'd say tae ye, though, ony time ye chappit the door. Nae maitter fa ye were, nae maitter fit time o day. "Fit like? Ye'll hae a fly cup afore ye go." Every single time. Ye couldna get by him withoot a cuppie in yer haund.

The mother: (quiet, and it comes oot exact, the way he'd say it) "Fit like? Ye'll hae a fly cup." Aye. That wis him tae a tee.

The aunt: I can hear him sayin it the noo.

The youngest: Me an aa.

The aunt: Here, did neen o ye ever ken this ane. Fan he wis a loon, afore ony o youse were even a thocht, he ran awa tae the hairst dance ae nicht withoot askin, and didna come hame till the marnin, and oor mither leathered him for it up and doon the close. And the neist year he gaed back tae the same dance, jist the same, like he'd learnt naethin ava.

The mother: Fit? He niver telt me that.

The eldest: Never!

The aunt: As true as I'm sittin here. He'd tell ye himsel if he could.

(the room gaes quiet a meenit, aabody wi their ain thochts)

The neighbour: Here, pass ower the plate, there's a puckle o sandwiches still sittin there gaun stale, we canna jist sit lookin at them aa nicht.

The youngest: Gie's een then.

The eldest: And the cakies an aa, dinna leave they.

The aunt: Ye still havena paid me back for yon fence, mind, fae the summer.

The eldest: Here we go.

The aunt: I'm jist sayin.

The eldest: I ken fine fit ye're sayin, and I'm nae gettin intae it the nicht.

The mother: Nae the nicht, the twa o ye. Nae the nicht.

The aunt: (a wee laugh, lettin it drap) Aye, fair eneuch. Some ither time.

The neighbour: He wis affa thrawn himsel, mind, ye couldna tell him a thing. D'ye mind the time o the big snaw, and he widna tak a taxi hame fae the toon, nae for love nor money. Walked the hale road in it, up tae his knees, feart tae naebody but himsel, jist tae save the fare. Cam in soakin, blae wi the cauld, and when yer mither telt him he wis a daft aul mannie he jist said, "Weel, I'm hame noo, amn't I."

The mother: He wis that. Widna spend a penny he thocht he could keep.

The neighbour: Ye couldna shift him aince he'd decided. Steen deif tae onybody.

The eldest: He wis jist thrawn, that's aa it wis. Thrawn tae the end.

The aunt: Aye. Thrawn.

The mother: (naebody says it richt oot, but they're aa thinkin the same thing noo) Thrawn, aye, but a gweed sowl wi it. That's fit he wis.

The eldest: That's him tae the life.

(the mother's een fill up, quiet, and she disna say naethin aboot it, jist wipes them wi the back o her haund, and the spik keeps gaun gentle roond her, naebody makin a show o it)

The aunt: D'ye mind, tae, he'd aye say the same tae the bairns fan they were greetin, "dinna fash yersel, it'll aa come richt," and it wid, mair or less, it aye did somehow.

The youngest: (his voice gaun a bit thick) He said that tae me the last time I saw him.

The neighbour: (laying a haund on the mother's airm, quiet, lettin her greet)

The mother: I'm fine, I'm fine. Jist gie's a meenit.

The eldest: Nae rush, Mither. Nane ava.

(a while gaes by, the spik quiet, and syne it turns practical, plain, naebody makin a ceremony o it)

The aunt: Richt. I'll phone the undertaker the morn's mornin, get the time settled.

The eldest: I'll dae the flooers, I ken fa tae ask.

The youngest: I'll see tae the hoose, keep it richt for fowk comin in.

The mother: Somebody'll need tae tell yer uncle, he'll nae hae heard yet.

The aunt: I'll dae that an aa. And the claik'll be roond the toon by the morn onywye, ye ken fit it's like here, aabody kennin afore ye've telt them.

The neighbour: Aye, that's the toon for ye.

The eldest: We'll manage it atween us, onywye. We aye dae.

(it's late noo, jist the neighbour still up, the ithers awa tae their beds, and afore he pits the licht oot he lifts fit's left in his glass)

The neighbour: Weel. Here's tae him, then. A gweed chiel, a thrawn aul mannie, and the best o them. Fit like, aal freend, wherever ye are the nicht. We'll aa hae a fly cup on ye the morn, and every morn efter. Aa the best tae ye.
