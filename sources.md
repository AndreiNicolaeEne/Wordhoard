# Sources for the dead dialects

The 26 living dialects are synthetic and have no sources. Every text under a
dead dialect is an excerpt of a real period text, and this file says where
each one's material comes from. The works themselves are public domain.
Entry links go to the live pages; dated Internet Archive snapshots are
listed at the end for every source that has one (a single source has none,
and the list says which). Captures were cleaned before excerpting, apparatus,
line numbers, notes, and page furniture stripped, the period text itself
untouched. Every excerpt's text is drawn from the captures listed here. A
few dozen characters lost at capture time survive as replacement marks,
concentrated in seventeen ayenbite excerpts in the 101 to 150 range and one
shakespearean excerpt.

## ayenbite-kentish

Dan Michel, *Ayenbite of Inwyt* (1340), the complete text: Richard Morris's
transcription collated with British Museum MS Arundel 57 by Pamela Gradon,
from the Corpus of Middle English Prose and Verse, University of Michigan,
whose rights statement places the material in the public domain:
<https://quod.lib.umich.edu/c/cme/Ayenbite?rgn=main;view=fulltext>

## beowulfian-english

Old English prose and verse. Excerpts 001 to 100 draw on the Chronicle
tradition, kept whole in one range because its manuscripts are parallel
recensions of one text:

- The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, manuscripts A, B, C, D, and E, from Tony
  Jebson's transcriptions: <http://asc.jebbo.co.uk/a/a-L.html>,
  <http://asc.jebbo.co.uk/b/b-L.html>, <http://asc.jebbo.co.uk/c/c-L.html>,
  <http://asc.jebbo.co.uk/d/d-L.html>, <http://asc.jebbo.co.uk/e/e-L.html>

Excerpts 101 to 150 draw on a disjoint set of works:

- *Beowulf* and *The Fight at Finnsburh*, Old English text, Project Gutenberg
  ebook 9701: <https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/9701>
- Wulfstan's *Sermo Lupi ad Anglos*, *The Wanderer*, *The Dream of the Rood*,
  and Ohthere's voyage, from Peter S. Baker's Old English Aerobics:
  <https://www.oldenglishaerobics.net/sermo.php>,
  <https://www.oldenglishaerobics.net/wanderer.php>,
  <https://www.oldenglishaerobics.net/dream.php>,
  <http://oldenglishaerobics.net/ohthere.php>
- King Alfred's preface to the *Pastoral Care*:
  <https://asengland.wordpress.com/oe-prose/king-alfreds-preface-to-the-liber-regula-pastoralis/old-english-version/>
- The Old English *Physiologus*, Project Gutenberg ebook 14529:
  <https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/14529>
- The prologue to the Laws of King Alfred, Old English text from Jay Paul
  Gates, "Prologue to the Laws of King Alfred: An Edition and Translation for
  Students", The Heroic Age 18 (2018), an open-access journal:
  <https://jemne.org/issues/18/gates.php>

## hocclevean-english

Middle English chancery and signet documents, kept as individually numbered
document files. The documents are fifteenth-century public records; the text
was captured from *An Anthology of Chancery English* (Knoxville: University
of Tennessee Press, 1984) as digitized in the Corpus of Middle English Prose
and Verse, University of Michigan, whose rights statement for the item reads
"No Copyright - United States":
<https://quod.lib.umich.edu/c/cme/ChancEng>

Excerpts 001 to 100 and 101 to 150 use disjoint sets of documents. Because
the documents are one formulary genre (royal signet letters and petitions),
the two ranges share stock openings and formulae even though no document
appears in both.

## chaucerian-english

Geoffrey Chaucer, *The Canterbury Tales*, the Ellesmere manuscript text from
Frederick J. Furnivall's Chaucer Society print (1868-79), as digitized in the
Corpus of Middle English Prose and Verse, University of Michigan:
<https://quod.lib.umich.edu/c/cme/AGZ8232.0001.001>

## cursor-mundi-english

*Cursor Mundi*, the Cotton manuscript text from Richard Morris's Early
English Text Society edition (1874-93), via the Corpus of Middle English
Prose and Verse, University of Michigan, whose rights statement reads "No
Copyright": <https://quod.lib.umich.edu/c/cme/AJT8128.0001.001>

## shakespearean-english

Early Modern English. Excerpts 001 to 100 are all from one work:

- Shakespeare's First Folio, Project Gutenberg ebook 2270:
  <https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/2270>

Excerpts 101 to 150 draw on a disjoint set:

- Sir Philip Sidney, *The Countess of Pembroke's Arcadia*, book 1,
  transcribed by Risa Bear from the Sommer facsimile, Luminarium Renascence
  Editions: <https://www.luminarium.org/renascence-editions/arcadia1.html>
- Edmund Spenser, *The Faerie Queene*, book 1, from Luminarium Renascence
  Editions: <https://www.luminarium.org/renascence-editions/queene1.html>
- Letters of Elizabeth I, from elizabethfiles.com:
  <https://www.elizabethfiles.com/resources/letters-of-elizabeth-i/letter-to-james-vi-of-scotland-1585/>
- Francis Bacon, "Of Studies": <https://jacklynch.net/Texts/studies.html>

## Which excerpt came from what

The living dialects need no map: every filename is its scenario number. For
the dead dialects, so nobody has to reconstruct this from the texts:

- **beowulfian-english**: 001 to 028 Chronicle MS E, 029 to 053 MS D,

  Excerpts 101 to 150 use documents 2, 4, 6, 13, 16, 20, 23, 25, 27, 30, 33,

- **cursor-mundi-english**: 001 to 100 span the opening 22 percent; 101 to
